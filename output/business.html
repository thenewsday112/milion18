The most dangerous thing at a pumpkin patch may not be the pumpkin. And the greatest health risk at an apple orchard may have nothing to do with the apples.

It could be the pony your child rides. The goat they pet. The hamburger cooked on a makeshift grill. Or the shared barrel of water where children take turns bobbing for apples.

These are the experiences that make fall traditions memorable. But they can also introduce risks involving animal-borne pathogens, contaminated surfaces, unsafe food handling, and inadequate sanitation.

For most families, a day at the farm ends with photographs and happy memories. For others, exposure to an invisible pathogen can mean an emergency room, kidney failure, or worse.

Young children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with weakened immune systems can face particularly serious consequences.

The problem is not that pumpkin patches and apple orchards are inherently unsafe. It is that we have spent decades celebrating these family traditions without consistently recognizing the public health responsibilities that come with them.

And as a developing E. coli investigation in California reminds us, those responsibilities are far from theoretical.

A pumpkin patch is rarely just a pumpkin patch
In San Luis Obispo County, CA, public health officials are investigating five cases of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli and a possible connection to Brookshire Farms, a seasonal agricultural attraction. Several children have reportedly experienced severe illness.

The farm voluntarily closed and has cooperated with investigators. The source of the infections has not been established.

That distinction is essential. A suspected association does not establish causation, and investigators must be allowed to follow the evidence.

But the investigation raises questions that extend far beyond one farm.

When families visit agricultural attractions, they are often entering environments where livestock, recreational activities, food preparation, shared equipment, and large crowds intersect.

A working farm may be accustomed to managing the hazards of agricultural production. Hosting hundreds or thousands of visitors, particularly young children, creates an entirely different set of responsibilities.

The public may see a wholesome family destination.

A public health professional sees multiple opportunities for pathogens to move between animals, the environment, food, and people.

Both perspectives can be true.

The pony ride, the petting zoo, and the invisible passenger

Consider a child who rides a pony, pets a goat, or feeds a calf.

Cattle, sheep, and goats can carry Shiga toxin-producing E. coli without appearing sick. Other animals may carry Salmonella, Campylobacter, or Cryptosporidium, a parasite capable of causing severe gastrointestinal illness.

Transmission does not always require direct contact with an infected animal. Pathogens can be present in manure, soil, bedding, fencing, gates and on other surfaces. They can travel on shoes, clothing, stroller wheels and hands.

A child may touch a fence, climb into a stroller, and eat a cookie minutes later. That simple sequence can create an exposure pathway.

Young children are particularly vulnerable because their natural behaviors include touching their faces, putting fingers in their mouths, and playing close to the ground.

These are not failures of parenting. They are predictable behaviors that should be anticipated when designing attractions for children.

The responsibility cannot rest entirely on a parent remembering to bring hand sanitizer.

Operators must provide appropriate separation between animal areas and eating spaces, accessible handwashing facilities with soap and running water, trained staff, and clear instructions for visitors.

Hand sanitizer can be useful in some circumstances, but it is not an adequate substitute for handwashing in every animal-contact setting.

What about that hamburger from the temporary grill?
Now consider another familiar fall attraction: the food stand at the same location.

Perhaps it is a temporary grill serving hamburgers and hot dogs. Maybe it is a concession trailer preparing sandwiches, caramel apples, or freshly pressed cider.

These operations may be temporary, but the microorganisms do not care.

Ground beef still requires cooking to a safe internal temperature of 160 degrees F. Poultry requires 165 degrees F. Cold foods requiring refrigeration must be maintained at 40 degrees F or below, while hot foods should be held at 135 degrees F or above under the FDA Food Code.

Employees still need appropriate handwashing facilities, safe water, clean utensils, and procedures to prevent cross-contamination. And food handlers must be able to manage these responsibilities while serving long lines of hungry families.

A makeshift operation does not necessarily mean an unsafe operation. Many temporary food vendors maintain excellent food safety practices. But temporary facilities can introduce challenges involving refrigeration, temperature control, potable water, sanitation, waste disposal and employee hygiene.

The risks become particularly concerning when food preparation occurs near animal environments or when visitors move directly from petting zoos and livestock areas into eating spaces. A hamburger does not become safer because it is served beside a pumpkin patch.

Even bobbing for apples deserves another look

Some fall traditions introduce risks that have little to do with cooking.

Take bobbing for apples.

The traditional activity involves multiple participants placing their faces and mouths into a shared container of water while attempting to retrieve floating apples.

From an infectious disease perspective, the concern is obvious. Shared water can become contaminated with saliva, nasal secretions, and microorganisms transferred from participants’ faces and hands. The activity creates opportunities for person-to-person transmission, particularly when the water is not replaced between participants.

This does not mean every apple-bobbing event causes illness. It means the traditional format creates avoidable opportunities for exposure.

There are safer alternatives that preserve the fun, including individual containers, individually handled apples, or games that eliminate shared mouth contact with water.

The point is not to eliminate childhood traditions. It is to recognize when a tradition can be redesigned to reduce foreseeable risks.

History has already warned us
These concerns are not hypothetical.

In 2004, an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak associated with a petting zoo at the North Carolina State Fair resulted in more than 100 illnesses, including children who developed hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS), a serious complication that can result in kidney failure, brain damage or death.

Investigators documented risks associated with contaminated animal environments and the movement of pathogens beyond animal enclosures.

Other outbreaks have been linked to livestock exhibitions, pumpkin patch field trips, and agricultural attractions where visitors became ill without necessarily touching animals.

Apple orchards have also figured prominently in food safety history, particularly through outbreaks associated with unpasteurized cider.

The 1996 Odwalla outbreak, which killed a child, demonstrated the consequences of inadequate controls in juice production and helped accelerate changes in federal juice safety requirements.

In 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published an analysis of animal-contact outbreaks reported between 2009 and 2021.

During that period, 557 outbreaks resulted in 14,377 illnesses, 2,656 hospitalizations, and 22 deaths.

These figures concern animal-contact outbreaks across multiple settings, not every illness associated with agritourism. They do not include all possible risks associated with temporary food service or shared recreational activities.

But they demonstrate that the underlying public health problem is longstanding and well documented.

We have known for decades that agricultural environments can expose visitors to infectious disease hazards. The question is whether we have done enough with that knowledge.

Not everyone faces the same consequences
A healthy adult exposed to a gastrointestinal pathogen may experience several miserable days of illness and recover.

For someone else, the same exposure can have devastating consequences.

Young children face increased risks from certain infections, including STEC-associated HUS.

Older adults may be more susceptible to serious complications and dehydration.

Pregnant women face particular concerns from pathogens such as Listeria monocytogenes, which can cause pregnancy loss, stillbirth, or severe newborn illness.

People with weakened immune systems may experience more severe infections or prolonged illness.

These differences matter when agricultural attractions promote themselves as family-friendly destinations. A destination designed for families must account for the vulnerabilities of the people it invites. That means prevention cannot be based solely on what a healthy adult might tolerate.

It must consider the child reaching through the fence, the grandmother enjoying a hayride, the pregnant mother purchasing lunch, and the visitor whose immune system cannot respond effectively to an infection.

The standard of protection should reflect the people most likely to be harmed, not merely those most likely to recover.

When entertainment expands, responsibility must expand with it

Agritourism has become an important source of revenue for many agricultural businesses. Pumpkin patches have evolved into seasonal entertainment destinations. Apple orchards may offer restaurants, bakeries, animal encounters, rides, games and festivals.

There is nothing inherently wrong with that evolution. But each additional attraction introduces responsibilities that may extend beyond traditional agricultural practices.

A pony ride requires attention to animal health, hygiene, and visitor contact.

A petting zoo requires controls for animal-associated pathogens and environmental contamination.

A temporary food stand requires appropriate food safety practices and equipment.

An apple-bobbing station requires consideration of shared-water hygiene.

A cider operation requires controls appropriate to the product and processing method.

And the entire property requires attention to how visitors move between these activities.

A family might pet goats, ride a pony, purchase hamburgers, and share a picnic table within the same hour. That movement is precisely why public health protection must be considered across the entire visitor experience.

Individual attractions cannot be treated as isolated activities when the same visitors, employees, equipment,and environments connect them.

Prevention should begin before opening day
Public health guidance for animal exhibits and temporary food service already exists.

The CDC and public health veterinary organizations have long emphasized handwashing, separation of animal and eating areas, visitor education, and environmental controls. Food safety regulations establish expectations for cooking, holding temperatures, sanitation, and employee hygiene.

The problem is not necessarily an absence of scientific knowledge. It is whether those expectations are consistently understood, implemented, and verified across seasonal operations.

I believe agricultural attractions should conduct comprehensive public health risk assessments before welcoming visitors each season.

Those assessments should consider the entire property, including animal exhibits, pony rides, food vendors, cider operations, shared recreational activities, sanitation facilities and visitor traffic patterns.

Seasonal employees should receive practical training appropriate to their responsibilities.

Operators should be prepared to respond quickly when illness is reported, cooperate with public health authorities, and communicate transparently with visitors.

Local regulators should also consider whether oversight systems adequately address businesses that combine agriculture, entertainment, animal contact, and food service.

This is not about burdening farmers with unnecessary requirements.

It is about recognizing that a business inviting families onto its property has responsibilities that extend beyond agricultural production.

The cost of doing nothing
I know what it means for an ordinary day to become the beginning of an unimaginable tragedy. For more than three decades, that experience has shaped my work in food safety, public health, and consumer protection.

I also know that families rarely enter these situations believing something terrible is about to happen. Parents take their children to restaurants, grocery stores, school events, and farms because these are normal parts of life. They trust that the businesses and institutions welcoming them have taken reasonable steps to protect their health.

That trust carries responsibility.

When a child becomes seriously ill following a family outing, the consequences extend far beyond the immediate medical emergency. There may be hospitalization, kidney complications, long-term health consequences, financial hardship, and emotional trauma.

In the worst cases, there is a chair at the family table that will remain empty forever.

We should not need another tragedy to recognize the importance of prevention.

Keep the traditions. Change the expectations.
I want families to visit pumpkin patches and apple orchards.

I want children to meet farm animals, learn about agriculture, explore the outdoors, and experience traditions that connect generations.

But I also want operators, regulators, and public health leaders to recognize that the hazards associated with these experiences extend far beyond the food being grown or sold.

Sometimes the risk is the hamburger cooked on a temporary grill.

Sometimes it is the goat that appears perfectly healthy.

Sometimes it is the contaminated fence a child touches before eating.

Sometimes it is the shared water in an apple-bobbing barrel.

And sometimes investigators must work for weeks to determine exactly how an exposure occurred.

The lesson is not that every farm attraction is dangerous. It is that familiar, wholesome surroundings do not make infectious disease hazards disappear.

We should preserve the traditions while strengthening the protections that make them possible.

Because the moment a farm invites families through its gates, it becomes more than an agricultural operation. It becomes a place with public health responsibilities.

And the success of a fall festival should never be measured solely by the number of pumpkins sold, pony rides completed, or photographs taken.

It should also be measured by whether families return home healthy.

The most important thing a child should bring home from a pumpkin patch is a memory, not an infection.

Publisher’s Platform: Radical transparency? What I am asking the FDA to do Is not radical at all

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has rightly made “radical transparency” the motto of his Department of Health and Human Services. HHS even has a page by that name. I think it is a good motto, and I want to help Secretary Kennedy and Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas deliver on it in food safety, with two citizen petitions that ask for nothing the federal government has not already done, has not already said it believes in, and, as of Oct. 26, has not been told by the Department of Justice that it has the authority to do. These petitions are meant as a hand up.

Before getting to the petitions, it is worth looking at where the phrase came from and how it found its way into food safety, because HHS and FDA put it there themselves.

Where “radical transparency” came from

The phrase did not start in Washington. It is a management idea popularized by Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio, who built his hedge fund around what he called radical truth and radical transparency and pitched it in a widely watched 2017 TED talk. Dalio’s own summary of the business case is worth keeping in mind for what follows: if you are handling things well, radical transparency will make that clear, and if you are handling things badly, it will make that clear too.

Kennedy made it his own at his confirmation hearings. Before the Senate Finance Committee on Jan. 29, 2025, he promised “radical transparency” within the department. Answering Senator Grassley, he said that if Congress wanted information, the doors are open. And he added a line every outbreak investigator should tape to the wall: “We should tell Americans when we don’t know.”

Three weeks later, in his first address to HHS staff on Feb. 18, 2025, he gave the idea its philosophical core: “Only through radical transparency can we provide Americans with genuine informed consent, which is the bedrock and the foundation stone of democracy.”

At the start, the phrase was mostly about Congress, conflicts of interest and vaccines. It did not stay there.

How it moved into food, then into outbreaks and recalls

On March 10, 2025, HHS directed FDA to explore closing the self-affirmed GRAS pathway, and the press release said HHS and FDA “are committed to promoting radical transparency to make sure all Americans know what is in their food.” That is the first time I can find the phrase attached to food.

Marty Makary, then FDA’s incoming commissioner, brought it with him. In his confirmation testimony he described a generational opportunity to usher in radical transparency. Then, on July 9, 2025, FDA sent a letter to food industry leaders about recall communications that said this: “At the FDA, we believe that radical transparency in food safety and nutrition actions is key to reducing foodborne illness and chronic disease.” Read that again. Foodborne illness. Recalls. Radical transparency. FDA’s words, not mine.

The next day, FDA published more than 200 drug rejection letters under the headline “FDA Embraces Radical Transparency”, and in September it announced it would release them in real time, calling radical transparency “one of the guiding principles of this administration.” Those letters are redacted for confidential commercial information, but, in FDA’s words, they “will contain company names.” Hold that thought.

On Sept. 24, 2025, FDA’s food program launched Executive Incident Summary abstracts for closed outbreak investigations. The constituent update opened: “The FDA is reinforcing our commitment to radical transparency to ensure Americans know what is in their food and that it is safe to consume.” Both the recall letter and the abstracts came out of FDA’s food program, led at the time by Deputy Commissioner for Food Kyle Diamantas. By then the phrase had become the administration’s brand more broadly; that same month the Senate HELP Committee held a hearing titled “Restoring Trust Through Radical Transparency.”

The people have changed since then; the commitments have not. Makary resigned in May 2026, and Diamantas, the same official whose food program wrote radical transparency into the recall letter and the outbreak abstracts, has been acting commissioner ever since. In August, President Trump nominated Dr. Heidi Overton to be the next commissioner. The commitments the agency put in writing are still there for its leaders to build on, and that continuity is an asset.

For my part, the place to start is food safety, where HHS and FDA have already laid a strong foundation in their own words.

The unfinished part

The outbreak abstracts were a real step forward, and FDA deserves credit for them. The one piece still missing is the piece the public most needs: who and where. Food Safety News reported that FDA has withheld company names in its mango outbreak summary, and Food Safety Magazine described a summary of a fatal Listeria outbreak in which FDA redacted all key details, down to the states where the patients lived. When Stop Foodborne Illness petitioned FDA in February to stop treating company names as confidential, invoking radical transparency, FDA answered that the names are protected by the Trade Secrets Act. That was an honest statement of the legal question the agency believed it faced, and, as I explain below, that question now has an answer.

The cost of that gap shows up in real outbreaks. In September FDA closed a romaine E. coli investigation and named no grower, no processor and no brand. In October, in a 50-person E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to chicken wraps, the public learned the brand from a lawsuit and a newspaper, not from FDA or FSIS – although FSIS later confirmed the source to a reporter (I represent plaintiffs in that outbreak). However, when FDA declared that outbreak over on Oct. 28, its entry still did not name the brand, the maker or the states, and it explained that more detailed product information would not have given consumers an action they could take to prevent illness. I understand the reasoning, but it assumes the only reason to name a food is so people can throw it out. As I explain below, that is not the only consumer who needs to know.

Not who might have been the source, but who was

I want to be clear about one thing, because it is the heart of both petitions. I am not asking FDA to name every firm that might have been the source of an outbreak, or to publish working hypotheses while an investigation is still sorting out the facts. Everyone remembers the 2008 tomato warning that turned out to be peppers. I am asking FDA to name the firm its own investigation found actually was the source.

The 2024 romaine outbreak is the clearest example I know. That fall, an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to romaine sickened 89 people in 15 states, hospitalized 36, caused seven cases of hemolytic uremic syndrome and killed one person. FDA closed the investigation with the word romaine and nothing more. When the traceback summary was first released, the processor, the grower and the ranch were blacked out. When it was later released without redactions, only after firms in the supply chain signed authorizations consenting to the release of their own information, the same document showed that the traceback had converged on one processor and one grower. There was no uncertainty to protect. The government had the answer the whole time, and the public learned it through records requests more than a year later, after the season, the product and any chance to act had passed.

That is the line the petitions draw: disclosure of what FDA has confirmed, through the same traceback and laboratory evidence it relies on to close an investigation, and not of what it merely suspects.

The two petitions

That is why I am filing two citizen petitions together.

The first, on outbreak firm disclosure, asks FDA to amend its regulations to say that the name and location of a firm is not confidential commercial information when epidemiologic evidence implicates a food in an outbreak and traceback or laboratory evidence, including whole genome sequencing, identifies the firm as the source of the contaminated food. That is the same evidence FDA already uses to close an investigation, and it is not a suspect list. It asks FDA to disclose implicated growers, processors and distributors whether or not there is a recall, and to finish the transparency initiative it started by publishing those abstracts without redacting firm names.

The second, on recall consignee lists, asks FDA to publish the list of retail stores that received a recalled food in every Class I recall (the kind that can cause serious illness or death) and to classify every food recall, and publish the classification, within a set number of days.

That is it. Neither petition asks FDA to reveal a recipe, a process, a customer price or anything else that is a genuine trade secret. They ask for who and where.

Why none of this is radical

Start with USDA. For meat and poultry, FSIS has published retail consignee lists in Class I recalls under a final rule adopted in July 2008, during the Bush administration. A consumer who bought recalled ground beef can find out whether their store got it. A consumer who bought recalled cheese, sprouts or bagged salad usually cannot. Eighteen years on, the beef and poultry industries are still standing.

Congress noticed the gap. In the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011, it told FDA, in its new mandatory recall provision, to consider USDA’s consignee policy. FDA did and wrote a guidance document saying it would publish consignee lists in some cases.

FDA itself has named farms, processors and brands in the same circumstances it faces today, at least eight times since 2006. States do it routinely.

And then there are those drug rejection letters. FDA has already decided, under the banner of radical transparency, that a drug company’s name is not the confidential part of a letter rejecting its application. Applying that same principle to food would bring the agency’s two halves into line: if a pharmaceutical company’s name can be published with a failed application, the name of a farm that whole genome sequencing has tied to children on dialysis can be too.

None of this is radical. It is a proven practice waiting to be adopted.

Good for consumers

Kennedy said radical transparency is how Americans get “genuine informed consent.” You cannot consent to anything about a food when the government knows who made it and will not say. A named firm and a published store list let a family check their refrigerator, let a doctor connect a patient’s illness to a source, and let a health department find cases it would otherwise miss. Over time, names build a track record, so consumers can tell which companies keep turning up in outbreak after outbreak.

Gross deception of the consumer, and who the consumer is

Section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act lets FDA share information about food in situations involving, in the Commissioner’s opinion, imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Most of the conversation about outbreak disclosure has focused on the first of those, imminent danger. The second deserves just as much attention, and I think it is the more natural fit for what these petitions ask.

Think about who the consumer is. It is the mom and dad in the grocery aisle trying to buy what is safe for their kids. It is also the buyer for Walmart or Taco Bell, or for a school district or a hospital, deciding which supplier to keep and which to drop. Every one of them makes that decision on the information available. When FDA has confirmed which company’s food made people sick and that fact stays in the file, each of those decisions is made on a record the government knows is materially incomplete. The parents cannot steer around a brand, and the buyer cannot weigh a supplier’s record, that FDA has already identified.

Deception does not have to be a false statement; an omission can mislead just as surely. A commodity-only advisory that leaves shoppers blaming every grower of a crop, a firm publicly denying it was the source while the agency’s traceback converged on it, or an outbreak table that still lists a food as “suspect” after a federal agency has confirmed it to the press each leaves consumers with a false picture that FDA holds the record to correct. And unlike imminent danger, deception does not end when people stop getting sick. The parent and the buyer are still making choices next season.

The limiting principle is FDA’s own knowledge. This ground reaches what the agency has confirmed, not its working hypotheses, which is exactly the line the petitions draw. I wrote about this in more detail when FDA closed the chicken wrap outbreak, and I have revised both petitions to rest on it.

Good for business

This is the part the industry should hear most clearly. When the government names a commodity and stops, consumers avoid the whole commodity. During this summer’s Cyclospora outbreak, Numerator data reported by Grocery Dive showed that 6.5 million households stopped buying salad mixes and kits in a single month. That cost fell on every grower and processor in the category, including the ones who did everything right. In 2008, tomato growers lost a season to a Salmonella outbreak that turned out to be peppers. Anonymity does not prevent harm; it spreads one firm’s harm across an entire industry.

A name is the only thing that separates the firm that caused an outbreak from the competitors who did not. It lets a buyer at a national chain weigh a supplier’s history the way a parent weighs a brand, lets insurers price risk and gives every firm a reason to find its problem before next season. Ray Dalio would recognize the logic: if you are running a safe operation, transparency makes that clear.

Good for HHS and FDA

The agencies promised to rebuild public trust. Few things would build trust faster than the public hearing the name of an implicated firm from FDA first, rather than from a court filing or a reporter. A clear rule also gets FDA out of the business of making ad hoc, case-by-case judgments that look inconsistent from the outside, and it makes the food program consistent with what FDA is already doing for drugs. It lets the agency finish an initiative it launched itself. And it is a concrete, announceable win: no new statute, no new appropriation, a rule FDA can write on its own.

And now DOJ says FDA has the authority

The sticking point for FDA has been legal, and it has been a fair one: the agency has said the Trade Secrets Act ties its hands. On Oct. 26, 2026, the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel answered that argument in an opinion addressed to HHS.

OLC agreed that FDA’s current regulations generally do not authorize disclosure of supply chain information, so the Trade Secrets Act largely forbids it, and that the existing route through 21 CFR 20.91 is exceedingly narrow in practice. But it concluded that section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, together with FDA’s rulemaking authority in section 701(a), permits the agency to amend its regulations to authorize disclosure of the names and locations of firms in a supply chain in situations involving imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Disclosure under such a regulation, OLC said, would be authorized by law and would not violate the Trade Secrets Act.

OLC also drew a line: a section 705(b) regulation authorizing disclosure after an outbreak has already ended would likely be inconsistent with the statute. That is imminent-danger logic, since an outbreak’s danger ends. But OLC treated the two statutory conditions as independent, allowing a regulation that permits disclosure when either one is met, and deception of the consumer does not end with the last illness. OLC did not decide whether its timing limit reaches the deception ground, and I have said so in the petition. The petitions also rest on a separate rule that a firm’s name and location simply is not confidential commercial information in the first place, so FDA has more than one sound path.

In other words, the obstacle has never been the statute. It has been FDA’s own regulations, and the Department’s lawyers have now told HHS that FDA can change them. The petitions ask it to.

Secretary Kennedy, Acting Commissioner Diamantas, Dr. Overton

HHS and FDA have already done the hard part: they made the commitment, and they put it in writing for food, in the GRAS announcement, in the recall letter to industry and in the launch of the outbreak abstracts. Mr. Diamantas, those last two came from the food program you ran. These petitions offer a way to complete that work and put your name on the result. You are also a lawyer, the first to lead the agency, and you will recognize that the OLC opinion clears the legal path the agency has long said it needed. Dr. Overton, if you are confirmed, this would be a meaningful early win to carry forward. And none of it requires anything radical. It is ordinary transparency, the kind USDA has practiced since 2008, that FDA has used at least eight times, that Congress asked you to consider in 2011, that you already apply to drug companies, and that the Justice Department says you have the authority to adopt.

Secretary Kennedy said we should tell Americans when we don’t know. These petitions ask for the other half of that promise, telling them when we do. I hope HHS and FDA will take it, and I am glad to help however I can.

‘The Gilded Age’ Is Back! Here’s What to Eat While You Watch the Show

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I can be persuaded to watch almost anything that promises extravagant set designs and beautiful costumes. If there’s history and a little mischievous social maneuvering involved, even better. My coworkers recently recommended The Gilded Age, and within two weeks, I’d watched all three seasons just in time for season four to drop.

There’s plenty to admire: Bertha Russell’s determination, Peggy Scott’s writing career and rooms furnished with a reassuring disregard for minimalism. I love the history, too, from the railroad fortunes and New York bankers to Lewis Latimer’s work in electric lighting and the woman who helped bring the Brooklyn Bridge to completion. Every episode gave me another reason to keep watching.

My husband is often still at the restaurant in the evenings, so once the kids are asleep, I have a few hours to settle in with a show. While I watched The Gilded Age, I usually poured a glass of wine (some of my favorites are under $25 at Walmart) and enjoyed the fire as Christine Baranski made an art of Agnes’s disapproval. But with the new season arriving on Nov. 1, I’m borrowing a few ideas from the show to make a watch party spread.

Set the Scene

I have a decidedly romantic streak when it comes to my surroundings, so the show’s interiors have my full attention. I’m particularly taken with the glassware and china that can make even a small supper feel indulgent. With a few soft lamps and the fire going, I’m looking forward to borrowing a little of that grandeur for my own evening.

  • A favorite plate and a special glass: Bannister may have reservations about Bertha’s colored glasses, but as a collector of vintage glassware, I find it rather inspiring. Thrift stores are wonderful places to look for floral china, small silver-plated trays and individual glasses to mix and match for a pretty tablescape.
  • A few flowers on the table: A handful of stems from the grocery store in a little pitcher is plenty. They’re equally charming when arranged in tiny bud vases or in one center arrangement. Finding flowers that mirror the colors in your china is a great way to make everything look put together.
  • Something soft beneath the dishes: Because so many watch parties happen in the living room rather than the kitchen, cover your coffee table with a tablecloth. It’ll look pretty, and it’ll catch any crumbs before they end up in the carpet. Add even more Gilded Age elegance with cloth napkins under silverware rather than paper napkins.
  • A little note for company: A handwritten card listing dishes like “Ada’s Clam Chowder” would be a nice addition if you have company for your watch party. Cream card stock and a nice pen are enough to give the food a thoughtful connection to the show.
  • Music before the show: A little opera while crafting vintage cocktails would be perfect for setting the scene, though I’d leave the battle over opera boxes to Mrs. Astor and Bertha.

A Glass for Agnes—or Ada

I’m more than happy to borrow the show’s habit of pouring wine into crystal glasses. Mine usually contains Champagne, but Ada’s season-three temperance campaign gives us a reason to put some thought into an alcohol-free option too.

Agnes’s feelings about wine appear to be settled, but there’s room for both sisters.

  • Champagne or sparkling wine: I’d choose a bottle I enjoy and pour it into a favorite glass. There’s simply no need to improve on a good Champagne.
  • A Champagne cocktail: Brandy and bitters give a Champagne cocktail a little extra ceremony, which is something perfectly befitting of a Gilded Age watch party.
  • An alcohol-free spritz: Sparkling water with cranberry juice and an orange twist would be my contribution to Ada’s cause, but any mocktail would make an equally good toast to the evening. I’d use the same glassware and leave the pledge unsigned.
  • Tea: Some evenings call for a pot of black tea and a little milk. I like bringing the pot into the living room on a tray.

Small Bites for High Society

The Russells may require a meticulously planned succession of courses, but I’d be content with a small selection of savory bites. Oysters, warm crab dip or little sandwiches would make a perfectly elegant dinner by the fire.

  • Oysters: Oysters make frequent appearances at the show’s lavish dinners, and a few with lemon would be enough to make the evening feel special. Our Oysters Rockefeller are baked with butter, spinach and Romano. And with John D. Rockefeller reportedly arriving this season, his namesake oysters certainly deserve a place at the table.
  • Crab: Marian’s visit to the Scotts is difficult to forget, though I’d rather take inspiration from their crab luncheon than her assumptions. A lovely crab meat au gratin would bring some of that indulgence home, served warm with toasted bread or crackers.
  • Asparagus: Few vegetables receive such careful instruction on how to eat them. After Bannister explains the difference between English and American asparagus etiquette, the Russell kitchen decided to avoid the matter altogether. I’d serve our roasted asparagus anyway, with a squeeze of lemon, and trust everyone to choose their own utensils.
  • Tea sandwiches: Mamie Fish’s doll-themed tea party is more commitment than I’m looking for, but I can appreciate the sandwiches. Cucumber and cream cheese, butter with thinly sliced radish or chicken salad would all be good on soft bread.

Dinner Across Fifth Avenue

For something more substantial, you can look to the show’s luncheons as much as its elaborate dinner parties. A bowl of soup or a sandwich can feel quite grand when the ingredients are good and someone else is responsible for the evening’s drama.

  • Clam chowder: A bowl of New England clam chowder is enough to win over Reverend Forte though it does little to impress Agnes. I’m with Ada on this one. Our New England clam chowder has tender potatoes, clams and a creamy broth that sounds perfect for a November season premiere.
  • Lobster rolls: The lobster left behind after Bertha’s disastrous first reception deserves a more appreciative audience. Our lobster rolls put that idea into buttery toasted buns, making them much more convenient for my own seating arrangements.
  • Steak: George’s Arizona business trip includes a steak order in a saloon, a considerable change of scenery from the Russell dining room. A steak seared in a hot skillet and finished with butter and fresh herbs would be a fitting way to bring a little of the show’s indulgence home.
  • Salmon: The exquisite salmon platters at Bertha’s dinners are hard to overlook, even among all the other beautiful dishes. For an easy at-home version, borrow the fish and simplify the presentation: a grilled fillet with lemon and garlic would be perfect for any cozy viewing party.

Dessert in the Drawing Room

A pot of tea and something sweet would be enough for me on an evening when dinner is already behind us. I like having dessert ready before I sit down, whether that means a trifle waiting in the fridge or a few pastries brought home in a paper bag.

  • Trifle: Bannister’s assessment of trifle as a nursery food seems unnecessarily limiting. Layers of cake, cream and fruit have plenty to offer the rest of us. Our strawberry trifle would be a good place to start. A splash of sherry over the cake would move it a little further from the nursery.
  • French pastries: Aurora Fane’s gathering put me in the mood for a plate of French pastries. Buy a few macarons, perhaps something chocolate, and let the bakery determine how ambitious the evening becomes. Arrange the pastries on a pretty plate, and voila!
  • Apple pie: For all the elaborate food in the show, apple pie is an appealing return to something familiar. Armstrong’s visit to her mother casts it in an unhappy light, but I’m prepared to overlook that for a warm slice with a buttery crust.
  • Ice cream: Ice cream has Agnes’s approval, which is no small recommendation. Strawberry would be a fitting choice from the period. A scoop in a small glass bowl with a crisp cookie would be all the dessert I’d need.

Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

1. Build an American Charcuterie Board

Spot the savings! The imported Swiss Gruyère on the right costs $7.97 for 6 oz., while the Wisconsin-made Gruyère right next to it is just $4.87 for 7 oz. That’s $3.10 less for an EXTRA ounce!

The U.S. produces excellent artisanal cheeses! Pair your American-made cheeses with domestic cured salami, crisp sliced apples, local honey, and candied Georgia pecans. If your holiday tradition calls for a celebratory toast, you might need to leave the French Champagne and Italian Prosecco behind. Luckily, California, Oregon, and Washington state produce exceptional sparkling wines at a fraction of the cost of imported bottles, giving you festive bubbles without paying international shipping and tariff penalties! If alcohol isn’t in your budget, period, swap it out and save yourself a bunch of money by making a festive mocktail instead!

3. The Real Math: Why We Retired Our Holiday Pot Roast

At Budget Bytes, we don’t typically build holiday recipes around $70 rib roasts, but a cozy beef chuck roast used to be one of our favorite reasonably-priced celebratory centerpieces. Historically, our absolute max to keep a recipe accessible on the site is staying under $3.50 per serving.

  • 2024 Beef Pot Roast (8 Servings): A 2.61 lb chuck roast ran $17.33. Combined with basic vegetables, bouillon, and seasonings, the whole pot cost $25.86, or $3.23 per serving.

Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 DMZ Review

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This is a standalone review that covers only Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4's DMZ mode. You can find our campaign and multiplayer review here.

Extraction shooters can feel a little like a modern trend, with some heavy hitters like Marathon and Arc Raiders shaking the table within the last year or so, but they’ve been a growing experiment in the shooter space for the past decade or so. Call of Duty dabbled in it with the original DMZ back in 2022 (you could even argue they were late to the party by then), and its return in 2026 is timely. I’ve logged quite a few bootsteps through the bombed out streets, scattered woodlands, and high security bases of this big new mode over the last week, and after hours of vehicle hijacking, SAM site hacking, and target elimination, I can say this bigger, better DMZ is a welcome newcomer to the extraction shooter slate.

The PvPvE dynamic of extraction shooters often lives and dies by how well the mission and level design encourages you to explore, rewards you for overcoming challenges, and guides you to interact with others in order to keep those spoils. The fictional Korean Hajin region your exploits take place in is well suited for this. Its dense pockets of urban development – a village in the north, the dangerous main city in the east, an isolated prison out west – are separated by rocky forests concealing all sorts of militarized checkpoints and other points of interest hiding loot to shove into your pockets.

Parts of it look a little samey, the consequence of modern urban development where (at least from the outside) most buildings look the same, especially compared to some of DMZ’s big sci-fi competitors that go above and beyond to look striking at first glance. But the map is so big that you could spend the entirety of your approximately half-hour runs in only a fraction of it. And where Battle Royales (like CoD’s own Warzone) move players around by having an encroaching death cloud forcing everyone into a smaller and smaller area, DMZ instead entices squads to adventure with its Dynamic Operations.

Dynamic Ops are spontaneous missions that appear as you travel across the battlefield which come in lots of forms, from securing a package to blowing up a target to escorting a VIP. Many of them are multi-stage affairs that have your fireteam moving from point to point as NPC enemies ramp up a more coordinated defense against you. These all get rewarded by supply drops that grant you loot, the value of which escalates based on how many of them you successfully complete. I prefer this sort of objective chasing to the more standard task lists in other games like this (which also exist here, in fairness) because it makes for more engaging individual runs. I’ll prepare the best I can beforehand, but can’t be ready for every possible string of operations once I deploy.

There are many actions and events, like aforementioned supply drops, that are broadcast to everyone within earshot, hypothetically to tempt other players to be nosy (and potentially belligerent). Despite those lures, open skirmishes with others have been pretty limited, as I’ve found most people opt to mind their own business. The influx of players at launch hasn't really changed the main emphasis on the E in the PvPvE structure of these runs, though human enemies can regularly play spoiler to otherwise solid runs if you’re not diligent.

There was a base level of tension with every step I took or round I let off.

It would be a mistake to confuse passivity with pacifism – this isn’t the Arc Raiders playerbase, which spent many of the opening months of that game more interested in helping than hurting. Regardless of the trait investment, how good or bad I did in a run came down to how few bullets I ate in the process, and these trees, though valuable in specific cases, really didn't change that.

A personalised mRNA vaccine for melanoma, the deadliest form of skin cancer, is expected to be approved for use in the US, Europe and the UK next year. The vaccine is designed to stimulate immune responses against genetic mutations that are unique to an individual’s cancer. When used alongside standard treatment in a late-stage trial, it substantially cut the risk of cancer recurrence or death within the next two years.

“I think approval could be quick, and next year sounds reasonable,” says Alan Melcher at the Institute of Cancer Research in London, who wasn’t involved in the trial.

“It is a huge deal,” says Rebecca Lee at the University of Manchester, UK, who also wasn’t involved. Thousands of people in the UK alone could benefit from this, based on the trial’s patient population, she says. “It’s highly likely the US and Europe [including the UK] will approve it next year.” However, producing the vaccine is very expensive, and it may not be deemed cost-effective for use on the UK’s National Health Services (NHS), she says.

In August, drug-maker Moderna announced that the vaccine had positive results in a late-stage trial. More specific findings have now been released.

The trial recruited more than 1100 adults who had surgery to remove high-risk melanomas that were likely to recur or spread to distant sites. In the UK, 19,400 melanoma cases are diagnosed annually, of which about 25 per cent are deemed high risk.

Two-thirds of the participants were randomly assigned to receive a bespoke vaccine on top of standard immunotherapy, called pembrolizumab, which helps the immune system find and destroy cancer. Both were delivered every three weeks for up to a year. The remaining participants received placebo injections plus pembrolizumab.

The vaccine is made by genetically sequencing a small sample of a person’s cancer and comparing that sequence with the DNA in the individual’s healthy blood cells. This enables researchers to pinpoint mutations specific to the cancer.

Based on the same technology used in covid-19 vaccines, the cancer vaccine contains mRNA molecules that encode for proteins with up to 34 of those mutations. “The jab contains a fingerprint of proteins specifically associated with the cancer,” says Lee. “This helps [immune] T-cells recognise the cancer better, and then pembrolizumab prevents those T-cells from becoming exhausted once they’ve started attacking the cancer cells.”

About two years after the start of treatment, 82 per cent of the participants who received the vaccine plus pembrolizumab were alive without their cancer recurring, on average, compared with 70 per cent among those on pembrolizumab alone. “It’s a really exciting result,” says Lee. Side effects were similar between the two groups.

The results combine cancer recurrence and death rates into one figure, but since a treated cancer has to recur for it to cause death, this combined measure should be interpreted mostly as a sign of the vaccine reducing recurrence, says Melcher. The participants haven’t been tracked for long enough to robustly determine whether the vaccine improves survival rates. These results should be available within a few years, says Lee.

Sourdough Is a Living Fermentation System

Traditional breadmaking is largely a rapid gas-production process driven by baker’s yeast. Sourdough is different. Sourdough ecosystems contain:

  • wild yeasts
  • lactic acid bacteria (LAB)
  • endogenous cereal enzymes
  • organic acid production pathways
  • extended fermentation cycles

These systems dynamically alter:

  • starches
  • proteins

  • sugars
  • and importantly… fibers.

The fermentation process literally remodels fiber structures during proofing and fermentation. This is not passive nutrition fortification. This is active biochemical transformation. Recent research has shown sourdough fermentation significantly alters arabinoxylan structure, molecular weight distribution, fermentability, and water extractability in wheat systems.   • dough rheology
  • gas retention
  • viscosity
  • crumb structure

  • glycemic response
  • downstream short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production

The bakery aisle suddenly starts looking suspiciously like a gut health aisle wearing a beret.

  • microbial metabolism
  • acidification behavior
  • enzymatic interactions
  • texture development
  • fermentation efficiency

  • Support flavor carry in vanilla, chocolate, dairy, fruit, brown, and roasted profiles.
  • Help a formula feel more complete when sugar has been reduced or removed.

Used badly, R10 can overstay its welcome. Too much can leave a long sweet tail, blur crisp flavor notes, or make the product taste as though sweetness is still playing after the band has packed up and gone home.

The winning move is simple: build the formula correctly first, then use R10 at low ppm levels to polish the sensory experience.

What R10 Is, and What It Is Not

R10 is a natural flavor-modulation tool designed to shape the quality, timing, and persistence of sweetness within a finished formula. It is not just another entry in a sweetness-equivalence spreadsheet.

A product can be sweet enough on paper and still taste like it lost a bar fight with a bag of pennies. That is because sweetness intensity and sweetness quality are two different things. A formula has to be evaluated for:

  • How quickly sweetness arrives.
  • How it behaves at peak intensity.
  • Whether the middle feels hollow or connected.
  • Whether bitterness, metallic notes, or licorice character poke their heads up.
  • How long the sweetness and flavor remain after swallowing.

R10 helps smooth that curve. It can make sweetness arrive more gracefully, connect the mid-palate, soften the transition into the finish, and help the entire flavor system feel more intentional. R10 is not:

  • A bulk sweetener.
  • A one-for-one sucrose replacement.
  • A replacement for solids, body, browning, water activity, or texture.
  • A cure for a poor sweetener system.
  • Permission to toss a bitter stevia blend into a product and pray for miracles.

Think of it as the final 10% of sensory refinement. And, as with most things in formulation, the final 10% is often where the bodies are buried.

The Real Job: Shaping the Sweetness Curve

Sucrose remains the gold standard because it is maddeningly good at its job. It arrives predictably, peaks cleanly, carries flavor, adds body, and leaves without leaving muddy footprints all over the finish.

Most high-intensity sweeteners have a different game plan. Some come on too sharply. Some build slowly. Some linger too long. Some bring bitterness, metallicity, licorice, or a weird hollow gap between sweetness and flavor.

R10 helps the total system behave more cohesively. Its value is often not “more sweet.” Its value is better sweet:

  • More rounded onset.
  • More continuity through the mid-palate.
  • Less perception of bitter transitions.
  • Better flavor integration.
  • More satisfying finish.
  • Improved perceived fullness and sweetness persistence.

That is why it can have such a large sensory impact at very low addition levels. In reduced-sugar formulation, a few ppm can be the difference between “that’s pretty good for sugar free” and “wait…this has less sugar?”

That second reaction is where the money lives.

R10 works best in products that are already close, but not quite there. The product has acceptable sweetness and a competent flavor system, but it lacks the smooth, connected, sugar-like experience consumers expect.

Beverages

  • Sparkling waters

  • Energy drinks
  • Sports beverages

  • Coffee beverages
  • Protein RTDs
  • Dairy and plant-based beverages
  • Nutrition shakes

R10 is particularly useful when the product has an abrupt sweetener spike, a hollow middle, bitterness from minerals or protein, or a finish that feels disconnected from the flavor.

In carbonated beverages, do not expect R10 to single-handedly fight acid, carbonation bite, aggressive citrus oil, and a rough sweetener system all at once. It can help knit the profile together, but the acid curve and top notes must still be built intelligently.

Dairy, Dairy-Adjacent, and Protein Systems

Milk, flavored milk, yogurt, frozen desserts, creamers, and protein beverages are prime territory.

Protein, minerals, heat treatment, and stabilizer systems can all make a sweetener system taste chalky, bitter, thin, or metallic. R10 can help soften those edges and support dairy-associated notes such as:

  • Vanilla
  • Chocolate
  • Caramel
  • Cream
  • Brown sugar
  • Coffee
  • Malt

It does not make a poor protein system disappear. It simply helps the product stop announcing every compromise through a megaphone.

Bars, Bakery, and Snacks

Reduced-sugar bars and baked goods are sensory obstacle courses: protein, fiber, polyols, cocoa, minerals, heat, shelf-life drift, and enough water-activity drama to keep a formulator awake at night.

R10 can help improve sweetness continuity, round bitter notes, and support flavor persistence. But it cannot replace sugar’s functional work:

  • Spread
  • Browning
  • Tenderness
  • Humectancy
  • Water activity control
  • Bulk
  • Volume
  • Texture

That still requires real formula engineering. There is no fairy dust. There is only chemistry wearing a tiny cape.

  • Reduced-sugar chocolate systems
  • Chewy bars

Sauces, Syrups, and Fruit Systems

R10 can also be highly effective in:

  • Dessert sauces

These products often have high acid, strong fruit or roasted notes, and a need for sweetness to survive after dilution or application. R10 can help keep that sweetness and flavor experience connected.

The Right Formulation Sequence

The fastest route to a bad outcome is adding R10 to a broken base formula and expecting it to become a superhero. Build the formula in this order.

Step 1: Build the Functional Base First

Establish the product’s:

  • Baseline sweetness
  • Solids
  • Body
  • Cost
  • Processing tolerance
  • Water activity
  • Texture
  • Nutritional target

Depending on the application, this may involve sucrose, allulose, tagatose, soluble fibers, polyols, stevia, monk fruit, or another sweetener system.

R10 should not be asked to carry the workload of missing 8 to 10°Brix. If the product is thin, fix body. If it is flat, fix flavor. If it is bitter, inspect the sweetener, protein, minerals, cocoa, and process.

Then use R10 to refine the result.

Step 2: Set Acid and Salt Balance

Acid and salt do not merely change flavor. They change how sweetness is perceived. Before optimizing R10:

  • Finalize pH.
  • Confirm titratable acidity.
  • Establish the target acid blend.
  • Evaluate sodium and mineral contribution.
  • Confirm flavor loading.

A bright lemon beverage with too much citric acid may not need more modulation. It may need a smarter acid system. Formulation is often less about adding more players and more about getting the existing team to stop tripping over one another.

Step 3: Add R10 Late in Development

Once the formula is 80 to 90% right, screen R10 at controlled levels. Evaluate more than initial sweetness:

  • Sweetness onset
  • Peak sweetness
  • Mid-palate continuity
  • Bitterness
  • Metallic notes
  • Flavor identity
  • Flavor persistence
  • Aftertaste quality at 15, 30, and 60 seconds

Step 4: Revisit Flavor Load

If R10 improves flavor carry, the existing flavor may suddenly become too loud. This is often a win. You may be able to reduce flavor load slightly while improving balance and reducing cost. A product can taste more natural not because you added more flavor, but because the flavor now has a better place to land.

Step 5: Validate After Process and Through Shelf Life

Bench perfection is halftime. Taste the product:

  • Before processing
  • After processing
  • At pilot scale
  • After packaging
  • At realistic shelf-life intervals

Test after pasteurization, UHT, baking, extrusion, freezing, retort, or whatever cage match the product must survive. Reduced-sugar systems drift. Protein systems drift. Flavors drift. The calendar is undefeated.

Processing and Quality-Control Guardrails

Addition Method

At ppm levels, uniform distribution is the whole ballgame. For dry systems:

  • Create a validated geometric premix with a compatible dry ingredient.
  • Add the premix early enough to achieve uniform distribution.
  • Confirm mixer performance and blend time.

For liquid systems:

  • Prepare a compatible pre-dispersion according to the product specification.
  • Add with sufficient mixing.
  • Avoid adding at a point where the system is already too viscous for distribution.

For viscous systems:

  • Use a slurry or preblend.
  • Add before viscosity climbs high enough to sabotage dispersion.
  • Confirm distribution through retained samples and sensory comparison.

For plant scale:

  • Verify actual addition through batch records.
  • Use a gold-standard retain.
  • Compare pilot and production sensory results.
  • Do not assume “we added it” means “we distributed it.”

Heat and pH

The exact operating window must follow the current R10 technical documentation and be verified in the finished product. The practical rule is straightforward: Test R10 before and after the real process, at the product’s actual pH, solids, packaging conditions, and intended shelf life.

Do not casually assume that a clean bench sample will survive UHT, retort, high shear, a bakery oven, or six months in a warehouse with questionable air conditioning.

Regulatory and Labeling

R10 is intended to function as a flavor-modulation system. Labeling and permitted use must be confirmed using the current supplier documentation and the regulations of the target market.

“Natural” is not a vibes-based claim. It is a documentation and regulatory question. Let the dossier do its job.

A Sensory Protocol That Produces Useful Answers

A loose “I think Sample C is smoother” conversation after six samples is how good people end up arguing with ghosts. Use a repeatable process:

  1. Make a matched control with no R10.
  2. Create a controlled R10 ladder, typically titrate to 10, 20, 30, and 40 ppm.
  3. Blind-code all samples.
  4. Serve at the same temperature, carbonation level, serving size, and age.
  5. Score sweetness onset, peak, roundness, bitterness, flavor fidelity, and finish on a 0 to 10 scale.
  6. Capture aftertaste observations at 15, 30, and 60 seconds.
  7. Select the lowest dose that delivers the sensory objective.
  8. Repeat after processing and at shelf-life checkpoints.

The lowest effective dose is usually the best dose. It protects cost, creates sensory headroom, and reduces the chance that normal process variation turns “polished” into “why is this still sweet in the parking lot?”

An analysis of tightening guar supply, rising raw material costs, and the implications for food and beverage manufacturers using partially hydrolyzed guar gum as a soluble dietary fiber.

A tightening guar market creates new challenges for PHGG

The global guar market is experiencing significant price pressure, and manufacturers incorporating partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG) into their formulations should be paying attention.

According to an October 9, 2026 supplier market update, guar splits prices have increased approximately 30% since April, including a nearly 5% increase in November futures on October 8 alone.

While much of the market discussion has focused on conventional guar gum, the implications extend to PHGG, a specialized, enzymatically hydrolyzed derivative of guar gum used primarily as a soluble dietary fiber.

PHGG occupies a very different functional position from conventional guar gum. Rather than providing substantial viscosity or thickening, PHGG is manufactured to deliver soluble fiber with relatively low viscosity, making it particularly useful in beverages, nutritional products, powdered supplements, and other applications where manufacturers want to increase fiber content without dramatically changing texture.

That distinction is important because PHGG manufacturers depend on the same underlying agricultural supply chain as conventional guar gum producers.

When guar seed and guar splits become more expensive or less available, PHGG producers face increased raw material costs, even though the final ingredient undergoes additional specialized processing.

For food and beverage manufacturers, the concern is not simply whether PHGG prices will rise. It is whether raw material volatility could affect the cost, availability, and continuity of supply of an increasingly important functional fiber.

Understanding the connection between guar splits and PHGG

PHGG begins with guar seed, primarily cultivated in India. The seed is processed to separate the endosperm, which contains the galactomannan polysaccharides responsible for guar gum’s functional properties.

The manufacturing process generally follows this sequence: Guar seed → Guar splits → Guar gum → Controlled enzymatic hydrolysis → Purification and drying → PHGG

Conventional guar gum consists of high-molecular-weight galactomannans capable of producing significant viscosity in aqueous systems. 

During PHGG production, controlled enzymatic hydrolysis reduces the molecular weight of these polysaccharides, producing a soluble fiber ingredient with substantially lower viscosity. The resulting material retains much of the underlying carbohydrate structure while exhibiting different rheological and functional characteristics.

This additional processing creates a more specialized ingredient, but it does not eliminate dependence on the original guar crop. A disruption at the agricultural level can therefore propagate through the entire value chain, from guar splits to finished PHGG.

However, a 30% increase in guar splits prices does not automatically translate into a 30% increase in PHGG prices. Finished PHGG costs also reflect processing yields, enzyme treatment, purification, drying, quality assurance, transportation, and producer margins.

The extent of price transmission will depend on the manufacturer’s cost structure, raw material inventory, and contractual commitments.

The 2026 supply picture

Preliminary estimates cited in the October supplier update suggest that India’s 2026 guar harvest may be limited to approximately six million bags, representing around 600,000 metric tons of guar seed.

Based on an estimated 28% recovery rate, that crop could yield approximately 168,000 metric tons of guar splits.

The supplier also reports combined guar gum and guar splits exports averaging approximately 25,000 metric tons monthly over the previous year.

These figures suggest a potentially tight raw material environment, although they should not be interpreted as a precise supply deficit. Finished guar gum and guar splits represent different stages of processing, and the market’s actual balance depends on conversion yields, domestic consumption, carryover inventories, and available stocks. Nevertheless, several reported developments deserve attention.

Farmers are reportedly withholding guar seed in anticipation of higher prices. Speculative traders are purchasing guar splits, potentially reducing the quantities immediately available to industrial processors. Meanwhile, uncertainty surrounding the final crop size makes forward procurement more difficult.

For PHGG producers, this combination can create two overlapping challenges: rising input costs and increased competition for suitable raw material.

Unlike conventional guar gum, PHGG is not necessarily a commodity that manufacturers can replace quickly with another supplier’s material. Differences in molecular weight distribution, fiber concentration, residual viscosity, solubility, sensory characteristics, and regulatory documentation can make supplier qualification more complicated. That increases the importance of securing consistent, specification-compliant supply.

Why PHGG manufacturers face a different risk profile

The market for PHGG is more specialized than the market for conventional guar gum. PHGG production requires controlled enzymatic processing and additional quality controls to achieve the desired molecular weight distribution, solubility, low viscosity, and dietary fiber specifications.

These characteristics are particularly important for manufacturers using PHGG in clear or lightly flavored beverages, nutritional powders, functional foods, and digestive wellness products.

PHGG is valued because it can deliver meaningful quantities of soluble fiber without the substantial thickening associated with conventional guar gum. Published research also describes its suitability for certain acidic and thermally processed food systems, although stability must be confirmed under the specific formulation and processing conditions. 

This specialization introduces additional supply-chain considerations. A producer facing higher guar splits costs cannot necessarily switch to a different raw material without evaluating its impact on processing efficiency and finished-product specifications. Likewise, a food manufacturer purchasing PHGG cannot assume that all commercially available grades will behave identically.

For manufacturers developing fiber-fortified products, a supply disruption could therefore mean more than paying a higher price. It could require requalification of an ingredient that plays an important nutritional and functional role in the formulation.

Independent industry reporting reinforces the concern surrounding guar prices. A September market analysis documented substantial increases in Indian guar seed and guar gum futures, citing lower acreage, weak monsoon conditions, and restricted supply.  More recently, an October 7 report from Rantec Corporation identified continued price pressure associated with dry growing conditions and reduced planting acreage.

by Thom King CFS – Founder, Chief Innovation Officer, Food Scientist, Icon Foods

Allulose is one molecule. Same six carbons. Same molecular weight. Same approximately 70% sweetness relative to sucrose. Same reducing-sugar chemistry. Put it in a formulation in the United States and you have one regulatory conversation. Drive that formulation north into Canada and the conversation changes. Fly it across the Atlantic into the European Union and somebody takes the ball away entirely.

Welcome to international food regulation, where chemistry remains stubbornly consistent while regulatory classifications develop frequent-flyer personalities.

For food and beverage formulators, allulose provides a particularly fascinating case study because the science surrounding the molecule is increasingly mature, while regulatory acceptance remains decidedly geographical. As of August 2026:

United States: allulose is commercially used in conventional foods under GRAS conclusions and enjoys unusually favorable nutrition-label treatment.

Canada: allulose does not appear on Health Canada’s current List of Permitted Sweeteners for conventional foods, although D-allulose does appear as a non-medicinal ingredient in some licensed natural health products.

European Union: allulose has not been authorized for general food use under the EU Novel Food Regulation.

Same molecule. Three regulatory scoreboards. Let’s unpack this.

First: What Exactly Is Allulose?

Allulose, historically called D-psicose, is a monosaccharide and the C-3 epimer of fructose. Translation from food-science Klingon: Take fructose. Rearrange the stereochemistry around one carbon. Suddenly the resulting molecule behaves very differently metabolically.

Commercial allulose is typically manufactured through enzymatic epimerization of fructose, followed by purification and concentration or crystallization. Allulose delivers roughly 70% of the sweetness of sucrose, although perceived sweetness varies with the food matrix, temperature, acidity, flavor system and accompanying sweeteners. But sweetness isn’t why formulators get excited about allulose. We’ve already got plenty of molecules that can make things sweet. The magic is that allulose can perform some of the physical jobs of sugar. It provides bulk. It contributes solids. It participates in Maillard browning. It affects freezing depression point. It contributes to mouthfeel. It can modify water activity.

And its temporal sweetness profile is considerably more sugar-like than many high-intensity sweeteners. That makes allulose something much more useful than simply another sweetener. It is a functional carbohydrate. And that’s where regulatory classification starts getting interesting.

Three Countries Walk Into a Formulation Lab

Imagine we’ve developed a reduced-sugar protein bar containing allulose. Same formula. Same manufacturing process. Same finished product. Now we’re going to sell it in three markets. Here’s the regulatory snapshot.

Market Current Position Practical Formulator Takeaway United States Commercially used in conventional foods under GRAS conclusions Broad formulation opportunity Canada Not listed as a permitted conventional-food sweetener Do not assume U.S. formulation is Canadian-compliant European Union Not currently authorized for general food use as a novel food Cannot simply launch the U.S. formulation in the EU

That table should be taped to the wall of every formulation lab developing global reduced-sugar products. Because one of the most expensive sentences in food development is: “We already finished the formula. Can Regulatory check it now?” No. Regulatory should have been in the huddle before kickoff.

The United States: Allulose Gets the Ball

The United States has taken the most commercially accommodating position toward allulose. Multiple allulose ingredients have been the subject of GRAS notices to FDA, supporting use across a broad range of food categories. But the regulatory development that really changed the game came from nutrition labeling. FDA issued guidance allowing allulose to be excluded from the amounts declared for Total Sugars and Added Sugars on the Nutrition Facts label.

FDA also indicated that manufacturers may use 0.4 kcal/g when calculating calories from allulose. That is a radically different nutritional treatment from ordinary sugars. For formulators, this creates an unusual combination: Sugar-like functionality without conventional sugar labeling treatment. That’s a hell of a formulation tool.

Then We Drive North

Cross the Canadian border and things change. This is where American formulators need to resist a very dangerous assumption: “FDA accepts it, therefore Canada probably does too.” Canada is not FDA with better hockey. Health Canada operates under its own regulatory framework. Health Canada’s List of Permitted Sweeteners identifies sweeteners authorized as food additives, the foods in which they may be used and their conditions of use. Allulose does not currently appear on that list. That means formulators should not simply take a U.S. product containing allulose as a sweetener, slap bilingual packaging on it and ship it north. That’s not regulatory strategy. That’s more like regulatory roulette.

But Canada Has an Interesting Allulose Wrinkle

Here’s where Canada becomes particularly interesting. Health Canada’s databases show D-allulose being used as a non-medicinal ingredient in licensed natural health products. That tells us something important, but it needs to be interpreted correctly. It does not establish broad authorization for allulose as a sweetener in conventional foods. A natural health product and a conventional food operate under different regulatory frameworks. This is exactly the kind of nuance that gets murdered in a Google search. Someone finds a Canadian product containing allulose and concludes: “Allulose is approved in Canada.” Not necessarily. The regulatory question is not simply: Does some product somewhere in Canada contain allulose? The correct question is: Is allulose legally permitted for my intended use, at my intended level, in my specific product category under the regulatory framework governing that product? That’s the question formulators need to ask every single time.

Canada’s Novel Food System Adds Another Layer

Canada also has a pre-market safety framework for novel foods. Health Canada describes novel foods as foods or ingredients that do not have a history of safe food use or foods produced through processes that create a major change. Novel foods require safety assessment before sale in Canada. Health Canada evaluates matters including:

  • composition;
  • nutritional quality;
  • potential toxicity;
  • allergenicity;
  • dietary exposure;
  • manufacturing methods; and
  • other safety considerations.

But Canada’s regulatory framework and Europe’s Novel Food Regulation should not be treated as interchangeable. The fact that an ingredient encounters regulatory hurdles in both jurisdictions doesn’t mean those hurdles are legally identical.

For a Canadian commercialization project involving allulose, the prudent move is therefore to establish the ingredient’s regulatory status and intended use with Health Canada before locking the commercial formulation. The regulatory classification comes first. The ingredient purchase order comes considerably later.

Now We Fly to Europe

Europe gives allulose an entirely different workout. Under Regulation (EU) 2015/2283, foods without a history of significant consumption within the European Union before May 15th 1997 may fall within the Novel Food Regulation. Allulose does. Novel foods require authorization before being placed on the EU market. The process essentially runs: Applicant → European Commission → EFSA scientific assessment → regulatory decision → Union List authorization. And allulose hasn’t crossed that finish line.

The 2025 EFSA Opinion

This is probably the most misunderstood part of the European allulose story. In June 2025, EFSA published a scientific opinion concerning D-allulose following a novel-food application. The headline conclusion was: The safety of D-allulose could not be established based on the available information. Stop there and it sounds terrible. Keep reading. EFSA identified data gaps involving areas including:

  • identity;
  • production process;
  • proposed uses and use levels;
  • genotoxicity; and
  • human data.

EFSA requested additional information from the applicant. According to EFSA, the applicant did not provide the requested additional information despite multiple contacts. EFSA therefore concluded that safety could not be established from the information available. That is not remotely the same scientific statement as: “Allulose has been demonstrated to be unsafe.” Those statements should never be conflated.

Safety Not Established Doesn’t Equal Unsafe

This distinction is Regulatory Science 101. Suppose we’re validating a thermal kill step. I ask for the time-temperature data. You hand me half the records. I ask for the missing records. You don’t send them. I cannot conclude the process is safe. But I also haven’t demonstrated that the process is unsafe. I have demonstrated something much less exciting: I don’t have enough data.

That’s essentially the problem EFSA encountered. The assessment failed to reach a positive safety conclusion because the dataset was insufficient. That leaves the scientific door open for another properly supported dossier. And several companies have shown interest in getting allulose through that door.

Why Europe Cares About Exposure

One of the biggest issues surrounding allulose authorization isn’t simply whether humans can tolerate a spoonful of the stuff. Regulators have to consider aggregate dietary exposure.

Imagine allulose becomes commercially successful. It’s no longer just in a protein bar. Now it’s in:

  • beverages;
  • yogurt;
  • cookies;
  • chocolate;
  • frozen desserts;
  • sauces;
  • cereal;
  • chewing gum;
  • nutrition bars; and
  • tabletop sweeteners.

A consumer could encounter allulose six times before dinner. That’s why proposed use levels matter. Gastrointestinal tolerance is particularly important for carbohydrates that are incompletely absorbed or metabolized differently from conventional sugars. The dose makes the digestive drama.

And Canada Will Care About Exposure Too

This is where the Canadian and European regulatory philosophies start to rhyme even though they aren’t singing exactly the same song. Health Canada also uses pre-market safety assessment when novel foods are involved. That means a serious Canadian allulose strategy would need to consider not merely: “Is allulose toxic?” but: “What happens when Canadians consume realistic quantities across the proposed food categories?” That’s a much better scientific question. Ingredient safety is rarely just about molecular identity. It’s: Identity × Dose × Exposure × Population × Food Matrix × Frequency. That equation should live in every formulator’s head.

Why Are We Fighting This Hard for One Sugar?

Because allulose can do something most high-intensity sweeteners cannot. It can work. Sugar reduction is frequently discussed as though sucrose has one job, sweetness. We all know that’s nonsense. Sucrose contributes:

  • sweetness;
  • bulk;
  • solids;
  • viscosity;
  • texture;
  • mouthfeel;
  • water activity;
  • freezing-point depression;
  • browning;
  • flavor development;
  • crystallization behavior; and
  • preservation effects.

Remove 100 grams of sugar and replace its sweetness with 300 milligrams of a high-intensity sweetener and congratulations: You’ve replaced the sweetness. You’ve also created a 99.7-gram hole in your formulation. Have fun filling it. Allulose helps fill that hole.

Allulose and Browning

Allulose is a reducing sugar, sucrose is not. Therefore it can participate readily in Maillard chemistry when amino compounds and appropriate processing conditions are present. This can be fantastic. Or it can torch your formulation. In bakery systems, bars, sauces and confectionery, allulose can create desirable browning and flavor development. But replacing sucrose one-for-one without reconsidering thermal processing can dramatically alter finished-product color and flavor. Your golden cookie can become a hockey puck with trust issues. Time, temperature, moisture, pH and protein content all matter.

Allulose in Frozen Desserts

Here’s another place allulose earns its paycheck. Allulose can strongly influence freezing-point depression. That means it can dramatically change:

  • hardness;
  • scoopability;
  • ice fraction;
  • melting behavior;
  • storage stability; and
  • sweetness balance.

A reduced-sugar ice cream therefore isn’t solved by simply asking: “How much allulose replaces the sugar?” The better question is: “What combination of carbohydrates, bulking agents and sweeteners gives me the sweetness, solids and freezing curve I need?” That’s formulation rather than substitution. Big difference.

Beverage Formulation

Beverages provide another excellent example. Removing sucrose eliminates more than sweetness. Body disappears. Flavor delivery changes. Acid perception changes. Aromatics change. Sweetness timing changes. Allulose can restore some of that missing body while contributing a relatively sugar-like sweetness profile.

But because allulose is less sweet than sucrose, using it alone may require more material than is commercially, sensorially or physiologically desirable. That’s where blended systems become powerful. Allulose can establish: bulk + mouthfeel + early sweetness. while high-intensity sweeteners or sweetness modulators close the remaining sweetness gap. Now we’re playing chess rather than Whac-A-Mole.

The Global Formulator’s Problem

This brings us to the bigger issue. Imagine you’re developing one product for: United States + Canada + European Union. If allulose forms the structural foundation of your formula, you may have just designed yourself into a regulatory corner. The U.S. version might work beautifully. The Canadian version may require a different regulatory strategy or reformulation. The EU version currently requires another solution altogether. So instead of thinking about one formula, think about a global formulation platform.

Build Modular Sweetness Systems

This is where formulators can get ahead of regulatory affairs instead of handing them a grenade with the pin already removed. Separate your sweetness system conceptually into functional modules:

  • Module 1: Bulk – What replaces sugar solids?
  • Module 2: Sweetness – What generates sweetness intensity?
  • Module 3: Temporal Profile – What provides sucrose-like onset and decay?
  • Module 4: Mouthfeel – What restores body and viscosity?
  • Module 5: Processing – What happens during baking, extrusion, UHT, retort or freezing?
  • Module 6: Nutrition – What happens to calories, sugars, carbohydrates and claims?

Now individual components can change by geography without forcing you to reinvent the entire product. That’s regulatory portability. And increasingly, it’s going to be a competitive advantage.

The Three-Market Scoreboard

The allulose situation can therefore be summarized pretty simply. United States, most commercially permissive of the three. Allulose is already being used broadly in food formulation, with particularly favorable FDA nutrition-label treatment. 

Canada, Proceed carefully. Allulose is not currently included in Health Canada’s List of Permitted Sweeteners for conventional foods. D-allulose does appear in certain licensed natural health products, but that should not be interpreted as blanket approval for conventional food applications. Talk to regulatory before commercialization. Preferably before you’ve ordered 40,000 printed wrappers.

European Union, not currently authorized for general food use. Allulose remains subject to the EU novel-food authorization process. EFSA’s 2025 assessment did not establish safety because requested supporting information was not supplied. That was an unsuccessful dossier assessment, not affirmative evidence that allulose is unsafe. For now.

The Bigger Lesson

Allulose exposes one of the most fascinating tensions in modern food science. We’re developing carbohydrates that don’t behave metabolically like the sugars our regulatory systems were built around tagatose, allulose, and other rare sugars coming behind them. The traditional regulatory buckets of sugar versus sweetener are getting increasingly uncomfortable. Because these molecules can be carbohydrates. They can provide bulk. They can participate in Maillard chemistry. They can affect freezing behavior. They can contribute sweetness. Yet physiologically they can behave very differently from sucrose or glucose. The molecules changed. The regulatory filing cabinets haven’t completely caught up.

Food For Thought

Allulose is an extraordinary example of why modern food formulators need to understand regulatory science almost as well as ingredient science. In the United States, allulose is already a serious commercial formulation tool.

In Canada, its use in conventional foods requires substantially more regulatory caution, and formulators should not infer broad food authorization from its appearance in licensed natural health products.

In the European Union, allulose remains outside general food authorization pending a successful novel-food pathway.

None of that changes the molecule. What changes is the regulatory framework surrounding it. And that’s the lesson formulate globally from day one. Don’t build a product for America and then try to force it through Canadian and European regulatory systems afterward. Build a sweetness architecture that can travel. Because in the next generation of sugar reduction, the winning formulations won’t merely taste good. They’ll taste good, perform properly, survive manufacturing, meet nutritional objectives and cross borders without Regulatory Affairs chasing the formulation team through the parking lot. That’s the real sweet spot.

Introduction

Can you tell us a bit about yourself? What town and region in Italy are you from?

I’m Milanese, born here in 1970. We opened Bela Tusa in May 2025 on Via Pietro Borsieri, in the Isola neighbourhood, together with Norma Galdamez, who is my business partner and a mixologist who runs the bar.

Isola is the neighbourhood where the Porta Nuova towers have been built. We’re on the ground floor, just a few metres from the development, and we cook food that Milanese families used to make at home.

On the walls of our trattoria are sixty years’ worth of photographs by Virgilio Carnisio documenting working-class Milan. The courtyards of the old tenement buildings, washing hanging out to dry, old-fashioned trattorias, and trades photographed as they were disappearing.

“Si mangia dentro la città che non ce l’ha fatta.”

Note from Emily: I left this last sentence in Italian as the direct translation is “you eat inside the city that didn’t make it”. It means that outside in the Isola neighbourhood, is a new and modern Milan, but inside the trattoria you are eating traditional food and looking back at photos of the old Milan, like stepping back in time.

Regional Cuisine

What do the locals eat in your region? Are there specific dishes your region or town is known for?

The first thing I’d like to say to anyone who arrives here in Milan is that it’s not a city of pasta and tomato sauce. It’s a city of rice and butter. The rice fields start right on the outskirts of the city, and for centuries butter has been the cooking fat here.

That’s where the whole repertoire comes from: yellow risotto with ossobuco; yellow because of saffron. Costoletta (cutlet) with the bone; Mondeghili, which are fried meatballs made with leftover boiled beef. Cassoeula, when the cold weather arrives. And beef cheek, the dish people talk about least but the one closest to our hearts.

It’s worth saying a little more about Mondeghili because the name itself carries the history of the city. Mondeghili comes from the Spanish word ‘albóndiga’, and before that from the Arabic ‘al-bunduqa’ which means small hazelnut. Milan was ruled by Madrid for 170 years, and it was the poorer side of the cuisine that preserved these words and names. A dish invented so that nothing would go to waste, with an Arabic name that arrived here through Spain.

Photo courtesy of Bela Tusa Trattoria

Italy is known for producing beautiful seasonal produce. How does the cuisine of your region change with the seasons?

Milanese cuisine is shaped by the plains and winter, and you can see it.

Autumn and winter are its true seasons. Cassoeula waits for the first cold weather, because the Savoy cabbage needs a frost before it’s good. It’s the dish we look forward to the most.

Spring and summer here lighten things up. Risotto remains but takes on something different. This summer we made risotto with figs, lemon zest and basil. It’s the dish that people wrote to us about the most.

There’s also something that isn’t seasonal but is part of everyday life, and that’s bread. The Milanese michetta is hollow inside, almost all crust, and you have to ask for it before ten o’clock. In the humidity of the Po Valley, a dense crumb becomes rubbery within a few hours, so the city developed a bread designed to be eaten the same day. By evening, it’s no longer the same, which is why it doesn’t appear in any guide.

Three things at the table and one outside it.

At the table: yellow risotto with ossobuco, mondeghili and costoletta. These are the three dishes we take seriously.
This cooking is not disappearing because of taste; it is disappearing because of time. Ossobuco needs three hours, and nobody has three hours. That is why it survives in trattorie and not in houses. The day a trattoria closes, you do not lose a restaurant; you lose the last place where that recipe was still being made all the way through.

Outside: go into a bakery before ten in the morning and ask for a michetta. We get our bread from Tondo, at 12 Via Cola Montano, just a short walk from here. It’s a tiny bakery run by Silvia Cancellieri, closed on Sundays and Mondays, and they describe themselves as a “radical bakery” rather than a bakery in the traditional sense. Organic flours, sourdough starter and the name of the farmer behind every flour.

You could eat there for just a few cents, and workers who couldn’t go home at lunchtime would have their lunch there. Anyone who wants to understand where Milanese cuisine comes from needs to know that it was born there, not in restaurants.

Ravioli in brodo (broth) and capon: these are the classic dishes that can’t be missing

A note from Gianmarco on mondeghili: they are a recovery dish. Nothing was bought to make them. Whatever boiled or braised meat was left from the day before went in, which in our kitchen right now means the trimmings from the ossobuchi and the cheeks. If you make them with meat bought for the purpose, you will get a good meatball, but not this one.

MONDEGHILI (Deep Fried Meatballs)

Serves 5 to 6, about 28 meatballs

Ingredients

  • 400 g stale bread, plus cold water to soak it
  • 200 g braised veal and beef, in our case the parts of the ossobuchi and the cheeks that do not go out on plates
  • 400 g lean minced beef
  • 150 g Grana Padano aged 12 months, grated
  • 4 eggs
  • 75 g mortadella, minced
  • 30 g salt
  • 3 g garlic powder
  • gremolada to taste: chopped parsley and grated lemon zest, in a ratio of 10 to 1
  • black pepper, ground, to taste
  • breadcrumbs for coating
  • seed oil for frying

Method

1. Soak the stale bread in cold water in a bowl.

2. Put the braised meat through the mincer while it is still warm, together with the mortadella and the softened bread.

3. Add the minced beef, the eggs, the salt, the pepper, the garlic powder, the gremolada and the grated Grana.

4. Work the mixture hard, until it is even and compact.

5. Shape them like ordinary meatballs, pressing the air out, then flatten them slightly.

6. Coat in breadcrumbs and fry in seed oil at 180 degrees.

Each one weighs 52 to 53 g raw. A portion is five.

Tips

Why each step is done that way, which is the part no recipe online gives you

The meat goes through the mincer warm, not cold, because warm it breaks down properly.

You work it hard because the mixture has to come out compact, or the meatballs fall apart in the pan.

You press the air out because any air left inside makes them crack while they cook.

You flatten the balls because that is how the old recipe wants them, otherwise, you have made a plain meatball. The traditional version is cooked in butter in a pan, and flattening them helps them cook through. We fry them, but the shape stayed. It is the fingerprint of a way of cooking nobody uses any more.

RUSUMADA (Red Wine Zabaglione)

Serves 2

This is the one Milanese people over sixty remember, and almost nobody makes any more. It was what a child was given when they were thin, or after an illness: egg yolk beaten with sugar, let down with red wine. Half medicine, half treat. We add cream, which the old version did not have.

  • 4 egg yolks
  • 38 g sugar
  • 275 ml red wine
  • 75 ml single cream

Beat the yolks with the sugar until pale and thick. Add the cream, then the wine, and keep beating until it is smooth. Serve cold, in a glass.

Tips

Since the yolks are not cooked: use pasteurised eggs, or the freshest you can find.

We make it in one batch of about six litres, and the proportions are what carry over: red wine 59%, yolk 16%, cream 16%, sugar 8%.

Strawberry Jam

Many traditional recipes use an equal amount of sugar to fruit, by weight, but I find it’s okay to decrease it to these proportions. Sugar is both a thickener and a preservation agent. If you reduce it too much more, your jam may not thicken and won’t keep for a long time. (Which is okay if you want thinner jam, you keep it in the refrigerator, and eat it relatively quickly.) This recipe can be scaled up or down, depending on how many strawberries you have. But if you want to make a lot more, best to make it in a couple of batches. To prepare the jars, pour boiling water into them, as well as into the lids. Let them sit 5-10 minutes, then carefully pour out the hot water and set them upside down on a cooling rack until ready to fill. Since I keep my jams in the refrigerator, I don’t put them up them in a traditional sense. I usually just overturn the jars and let them cool, which provides a reasonable seal, then refrigerate them. I find my jams keep well this way for up to one year. Check the National Center for Home Food Preservation website for instructions on how to can fruit jams. Note: Last season strawberries tend to have less natural pectin than ones that arrive earlier. So your jam may be less jelled. One strategy is the finely grate a green apple into the strawberries before cooking them, which adds pectin. (There are guidelines here for using liquid and powdered pectin.) I don’t mind the slightly runnier jam and it’s terrific on yogurt or served with fresh goat cheese for dessert.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds (900g) strawberries
  • 1 1/4 pounds (2 2/3 cups, 580g) sugar
  • one lemon organic or unsprayed
  • optional: kirsch (or eau-de-vie, or crème de cassis)

Instructions

  • Wash and hull the strawberries and cut them into quarters. If some are larger or smaller, just make sure the pieces all about the same size.
  • 2. Toss them in a large pot, such as a Dutch oven casserole made of a nonreactive material, with the sugar. Cut the lemon in half, squeeze the juice into the pot, and add the lemon halves to the berries. Stir well, cover, and let sit a couple of hours (up to 8 hours) at room temperature, stirring one or two times while they marinate.
  • Put a small plate in the freezer. Turn the heat on under the strawberries to medium-high and cook the strawberries, stirring occasionally, as they reduce and the juices thicken. If a lot of foam rises to the surface, skim it away. When the syrup is the consistency of warm maple syrup – it will take about 10 to 15 minutes or so, depending on the pot and berries, turn off the heat and put a spoonful of the jam on the frozen plate and return the plate to the freezer. Check it in a few minutes; if it wrinkles when you nudge it, it’s done. If not, return the plate to the freezer and cook the jam a little more, testing it again. It may take a few times before you get it to the right consistency.
  • When the strawberry jam is done, remove the lemon and stir in a few drops of kirsch, if using, then ladle the jam into the prepared jars.

Summer Fruit Galette

Normally apricots give up a lot of juice when baked, but these late-season ones didn't. Fruit varies in moisture but the nice thing about the layer of frangipane is that it keeps the tart dough crisp on the bottom, in case your fruit is juicy. Almond flour is sometimes called almond meal. You can use blanched or unblanched, which has a darker color. If you're avoiding almonds, feel free to swap in hazelnut or pistachio flour. You can certainly use other fruits in this tart, such as sliced nectarines, peaches (peeled or unpeeled), plums, or figs. You'll need about 1 1/2 pounds (700g) of fruit. More important than precise numbers to follow, it's best to try to push the fruit as close together as possible, or if using sliced fruit, to overlap it as much as possible, because it'll cook down during baking. You can also make this without the frangipane but you may want to sprinkle the bottom of the tart dough with some crumbled cookies (such as speculoos or Graham crackers) or moon dust if the fruit is particularly juicy. You can also swap out raspberries or blueberries for the cherries, using them to fill in the spaces between the fruit. I add the cherries at the beginning, but if using raspberries or blueberries, you can add them during the last 15 minutes or so of baking. Ingredients

For the galette dough

  • 1 1/4 cups (175g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 7 tablespoons (100g, 3 1/2 ounces) unsalted butter cubed and chilled
  • 3 1/2 to 4 tablespoons ice water

For the frangipane

  • 3/4 cup (95g) almond flour
  • 1/4 cup (50g) sugar
  • 2 tablespoons (20g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons (30g, 1 ounce) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 1/4 to 1/2 teaspoon pure almond extract
  • 1 large egg, at room temperature

For the fruit galette

  • 1 1/2 pounds (700g) fresh apricots, halved and pitted, or quartered if large (see headnote for other fruits that can be used)
  • 18-20 cherries, sweet or tart, pitted
  • 1 tablespoon (15g, 1/2 ounce) unsalted butter melted
  • 2 tablespoons coarse sugar (or granulated sugar)
  • strained apricot jam, for glazing the tart

Instructions

To make the dough

  • To make the dough, mix together the flour, sugar, and salt in a medium bowl, or in the bowl of a stand mixer.
  • Add the cubed butter and mix, using a pastry blender (or the paddle attachment if using a stand mixer), until the butter is in small, but still visible pieces, about the size of corn kernels.
  • Add the 3 1/2 tablespoons of ice water and mix until the dough starts to come together. If it feels dry, add an additional 1/2 tablespoon of ice water. Form the dough into a disk with your hands, wrap it in plastic wrap and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes.

To make the frangipane

  • In a small bowl, mix together the almond flour, sugar, and all-purpose flour. Add the butter and almond extract, and mix in with a spatula until smooth. Mix in the egg.

To make the tart

  • Preheat the oven to 375ºF (190ºC). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper.
  • On a lightly floured surface, roll the dough to a 14-inch (35cm) circle, turning it over and dusting lightly with flour, to keep it from sticking to the counter.
  • Brush off any excess flour and transfer the dough to the prepared baking sheet. (At this point, you may want to chill the dough for a few minutes, as the frangipane may be a bit tricky to spread if the dough feels warm.)
  • Smear the frangipane on the tart dough, leaving a 2-inch (5cm) rim around the edges. (If the frangipane is quite thick, you can dollop it in 5-6 places over the tart dough, which'll make it easier to spread.) Place the apricots on the frangipane and put cherries in the spaces between the apricots. Fold the edges of the dough over the fruit to make a crust. After pleating and folding the rim, make sure there are no "gullies" in the pleats for fruit juices to run out of. Pinch together any cracks.
  • Brush the rim of the crust generously with melted butter then brush the remaining butter over the fruit. Sprinkle the rim with about 1 tablespoon of coarse or granulated sugar. Then sprinkle another 1 tablespoon over the fruit. (If using fruits like nectarines and peaches, you can reduce the sugar to a few teaspoons.)
  • Bake the tart until the crust is well-browned and the fruit is cooked through, about 40-50 minutes. Remove from the oven and let the tart cool for 5 to 10 minutes, then brush the fruit with apricot jam.

Notes

Serving: Serve with a compatible ice cream, such as vanilla, crème fraîche or noyau ice cream. (The last two are in my books, The Perfect Scoop, and My Paris Kitchen, respectively), or another favorite flavor. A dollop of crème fraîche, sabayon, or softly whipped cream are other options. (Although often I like it just as it is.) Storage: The dough can be made up to two days ahead and refrigerated, or it can be frozen for up to two months. The tart is best enjoyed the same day it’s made, but is okay the second day.

Scottish Rocky Road is the coming together of two greats. The joy and simplicity of Rocky Road, a classic in the eyes of many and the taste and nostalgia-inducing wonder of two Scottish sweet treats.

Ok, so we might be overdoing it a bit, but seriously, Scottish Rocky Road is ridiculous. All the tastes and textures you’d hope for as a fan of Scottish food in such a basic recipe.

You’re going to love it.

What is Rocky Road?

As mentioned above, Scottish Rocky Road is the coming together of classics. An Aussie one and a Scottish one.

“Aussie?” we hear you cry. Yes, Australian. Rocky Road has a fascinating history and it certainly didn’t begin life where we had assumed it had.

Rocky Road is believed to have originated in Australia back in 1853. Interestingly, it wasn’t the creation of a skilled chef or a talented home baker but rather the initiative of business owners trying to salvage sweets that had been damaged during transit from Europe. They blended these confections with local nuts and chocolate.

The name “Rocky Road” comes from the rough paths that these entrepreneurial treats had to travel to reach the gold fields where they were sold. We love this story, and although some American companies claim to have invented Rocky Road, we prefer to believe this fascinating tale from the Southern Hemisphere!!

We make it Scottish by adding two of Tunnock’s best sweet treats. For those in the know, you’ll already be excited, but for those not sure who or what Tunnocks is, let me explain.

What is Tunnock’s?

Scottish Rocky Road is made “Scottish” with the edition of two things: Tunnock’s Tea Cakes and Tunnock’s Caramel Wafers. Both are uber-popular treats here in Scotland. Tunnock is the name of the company that, since 1890, has been making sugary snacks. They started life in Uddingston, Glasgow.

The Tunnock’s Carmel Wafer began life in 1952. It is a dry wafer covered in caramel and coated in milk chocolate. It’s chewy, sweet, and ridiculously moreish!

The Tunnock’s Teacake arrived a wee bit later in 1956, and is iconic in both shame, taste and even the silver and red foil it’s wrapped in! A Teacake is a biscuit topped with a marshmallow ball covered in a thin layer of milk chocolate.

They melt in your mouth with a flavour like nothing else. Children throughout Scotland have had chocolatey faces and sticky hands because of the famous treats, so they couldn’t be missed out of our Scottish Rocky Road.

What is Scottish Rocky Road?

Scottish Rocky Road is the simple coming together of the Australian traybake, one of the world’s most famous traybakes and two of Tunnock’s most popular Scottish confectionaries that are known and celebrated throughout Scotland and, in many cases, the world.

The two things go perfectly together, and the only thing you will dislike is how much of this you will end up eating!

  • Metal spoon for stirring the chocolate – Don’t use a wooden spoon as you don’t want the chocolate to seize, and wooden spoons can carry water!
  • Large sharp knife cut cutting the Tunnock’s sweets

Ingredients for Scottish Rocky Road

  • 400g Milk Chocolate
  • 100g Butter
  • 200g Tunnocks Teacakes
  • 200g Tunnocks Caramel Wafers
  • 50g white or dark chocolate for topping (optional)
  • 2 tbsp Golden Syrup (optional)

Why all the optional ingredients?

Given the amount of chocolate, caramel, and marshmallow in this Rocky Road, it can be very sweet!

Golden Syrup is good for breaking up the chocolate a bit and making it softer to eat, but does add to the sweetness so you can opt to not include it if you would prefer.

We like white chocolate but using dark chocolate, or no topping at all can always cut through the sweetness, as can adding raisins to the mix as well.

We suggest you make the recipe as is the first time around, and then adjust to your own tastes for the following times you make it (because we’re sure you will!).

Rocky Road is great because you can add anything you like to it and make it your own!

How to Make Scottish Rocky Road – Step by Step Method

Chop the Tunnocks Teacakes into quarters, lay them on a baking tray covered in baking paper and put them in the freezer. The more time, the better, so you may want to do this in advance.

Slice the Tunnocks Caramel Wafers into thin pieces.

Melt the chocolate, butter and golden syrup if using – this can be done in 30-second blasts in the microwave on medium heat, stirring in between with a metal spoon or in a double boiler on the stovetop (a bowl over a pot of simmering water).

Allow the melted chocolate mix to cool briefly.

Put the frozen Teacakes and sliced Caramel Wafers in a bowl along with raisins, if using them.

Pour over the melted chocolate mix and stir briefly and gently to coat the biscuits, but not too much or the marshmallow will all melt.

Tip into a lined 8-inch square baking tray and even out so there are no air gaps, but be careful not to melt the marshmallow.

Top with slices of Tunnocks Caramel Wafers.

Place in the refrigerator for a minimum of 2 hours to set.

Decorate with melted white or dark chocolate, slice and enjoy.

How do you stop the chocolate from seizing?

A common mistake when making Rocky Road is melting the chocolate too quickly, and it seizes. This is when it goes thick and grainy.

To avoid this, make sure you go “low and slow” with the heat. Do short bursts in the microwave on a reduced temperature, or a very low temperature on the stovetop if using a double boiler.

Stir regularly to make sure the chocolate is melting and you’re not overdoing the heat.

Introducing water can also cause the chocolate to seize, so you want to avoid steam in the stovetop or condensation in the microwave, another reason to keep temperatures low.

This is also why you should use a metal spoon to stir rather than wooden, as they can retain water.

If you do find the chocolate seizes, then you can generally save it by introducing a small amount of fat. Add tiny bits of butter or even olive oil and stir through gently so it melts and the chocolate becomes smooth again.

Though Elliston Place moved to a newer space next door a few years ago, it still features the soda counter and pressed-tin ceiling, which makes the milkshakes and sundaes taste like the good old days.

International Market

2013 Belmont Boulevard
(615) 297-4453

Mary Craven (Courtesy International Market)

International Market introduced Nashvillians to Thai food in the 1970s, when it opened on Belmont Boulevard serving an exceptionally affordable steam table lunch of Thai and Chinese dishes. Now, with a new location (across the street) and new ownership (Anna and Arnold Myint, children of the original owners), the restaurant incorporates high-quality local meats and produce in an updated setting—though thankfully the original mustard-and-orange bench seating came along for the ride. The kitchen is now a proving ground for chef Arnold Myint, a James Beard semifinalist. Menu items venture beyond the typical selections of curries with five-spice duck lo mein, shrimp and pineapple curry, and red curry rice ball wraps. At lunch, diners can choose from the classic steam table or order from the menu; nighttime brings table service with Singhas and craft cocktails.

Locust

2305 12th Avenue South

Courtesy Locust

At Locust, chef Trevor Moran created his own culinary genre blending influences from Japan to his native Ireland. I’ve eaten briny Belon oysters that tasted—pleasantly—like licking a battery; a roast sole served with morels and uni; and a bowl of tiny sea snails served with toothpicks and spicy salt. The DIY beef tartare hand rolls—freshly ground bottom round, fluffy rice, and smoky pickled egg cream, plus freeze-dried capers and nori for rolling—are a must, as is the tuna crisp, featuring house-cured slices of tuna loin and belly atop a fried wonton crisp slathered with horseradish. The chefs deliver these dishes to the tables themselves to an eclectic soundtrack featuring everything from Meat Loaf to old-school hip-hop. The drink list is heavy on natural wines, though my go-to is the Toki Highball cocktail or a can of sake. In homage to Moran’s birthplace, there’s also Guinness by the pint, and Irish gin and tonics compete with Orion beer and sake cups.

Kisser

747 Douglas Avenue

Camille Tambunting

The diminutive Kisser, modeled after a Japanese kissaten, a neighborhood spot serving comfort food where locals can eat, drink, and hang out, has been a smash hit since opening in 2023. After spending years in some of the best kitchens in L.A. and Nashville, co-owner couple Brian Lea and Leina Horii decided to focus on their own vision. Inspired in part by Horii’s family sushi restaurant in California, the menu blends traditional Japanese cuisine and their fine-dining techniques with dishes like onigiri stuffed with snow crab and avocado, a perfectly crispy chicken katsu sandwich on fluffy housemade milk bread, and bowls of udon in delicate broth. It’s one of the best places in town to enjoy fresh fish, particularly the chirashi bowl brimming with pristine sashimi and glistening salmon roe. Make sure to end your meal with a green melon cream soda or a crackly miso crème brûlée.

Folk

823 Meridian Street
(615) 610-2595

Courtesy Folk

Folk is a neighborhood gem that Nashville needs now more than ever as hotels and chains threaten to take hold of the dining scene. The restaurant is perched on a quiet corner in East Nashville’s McFerrin Park and welcomes diners with wood-fired sourdough-crust pizzas—my favorite is adorned with littleneck clams, parsley, bonito, and lemon—and rustic Italian-leaning dishes like Marcella beans with spicy pepper relish, bouncy focaccia with whipped ricotta, and more. From the high-ceilinged dining room, adorned with paper lanterns and exposed-brick walls, you can glimpse the bustling kitchen and blazing pizza oven. Natural wines and classic cocktails are the move at the bar, where the afternoon light streams in during the daily apéro hour. The crowd of shockingly healthy houseplants are as well-nourished as the patrons.

Redheaded Stranger

305 Arrington Street
(615) 544-8226

Courtesy Redheaded Stranger

Not far from Folk stands a Tex-Mex taco shop with a sense of humor where you can gobble down one chef’s highly agreeable take on Taco Bell’s Crunchwrap. To make it, chef Bryan Lee Weaver (also of Butcher & Bee) fills his fluffy handmade tortillas with Texas red chili, pork green chili, or both when you order it “Xmas-style.” If tacos are more your speed, you’ll find toppings like brisket, tater tots, and freshly roasted Hatch chiles. To me, the sleeper hit is the vegetarian taco with charred poblano, whipped feta, and crispy rice, thanks to its variety of textures and smoky heat. The queso dip comes with more of those warm, velvety tortillas (never chips!), and the frozen margaritas are tart and free of sickly-sweet sour mix. The interior offers retro-diner vibes, with a Formica bar and red leather booths, but there’s nothing like grabbing a patio seat in the summertime to let those margs work their Texas magic.

Henrietta Red

1200 4th Avenue North
(615) 490-8042

Emily Dorio ( Courtesy Henrietta Red)

Lucky for Nashvillians, former Per Se chef and Nashville native Julia Sullivan came home to open her first solo restaurant in Henrietta Red. This airy, tiled Germantown spot boasts an oyster bar glistening with mollusks, including Maine’s salty Mookie Blues and Alabama’s buttery Murder Points. Despite Nashville’s landlocked status (the Cumberland River doesn’t count), Henrietta Red deftly combines coastal ingredients with Southern flavors in dishes like oysters roasted with ‘nduja butter and whole trout with dandelion greens. Wood-fired seafood is the focus here, but the fresh pastas and vegetables—particularly the beet salad with pistachio and mint—stand out too. Brunch brings its own reason to visit, with dishes like shrimp toast with beets and tahini, and smoked fish cakes with lemon-fennel aïoli.

Prince’s Hot Chicken

5814 Nolensville Pike
(615) 810-9388

Despite the ever-widening swath of restaurants serving Prince’s signature dish around the globe, you have to visit the original purveyor to get real-deal Nashville hot chicken. As the story goes, the ultra-spicy sandwich was created as punishment by a jilted lover of Thornton Prince; but, instead of catching on fire, Prince loved the spice so much that he’d go on to perfect the recipe that launched his business. Today, Prince’s remains the best in class, serving up fried chicken spiced with cayenne pepper oil atop a slice of white bread with a pickle (a foil to all that cayenne). If you’re sticking closer to downtown, swing by Prince’s outpost in the Assembly Food hall to get your hot chicken fix. 

Wendell Smith’s

407 53rd Avenue North
(615) 383-7114

Courtesy Wendell Smith’s

Wood-paneled walls line the dining room of this classic meat ’n’ three restaurant that’s been serving up Southern soul since 1952. Every day, the fourth-generation owners welcome a regular crowd hungry for roast beef, baked ham, and pit barbecue, plus daily specials like fried catfish, chicken and dumplings, and throwback fixins like candied yams, turnip greens, baked apples, fried corn, and creamed potatoes. Wendell Smith’s is a slice of Nashville that has hung on to its corner lot despite unchecked development all around it, still sharing space with the adjacent liquor store of the same name that’s been operating just as long. 

Loveless Cafe

8400 Highway 100
(615) 646-9700 

Courtesy Loveless Cafe

What began 70 years ago as a humble motel and café at the start of the Natchez Trace Parkway has been thriving from the moment Lon and Annie Love began serving biscuits and fried chicken to travelers out of their home kitchen. After their continuing success, the couple turned their home into a restaurant and opened 14 rooms as a motel to accompany their new dining destination. The motel went out of operation years ago, and its rooms are now home to retail space, with smoked hams, jams, and biscuit mix on offer. Though there is now expanded outdoor seating and an outdoor bar serving bloody Marys when the wait is long, there’s nothing better than grabbing a seat in the tiny dining room that still feels like home.  

Sperry’s Restaurant

5109 Harding Pike
(615) 353-0809

Courtesy Sperry’s Restaurant

An institution since 1974, this legendary West Nashville steakhouse is a windowless dining room featuring backlit stained glass, carpeting, a stone fireplace with a roaring fire (most nights), and sporty portraits of the hounds and horses that once lived on the grounds of the abutting Belle Meade Mansion. Martinis and Manhattans pair perfectly with Sperry’s steaks and seafood, from a filet Oscar smothered in crabmeat and béarnaise to king crab legs served with drawn butter. Nashville’s very first salad bar remains gloriously intact here, and diners can pile their plate with neon-green goddess and bacon crumbles (though the plated iceberg wedge salad topped with bacon and black olives is my choice). Bananas Foster, another highlight, is served tableside in a plume of booze-soaked flames and cinnamon-fueled sparks. The doors open at four for happy hour, when sausage-stuffed mushrooms and discounted cocktails draw a steady stream of locals each day.
