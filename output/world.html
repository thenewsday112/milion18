📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

If you told any Silent Hill fan, much less a horror aficionado, that they would get three well-reviewed titles in the franchise in three years, they wouldn’t have believed you. But all those years after that first Silent Hill Transmission, it’s incredible how well the series has bounced back, whether it’s the Silent Hill 2 remake, Silent Hill f or the recently released Silent Hill: Townfall.

Konami hasn’t been shy about future projects, with series producer Motoi Okamoto discussing potential second and third waves. Bloober Team’s Silent Hill 1 remake is doubtless included, but what has the first wave taught about which proposals should move forward? Okamoto told us that, “We’ve been observing a lot of feedback from players. There have been both positive and negative reactions to moving the setting beyond the town of Silent Hill itself, but overall, I feel that players have largely embraced that direction.

“Going forward, I hope players will look forward to seeing where in the world we might go next, and which studios around the world we might collaborate with.”

In terms of gameplay, however, the team is seemingly open to a variety of approaches as long as they maintain the “ebb and flow of tension” that Okamoto believes is “very important” for horror games.

“Silent Hill 2 strikes a good balance between moments of heightened tension and moments where that tension eases, while Silent Hill f gives players relatively more room to breathe. I feel that the gameplay in both titles was well received.

“Silent Hill: Townfall, by comparison, places greater emphasis on sustained tension, and my impression is that this resulted in more divided reactions to the gameplay. It was a valuable lesson in how important it is to carefully balance those moments of tension with opportunities for the player to breathe.” Which makes you wonder if it’s going to return to the approaches seen in Silent Hill 2 and f for the future.

Time will tell, so stay tuned for updates. In the meantime, the first cast members of Silent Hill 1 remake have reportedly been revealed, including Gabrielle Fritz (Wolfenstein 2: The New Colossus, Battlefield 2042) and Jade Matthew (Dragon Nest). Their roles remain unknown, though, so keep your eyes peeled for the official announcement.

Christensen was upstairs when he heard the door open, and when he found the burglar, he was holding his belongings. “I’m like, is this a delivery guy?” Christensen was quoted as saying. “And I look and I’m like, this is all my stuff.”

Remarkably, Christensen then offered to buy the man who broke into his house a train ticket home. No, a Jedi mind trick hadn’t caused the burglar to have a surprise change of heart. He just happened to be a fan who decided on a quick U-turn.

Christensen said that the apologetic burglar was struggling for money and was trying to buy a ticket home. And in an act of kindness that Master Yoda and Obi-Wan Kenobi would have been proud of, Christensen gave the burglar some clothes and shoes to help him in job interviews, then offered to drive him to the train station himself and buy his ticket home the next day.

The burglar failed to show up, however. Perhaps he feared a turn to the Dark side of the Force if he returned to the scene of the crime.

The 45-year-old Canadian first played Anakin Skywalker in 2002’s Star Wars: Episode II – Attack of the Clones. He went on to reprise his role in 2005’s Star Wars: Episode III – Revenge of the Sith and, via vocal cameo, 2019’s saga-ending Star Wars: The Rise of Skywalker. He’s since appeared in two Star Wars TV shows: 2022’s Obi-Wan Kenobi and 2023’s Star Wars: Ahsoka.

At Star Wars Celebration last year, Lucasfilm confirmed that Christensen will return as Anakin Skywalker in Season 2 of Ahsoka. In an interview with IGN, he told us that he felt even more connected to Anakin now "because I've had more time to think about him and sort of try to understand him."

"It's a very unique thing coming back to a role after all of these years and an interesting sort of exercise in the craft of acting, having to sort of account for that passage of time," he continued. "But I feel very fortunate that I have the opportunity to do it."

Summary

  • Chaotic new take on hockey out now on Xbox Series X|S.
  • Brutal fights, ridiculous takedowns and riveting action within a unique narrative.
  • How we imagined a game inspired by childhood hockey playing and storytelling.

What happens when five Finnish developers take the hockey they grew up playing on frozen ponds and turn it into a story about the greatest underground hockey player who ever lived?

When I think about my childhood, some of my best memories aren’t from school, holidays or birthday parties.

They’re from the ice.

Growing up in Finland, I spent enormous amounts of time on frozen outdoor rinks and ponds, playing hockey with friends. You didn’t need to organize it weeks in advance or check everyone’s calendar. You just went to the rink. Games happened. Friendships happened. Some of the people I met through hockey back then are still my friends today.

There was something special about those games. The rules could be flexible, teams could change, and somebody would inevitably try something ridiculous. That feeling became one of the starting points for Puckoff.

When we created Puckoff: Reloaded, we wanted to bring some of that feeling back. Many of the game’s rinks are frozen outdoor ponds inspired by the places where we grew up playing.

Then we asked ourselves a simple question: What if winning didn’t mean scoring more goals?

A Different Kind of Hockey

In most hockey games, you know what you’re supposed to do: Get the puck. Score goals. Win the game. In Puckoff: Reloaded, there are a few more options. You can score. Set up a teammate. Make the perfect pass. Or you can knock someone out.

And nobody in this world seems particularly bothered by that last option.

That’s the world we wanted to create: a story-driven hockey game where fighting isn’t a random side activity, but part of the sport and its strange underground culture. I’ve played a lot of the big hockey games over the years. They’re great at what they do, but I always found myself thinking: “Why can’t I just do that?”

You know the feeling. An opponent does something you don’t like, and you want to give them a little… extra attention. But the game tells you no. That’s when I realized one of the things I love about games. They let us experiment, cause chaos and do things we wouldn’t necessarily do in real life. So, I thought: what if a hockey game actually embraced that? That’s how Puckoff: Reloaded began.

In our version of hockey, scoring goals is still useful. It’s just not the only way to win.

We’re Not Just Making a Hockey Game

I’ve always loved storytelling, and for years I’ve had a particular dream: What would it be like if a big hockey game actually had a real story? Not just a career mode where you play a season and upgrade your player. I mean a proper story. Characters you care about. Mysteries. Rivalries. Friendships. A reason to wonder what happens next. That’s what we wanted Puckoff: Reloaded to be.

Between matches, the game uses a visual-novel-inspired presentation to tell a larger story. The hockey games aren’t disconnected challenges. They’re part of a journey. And that journey is about finding a legend.

The Search for Dominator

At the heart of Puckoff: Reloaded is a hockey legend named Dominator. He’s been playing underground hockey matches for decades, and nobody really knows how he’s managed to keep going for so long. What everyone does know is that he has never lost a game.

For the player character, Dominator isn’t just another hockey player. He’s the reason to keep playing. The dream is simple: find Dominator and eventually play alongside him. Getting there means meeting the people who inhabit this underground hockey world and proving you’re good enough to belong in it. Eventually, that leads to the Cage.

Welcome to the Cage

The biggest fights in Puckoff: Reloaded don’t happen in a traditional hockey arena. They happen underground.

The Cage is where some of the game’s most dangerous opponents are waiting. These boss matches are different from ordinary games: you’re not simply facing another team and trying to score more goals. You’re facing a character with their own identity, abilities and way of playing. And if you’re going to get closer to Dominator, you’ll have to get through them.

Our Dream is Bigger Than This Game

I’m proud of what our five-person team has created, but I also see Puckoff: Reloaded as the beginning of something. My dream has always been to make story-driven hockey games with the scope and production values of the big-budget games. I want hockey games where you remember the characters as much as the matches. Stories that make you care who wins. I want a game where you can spend an evening playing hockey with your friends, laughing when somebody gets knocked out—and then come back the next day because you genuinely want to know what happens to the characters.

We’re not a huge studio. We’re five people in Finland making the game we wanted to play. For me, that’s what Puckoff has always been about. It started with the frozen ponds where some of my best childhood memories were made. Now we’re taking those memories, adding a ridiculous amount of fighting, creating a hockey legend who hasn’t lost in decades, and turning the whole thing into a story.

We just have to find Dominator first.

Yaga Episode 3 Recap and Review

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Warning: This review contains full spoilers for Yaga Season 1, Episode 3. Episodes 1-3 are now streaming on AMC+ in the US and Crave in Canada, with new episodes releasing on Fridays at midnight PT.

Yaga Episode 3 wastes no time diving right back into this eerie, witchy tale, while answering some of our biggest questions after last week’s debut.

We pick up right where Episode 2’s cliffhanger left off – on the beach with Logan’s body – prior to getting confirmation from the police that, yes, it is in fact Logan’s lifeless body (not Henry’s) and that blood was found near a window open in his bedroom. There’s a moment of confusion for the viewer as Rapp sees Logan’s eyes jump open, and we see that they are ice blue – like Henry’s.

By now, we know Rapp has been hallucinating more frequently and this is possibly either from the drugs from the night before (because he honestly looks like he’s still high) or something dark and ominous that’s happening to him.

We see a pattern in relation to “time” in Yaga. This is confirmed when Carson recounts what happened the previous night at Henry’s party where we learn that “a few hours” had passed between seeing Logan disappear into his room upstairs, and Carson finding Rapp on the beach. Originally, it seemed like some time had passed, but nothing substantial.

Time Blips

In Episode 1, there was a time blip between text messages Rapp received seemingly one after another in the bar scene while speaking with Katherine. His text from Carson comes in at 8:11pm, and another text – only seconds later – comes in from Angel at 9:41pm. From what we see as viewers, time has stayed the same in the environment of where he is, but something is clearly controlling time in relation to him. (I noticed this after the publishing of the premiere review, and I blame missing these subtle clues on being only human and not a witch myself.)

As we continue on with more unexplained moments in this story, Rapp tries to put a move on Carson in his hotel room, which she rejects. He then goes to lie down on his bed and falls asleep almost too quickly – Carson comes and kneels down next to the bed and we see the clock turn to 3:33 (not the first time we’ve seen this either), while intense, whispering voices begin to take over as she leans in to almost kiss him. Rapp gently opens his eyes and sees Pam, Henry’s ex-girlfriend, seductively pulling a white feather out of his mouth.

He suddenly jolts out of his actual sleep and awakens some time later in the day. We also notice a small circle tattoo on Carson’s wrist before they head over to forensics, which seems to be placed in the shot intentionally for us to refer back to later…

Going to the mortuary, we see Logan’s corpse – well, most of it. In the episode’s biggest WTF moment, we learn Logan’s penis was severed from his body and he was castrated. It turns out he was also stabbed with an ancient knife made out of Obsidian and human bone – which Katherine later classifies as an Aztec ceremonial midwife sacrificial knife, connected to deities with symbols representing sexual desire and fertility. All signs so far point to Logan being murdered, and Carson and the police on staff are searching for more evidence, especially as this new case potentially relates to Henry.

We see Carson walk up to a small church to meet Logan’s mom, Alice (played by Sarah Strange). The dark, moody shots and heavy music come together to create an emotionally beautiful scene of grief between the two women. They sit on a bench in the cemetery together and more is revealed about Logan that we hadn’t known before. That includes Logan changing his hair and working out constantly – like he was trying to become Henry himself, perhaps.

Alice reveals her hate for the Parks and shares how she heard a rumor that Logan and Henry bought one of Pam’s lame hens from her and joked they were going to sacrifice it. We’re in the Era of the $200 Cup of Coffee

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Sey | Sey

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

These days, Bushwick is perhaps best known for its graffiti art, ecuavolley, and queer bars; the Brooklyn neighborhood is famous for its family-owned taquerias, American-Italian pastry shops, and the hallowed grounds of the Girls episode where Shoshanna accidentally does meth and runs around pantsless at a warehouse rave. Situate yourself at the heart of its largest park, Maria Hernandez, and you’ll see a kaleidoscopic blend of ravers, Latino families, and the baklava guy.

Starting this week, it’s also home to a $200 cup of coffee. 

Bushwick’s Sey Coffee recently announced that it would be touring a rare Panamanian geisha from Finca Sophia, a coffee farm in Panama’s Chiriquí highlands. Sey scooped up the geisha at auction for $44,275, which comes to $6,325 per kilogram, or roughly $200 per cup. The geisha is billed as extraordinarily aromatic, floral, and juicy; Doug Cohen, Sey’s director of brand strategy, advises enjoying it with a clean palate, completely black, and taking your time with it. “As the coffee cools, different aspects of its character become apparent, and that evolution is part of the experience,” he says. 

Even among fourth-wave coffee shops where prices continue to climb higher, Sey’s $200 cup is an outlier. “Coffee as a premium product is relatively new,” says Cohen. “For so long, coffee has been treated as a commodity, and its value has been constrained by that perception.” He says that both before and right after COVID, folks were becoming more curious about coffee, and educating themselves on platforms like Youtube, Discord, and online forums about how to appreciate it and “understand just how high the ceiling of quality can be.” The arrival of the $200 cup of coffee from a cafe in a neighborhood at the epicenter of conversations about both culinary innovation and gentrification feels ripe for closer examination.

So, how did this coffee end up at this price? “We have been purchasing coffee from Finca Sophia for several years,” Cohen says. “This release really grew out of that existing relationship.” When Finca’s general manager told Cohen that the farm’s yields were down by approximately 95 percent, Cohen says a bigger question was raised: “What does it mean to be a good partner when the people you have built relationships with are facing a difficult year?” Buying up the geisha beans felt like a way to both support Sey’s producer, and further develop what Cohen calls “elevated coffee experiences.” 

In the 2010s, the artisanal coffeehouse wave washed over the country, and especially Brooklyn, with hipster gusto. Sey Coffee was an integral part of that boom. Initially called Lofted Coffee, it operated out of its founders’ Bushwick loft until it rebranded to Sey in 2017, and expanded operations to its present Bushwick location on Grattan Street. That space has become something of a local icon since; Sprudge called its aesthetic “shoebox crossed with an airplane hangar”; the minimal-industrial, cream-hued interiors hit a very Axel Vervoordt-with-a-cyber-sigil-tattoo note (positive). The shop’s cachet has drawn coffee lovers, artists, yuppies, and artist-yuppies; customers have snacked on the gluten-free pastries from L’imprimerie (and now, post-L’imprimerie closure, pastries from Smør), and often post up with their dogs thanks to the semi-indoor-outdoor layout. 

Sey is hoping to elevate the coffee shop model to something more premium, with a stated focus on ethics and sustainability. Thus far, it’s earned acclaim for those efforts. Food & Wine named it the best coffee shop in New York and received positive media attention for opting out of tipping, choosing instead to pay its staff up to more than 80 percent above the NYC hourly minimum wage; full-time baristas can make up to $31 per hour in addition to receiving benefits and a share of the cafe’s revenue. 

Increasingly, coffee is getting the same treatment as high-end dining and tasting experiences. There’s the omakase-style menu at NYC’s Driftaway Coffee, or San Francisco’s Komakase coffee tasting room, both of which steer coffee away from quick consumption and toward a world of degustation that appears to have more in common with pricier wine and spirit tastings. The expansion of “espresso house” chain Watch House stateside is also an indicator: The London-based specialty roaster expanded to a fourth location in New York City this summer, raising eyebrows for its $32 cup of coffee while also reportedly scouting for more locations in Austin, Chicago, and Miami. 

The emerging demand may be just much about luxury as it is about craft. I can’t help but wonder if the $200 cup of coffee is happening in Bushwick because the Girls-era Bushwickian has grown up and wants more exclusive experiences. Is this what happens when, as culture writer Raven Smith said on his new BBC podcast last week, the indie outgrows its sleaze? “For us, this is not about pushing prices higher for the sake of it,” Cohen says. “It is about challenging how we assign value to coffee in the first place. What is an extraordinary coffee actually worth?” 

As much as we may lament climbing prices for specialty coffee and espresso, why wouldn’t this happen to the category when so many other aspects of food and beverage — from cocktails to sushi to bread — are following the same trajectory? Higher consumer costs notwithstanding, the marketing of specialty beans does steer U.S. coffee culture away from its productivity-focused consumption and the work-optimized “coffee break.”

“Why do we accept that certain wines can command thousands of dollars, yet question whether coffee can have comparable value?” Cohen asks. It’s a fair question, albeit one that I’d answer with another: Once we acknowledge that coffee should command a higher price, who still gets to drink it? 

Nocturne Episodes 1 & 2 Recap and Review

Spoilers follow for Nocturne Episodes 1 and 2, which are available on Apple TV now.

Apple TV’s new thriller series, Nocturne, aims to draw audiences in with just the right balance of the familiar and the unexpected. The show asks what happens when a boy thought to be the victim of a serial killer returns alive, and follows the criminal mastermind as he maneuvers law enforcement during their race to save any other victims who may still be out there. Over the course of these first two episodes, viewers are left with questions — a mix of plot-driven intrigue, interest in character development, and a touch of side-eye around potential logic gaps or plot conveniences.

Nocturne is based on Lars Kepler’s crime novels (The Sandman and Lazarus), and adapted by Rowan Joffe and John Hlavin for TV. These debut episodes lay the groundwork for convicted killer Jurek Walter’s unfolding scheme to free himself (he is astonishingly well played by Stephen Graham), set against multiple law enforcement officers’ attempts to a) stop him from doing more harm and b) find any of the still-living victims. Jurek was convicted of a series of brutal killings, which devastated a small town. He managed to get a jury to buy into an insanity defense, or we must presume he did, as he’s in a psychiatric hospital rather than a prison. (Side note: how he pulled that off is a nagging question, as nothing in his demeanor suggests an insanity defense would fly… If it’s simply that he’s a sociopath, wouldn’t that apply to all killers? Moving on.)

From a technical perspective, the show does a beautiful job of dropping us in this world. The opening shot of Episode 1, “Lazarus, Come Forth,” establishes a sense of foreboding, danger, and claustrophobia that will become a refrain throughout the series. We get the sense that the creature we are encountering is not unlike a lion in a cage. The camera moves down a cold, concrete hallway toward cells that feel (purposefully) hidden and forgotten — the shot is all the more suffocating because the dark walls of the cell entryways initially obscure the sides of the frame. This is our introduction to Jurek, and it’s notable that the series starts by putting us in the mindset of the killer. He feels trapped and eager to get out. For some yet-to-be-revealed purpose, his gritty, simultaneously menacing and soothing voice whispers to the patient next door, luring the man to seek freedom by any means necessary, even with violence (in this case, against himself). This is Jurek’s central arc across the first two episodes.

He is brilliant and adept at psychological warfare. He also seems to believe he’s entitled to his freedom, or at the very least that there is some legal loophole that will get his conviction overturned if only he can expose the lie that former homicide detective Jonah Lynn (Liev Schreiber) told to seal Jurek’s fate. Jurek taunts Jonah towards the close of the first episode with an offer of “the truth for the truth” when Jonah and Special Agent Quinn (Poorna Jagannathan, lately of Lanterns) visit him. In other words, Jurek will help them find more of his victims, specifically Felicia Frost — the missing sister of Michael, the boy who’s come back from the dead — if Jonah confesses to lying in court. This raises the stakes for everyone, including Agent Quinn, who’s already taken a risk by bringing a retired cop she doesn’t know this close to her case. If his old lie blows it up, that’s on her, with a missing girl’s life on the line.

It’s an interesting choice, and may ultimately be a misdirect. Here’s what appears to have happened: a hospital worker named Karl (Rory Culkin) is assigned to the team that oversees Jurek. Their first encounter, like others in the series, is crafted with impressive tension: Karl searches for a missing weapon beneath Jurek’s metal bed, gets caught by his pant leg, and can’t free himself before Jurek comes to from a sedative — the trapped animal motif plays throughout. Before the scene is over, Jurek has pressed a note into Karl’s pocket, urging him to smuggle it out to his lawyer (more on the questions this raises in a bit).

The note Jurek hands Karl seems to set everything in motion: the strong implication is that, lawyer or no lawyer, it contained Jurek’s instruction to an accomplice to free Michael. Jonah is actively drawn back into the case when Michael turns up alive, as the children’s mother, Ida Frost (Chrissy Metz), and the residents of the town trust him more than anyone else in law enforcement. Also advocating for his participation is FBI agent Saga Bauer (Zazie Beetz), his goddaughter, the child of his deceased partner, and someone he effectively raised. (She eventually goes undercover as a patient in Jurek’s secured ward in order to ascertain who is leaking messages and endangering lives.)

The reality is, Jonah has clearly never given up on finding the remaining missing persons associated with the case: as Agent Bauer discovers via his giant murder board, he’s been methodically searching an enormous grid, single-handedly, for years. The case is deeply personal for both of them. Jonah carries guilt for having quit the force after the (seemingly unrelated?) accidental death of his wife and child. And Jurek (or rather his accomplice) was responsible for kidnapping Bauer’s stepmother, Rebecca. It’s also implied that Bauer’s father committed suicide after his wife’s disappearance. Through Agent Quinn’s vocal skepticism, the show addresses the inappropriateness of having either Jonah or Bauer involved in the investigation, given their personal connections to Jurek. The story excuses the conflict of interest (not to mention that Jonah is no longer an officer of the law) by pointing to their familiarity with the case and the ticking clock on saving Felicia. The dynamics and interwoven tragedies all tied to Jurek stretch credulity, but the production value and strength of the performances are enough to keep the viewer engaged through a good deal of character, plot, and relationship table-setting. At its core, this is a battle of three savants with real stakes: two on the victims’ side, deploying their individual skills to best a mysterious, murderous genius. That brings us to the tightrope the series walks.

When crime dramas lean on preternatural intellect, whether it’s Sherlock Holmes or his foil, Moriarty, they run the risk of reading as a lazy sort of magic trick, where the detective or killer effortlessly “knows things” they’d need to in order to keep the story moving. Indeed, Jonah’s near-instant recognition of where Michael was being held, and Jurek’s Derren Brown-level ability to read people (pegging Karl’s guitar playing and Jonah’s partner’s violent alcoholism), open the door to the kind of conveniences that could lose a modern audience as the series unfolds. It already causes one to wonder: if Jurek is in possession of that kind of mental acuity, why did he get caught while his partner went free? Fortunately, Graham, who stunned the world with Adolescence last year, is deeply compelling, allowing us to give ourselves over to his performance.

That isn’t to undercut the potent talent of the rest of the cast, or the visual storytelling. The show is beautifully shot, with well-crafted production design. It immerses us in a rustic exterior world that vacillates between gray and worn, and comfortingly familiar. The forest landscapes are striking, but dense and dangerous in a way that mirrors the cold, stifling confines of the hospital Jurek is housed in.

Schreiber is delivering a grounded, understated portrayal of a grieving man who is just a bit too tired to fully fight against terrible ideas (Bauer’s undercover operation) and far too invested to stop himself from digging, with or without permission. One moment that stands out is when Agent Quinn tells Jonah she’s sorry for the loss of his wife and child; he nods, and unbearable pain flashes in his eyes for an instant before he moves on. Beetz is convincing and deeply affecting as a chameleon-like agent willing to harm herself (physically or otherwise) to close a case. It’s clear that this particular operation may drive her to a breaking point for multiple reasons: Jurek’s uncanny ability to read others and play on their weaknesses, her own stepmother’s disappearance (which he is connected to), and, not least of all, that it taps into the trauma of a woman who was the child of a mentally ill mother and addict father. That fear comes through as she prepares to enter the ward, right after visiting her mother. Beetz gives the sense that Bauer is unsure whether she’s crossing a line she won’t be able to come back from, where fact and fiction are too close. Bill Camp is also endearing as Chief Ellison, particularly as he talks about what the Frost kids meant to the town; he looks vulnerable, like a man who knows he is out over his skis, but wishes he wasn’t.

It took a moment for me to recognize Culkin as the compassionate nurse, Karl. Physically, his hair is doing a great deal to support the transformation, but this is also a side of the actor less seen on screen. There was one moment, when he first sees Jurek on the security cameras, in which I was left to wonder if he had some nefarious intent. But for the most part, he reads as a man of incredible kindness and skill with patients whom most of society writes off and exiles. That said, it’s odd that an employee who demonstrates an extraordinary amount of empathy is tapped for a job that puts him right in the crosshairs of a person known to exploit those instincts, and in a dangerous environment that requires a certain degree of toughness. Additionally, Karl, who is clearly driven by financial need (he accepts the new job the moment he’s informed that it comes with a raise and tells Jurek he can’t afford to lose it), inexplicably risks his career for a dangerous criminal he just met.

So, that leaves us with a couple of options: this is a piece of plotting that doesn’t logically track, or Karl isn’t who we think he is. If it’s the latter, perhaps Karl was “sent” into the hospital with purpose. It’s strangely convenient that Jurek’s day nurse went missing (and is likely dead), making room for Karl to take over. Theoretically, the patient who injures himself in the opening sequence could have gotten word to Jurek’s partner to get rid of the day nurse and make room for Karl. But how could Jurek have known that the exact right personality type for his purposes was working a few floors above him, let alone that he’d get the job? Was Karl a plant, then, and Jurek’s accomplice? That doesn’t square with their first encounter, where, with no witnesses, Jurek treats Karl as a stranger and insists he is still a human deserving care. And if someone else on staff was steering Karl, Jurek already has a partner on the inside, so why would he need Karl? Those are just a few of the questions that may or may not pay off at the close of the series. There are smaller dangling threads as well. For example, Jonah finds footprints both in the building where Michael was held and outside the missing day nurse’s home, so why in the world would he hold them back from the FBI? Ostensibly, he’d do anything to find Felicia, so that type of behavior simply doesn’t track. Ironically, the serial killer is doing a better job of asking for help and relying on a partner.

That said, the series works in no small part because Graham is simply impossible to look away from. He’s playing a killer who appears to have a (forgive the Dexter reference) code that makes sense to him. He says he “wants people to pay for their crimes.” Yes, that was in reference to Jonah’s “lie,” but the way he looks at the nurse who is rude to Karl reminds me of Hannibal Lecter’s deep disdain for bad manners. Graham’s depiction of Jurek is utterly magnetic and mercurial; when Jonah and Quinn visit, he breaks into a fit of giggles at the idea that she’s a “special” agent, as if the word itself is a joke, and moments later he’s commanding the room, telling them with understated menace that they’re only there because he brought them there. He really does seem to own them. The minute he’s off the screen, I crave more time with him.

This entirely serves the series, because it allows us to believe that others could fall under his spell, even Bauer, a smart, capable, stony FBI agent with oceans to lose. There is a deep irony that he’s maneuvering and clawing his way to freedom, given his brutal kill method of burying victims alive. He complains that he’s been in solitary, but his partner has kept a child in the dark so long that the boy went temporarily blind. There’s also a very small possibility that he is, in some way, innocent, and that he has been “made worse” by his circumstances. Either way, for those on both sides of the law, this is a story about freedom, loss, redemption, and revenge, and the first two episodes have given me enough to stick with it for now.

Abstract Premise Clonality is widespread among plants, yet the processes governing genetic diversity within the most ancient clones remains understudied. Methods To quantify the clone's spatial extent and map its underlying genetic structure and gain insights into its origin, we used unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry and two complementary genetic data sets including nuclear simple sequence repeats (nSSRs) and multiplexed intersimple sequence repeat genotyping by sequencing (MIG‐seq), a reduced representation SNP discovery method. Results The putative clonal stand at Mt Read has been severely impacted by fire, with living stems occupying approximately 5260 m2, a ~65% reduction in the stand's previously estimated size. Of all the pastries in France, one of my favorites is the financier. Enriched with nuts, and moistened with butter, every bakery in France offers their own version of them. They come in different sizes, shapes, and even flavors; almond is the most popular, but you’ll sometimes come across financiers made with ground hazelnuts or pistachios. I like them all. There are a few theories how this mini-gâteau got its curious name.

One is that, traditionally, they’re baked in small, rectangular molds. Once baked and unmolded, the little cakes resemble bars of gold, which appeals to people in the financial world. Another is that people in France are known to indulge in an afternoon sweet stop at their local bakery, called a goûter. Because people who work in the financial industry normally wear nice outfits or suits, something that’s neat to eat is appreciated, so they can stay presentable when heading back to the office.

I don’t have to worry about that, nor has anything come out of my oven ever turned to gold. (Quelle dommage!) But when I found myself with some leftover brown butter from infusing it in bourbon for Brown Butter Old Fashioneds, since I treat butter like gold, I repurposed it as a base for a batch of financiers.

Using browned butter keeps the butter flavor even more in focus. Some people get a little anxious when they see dark specks in pastries and desserts; I once had a waiter ask me what the dark flecks in the vanilla ice cream were. When I told him they were vanilla beans, and asked him (incredulously) what he thought they were, he replied, “I thought they were dirt.”

So after you brown the butter, when pouring it out of the pan, you don’t want to scrape up too much of the dark bits at the bottom of the pan, but I don’t mind a few in my cakes (in fact, I prefer them). They’re easy to make, keep well, and are the perfect afternoon snack – or as the French say, un snack.

From the 1983 Archives: The North Express – C. Hamilton-Ellis Recalls a Forgotten Railway

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In this archive article, C. Hamilton-Ellis recalls the Midland & South Western Junction Railway and its North Express service. Drawing on his own memories of the railway, he describes its history, locomotives and the journey through the Wiltshire countryside, accompanied by his railway painting.

The North Express and its origins

SUCH a title as the North Express might mean to us, nowadays, anything from a train on its way from Inverness to Wick, to another heading for the Baltic. But this North Express was to be seen at the head of Southampton Water, and on Salisbury Plain, and threading the Cotswolds. So to its origins.

There were several thorns in the side of the Great Western Railway, distinct from the London & South Western, which became rather a sort of sciatica. A true thorn was the Somerset & Dorset, which the dastardly Midland Railway and the unspeakable South Western had jointly snatched in the 1870s. Another was the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. In spite of its name, this was sturdily independent. It linked the Midlands with the Deep South, with the benevolent support of Paddington’s two old enemies, adding insult to injury by getting running powers over stretches of the Great Western.

The Junction Railway’s beginnings

Beginnings of the “Junction”, which so improbably extended from Andover to Andoversford, about sixty miles northward, had been modest and penurious. The Swindon, Marlborough & Andover Railway reached Marlborough in 1881, to push on to Grafton and ultimately to Red Posts Junction, a little west of Andover on the South Western main line. The Swindon & Cheltenham Extension Railway reached Cirencester from Swindon (GW station) in 1883. Desperate, with no apparent friends, the two companies amalgamated next year as the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. Through traffic, crossing over the Great Western at Rushey Platt, began in 1891.

A Receiver was in possession, but Midland, and LSWR, now had their eyes on this second potential dig at the GWR. The South Western released its promising Chief Storekeeper, (Sir) Sam Fay, to be the new General Manager of the MSWJR in 1892. Himself Southampton-born, he moved-in-fast and fairly licked the staggering outfit into shape and solvency. He got running powers, willingly granted, over the South Western from Andover Junction to Southampton Terminus. There were General Offices in an agreeable Victorian villa overlooking Swindon Town Station, run by Old Swindon. In 1899, with mission accomplished, Fay went back to Waterloo as Superintendent of the Line, before going to the toiling Great Central as General Manager in 1902.

The North Express service

For the first time, with the northern extremity completed, the “Junction” prospered. Under Fay, in 1893, came the forerunner of the North Express, with through carriages from Southampton to Sheffield, and a corresponding South Express. The company had then one express engine, a modest 4-4-0 by Dübs & Company. By 1914 at latest, starting early in the afternoon, it was quicker to ride from Waterloo to Cheltenham than from Paddington, thanks to a smart connection at Andover Junction. One recalls arriving at the latter, from London, and seeing the red North Express waiting on the left-hand curve from Andover Town, having left Southampton at 1.45 p.m. It went on to call at Swindon Town, then without a stop to Cheltenham, 35¼ miles, over the Cotswolds, in 54 minutes.

Memories of the railway

That is the train I have painted, with fond memories from long ago. My picture (reproduced overleaf) shows it, in 1915, coming down through Burderop Woods, above the head of Coate Water just south of Swindon, in the heart of Richard Jefferies’ beloved country, the scene of his best books. Almost continuously the line traversed country of singular beauty: along the eastern flanks of Salisbury Plain, through the Marlborough Downs and so by Chiseldon down to Swindon Town. Then came Cirencester, a delightful town to this day save that now its nearest railway station has to be Kemble Junction on the old Great Western line north to Gloucester.

At Cirencester, too, the company had its locomotive shops, and the handsome through station called Watermoor. (The Great Western had but a picturesque terminus, haunt of fussy rail-motors, on the other side of the town). Alas, poor Watermoor! Last time I saw it, twenty years ago, the buildings were deserted; vandalised and mucked-up by young yobos, such as occur even in such a nice borough as “Ciren”.

The locomotives

Going over the Cotswolds entailed marked contrasts, from the windswept plateau by Foss Cross to the delightful passage of Chedworth, and north of its double-tracked tunnel, a lovely descent to join the reluctant Great Western line out to Kingham. So, by running powers, the trains reached the welcoming arms of the Midland Railway.

Midland & South Western Junction trains scarcely resembled those of its mighty allies, save in the beautiful Midland crimson for engines and carriages. The engine tenders proudly bore the company’s initials in gold, in an extremely florid sort of italic script, all curved and scrolled, possibly inspired by that on the splashers of London & South Western express engines during the Adams period. The locomotive in my picture was one of a pair built in 1914, the last of James Tyrrell’s standard express engines. These two, Nos. 4 and 31, had each two domes, that on the second ring containing top-feed apparatus and being surmounted by a pair of Ross safety-valves, highly polished. This made the engines very exotic in the eyes of the small boy who once was I. There was virtue in the carriages, too. All too rarely for that time, all had electric lighting, even the horseboxes. The lavatory tanks were partly raised above the camber of the roofs, rather suggesting Holland.

The end of the railway

All gone, and for a long time now! Under the Railways Act 1921 this railway passed into the Great Western net, though not without a rearguard action lasting into the summer of 1923, the Government’s deadline. The Great Western gradually ran it down to rather a shadow of its old self. Yet it still served faithfully through the second world war, as well as the first conflict. But, as in other parts of the West Country, Paddington was to win in the end.

C. Hamilton-Ellis

An authentic wheel of Grana Padano carries a signature brand burned into its rind—so you know that if you see a diamond pattern and the cheese’s name, it must have been produced in the Valle Padana (Po River Valley) of Northern Italy according to strict rules with roots in a 12th-century Cistercian monastery. At the time, the region surrounding the city of Milan was primarily soggy marshland. The monks who built the monastery—Chiaravalle Abbey—were followers of the Rule of Saint Benedict, which, along with devotion to prayer, mandated that they practice agriculture with a focus on responsible, sustainable use of land. They first figured out how to turn the marshy landscape into arable fields so they could grow high-quality feed for dairy cattle. Since milk (and fresh cheese) didn’t last long, the next challenge for the monks was how to preserve this nutritious, valuable food source to nourish themselves and their community.

The method they developed—heating the milk in copper cauldrons, adding rennet (a coagulating enzyme), cutting the curds into tiny pieces, and molding, salting, and aging the cheese—remains largely unchanged today. By producing the first iteration of what is now known around the world as Grana Padano, the monks of Chiaravalle Abbey earned the distinction of operating the world’s first cheese dairy.

Easy to like, portable, and a good source of protein, the cheese became an emblematic food, enjoyed by Italians at all levels of society through the Renaissance and into the modern era. Its iconic name, meanwhile, developed over time. The monks called it “caseus vetus”—Latin for “old cheese”—but since the peasants didn’t know Latin, they referred to it as “grana” for its distinctive grainy texture. As production spread well beyond the monastery, each province had its own grana: Cheese made in Milan was “grana Milanese,” and “grana Mantovano” was from Mantua.

It wasn’t until 1954 that the Italian government codified the name Grana Padano to unite the different varieties of grana cheese under a single wider geographical indication and that the Grana Padano Protection Consortium was established. In 1996, Grana Padano was awarded the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) designation. Today the consortium incorporates 134 member dairies, who together produced over 6 million wheels of cheese in 2025. In addition to safeguarding the name, the organization is also responsible for the global promotion of Grana Padano PDO and governs every detail of production to ensure that each wheel meets the strict standards established under the PDO. 

The specifications start with the cows’ diet: All fresh forage and at least 75 percent of dry fodder (such as hay) must be grown within the cheese production area. Raw milk must be collected within 24 hours of milking and be partially skimmed, allowing for natural separation of the cream. After a natural whey starter is introduced to the copper (or copper-lined) milk vat, calves’ rennet is added for coagulation. The cheese is then broken into rice-size curds using a spino, which resembles a large whisk, and packed into round molds, where it sits for at least 36 hours. The wheels are immersed in brine for anywhere from 14 days to a month and aged for at least nine months and up to 24 months or longer. Wheels aged over 20 months can be labeled Riserva.

Bite into a chunk of Grana Padano PDO, and you taste the sweetness of fresh milk coupled with a savory hint of toasted nuts. Riservas may be flakier and more crystalline in texture than younger versions of the cheese but never dry or crumbly, and they must still have a delicate flavor. Grana Padano PDO is an exceptionally versatile cheese, equally delicious on a cheese board, wrapped with a slice of Prosciutto di Parma PDO and drizzled with aged balsamic for a sophisticated appetizer, as a filling for ravioli, or grated over soup or pasta.

It’s good for you, too. Lactose- and gluten-free, Grana Padano PDO is low in fat and an excellent source of protein, calcium, and vitamin B12. A 30-gram piece (just over one ounce) has as much nutrition as a pint of milk, making it a beneficial as well as tasty solution to the “afternoon slump” for both kids and adults. Whoever you share Grana Padano PDO with, be sure to tell them what they have in common with Italians from more than 1,000 years ago—that they’re savoring a taste of European culinary history.

Laab (a.k.a larb) is one of the most iconic dishes of Thai cuisine, beloved by both Thais and people around the world. It's such a classic that laab was the second recipe I ever posted on Hot Thai Kitchen way back in 2009! It's a warm salad of ground meat, dressed with a bright, spicy lime dressing and loaded with fresh herbs like mint and cilantro. You can enjoy it as a meal with sticky rice and fresh veggies, or serve it alongside other Thai dishes. It is also one of the easiest Thai dishes you can make at home, perfect for any Thai food beginners!

Cultural Context: What is laab?

Let's get the most important thing out of the way first: while many Thai restaurants write this dish on the menu as larb, the correct pronunciation and the way it should be written is laab. The R is not pronounced. (If you speak British or Australian English, this does not concern you 😉.)

Laab ลาบ is a salad that is local to Laos and the northeast region of Thailand known as Isaan. But today, it is popular in all regions of Thailand. It is typically made of ground protein (can be pork, beef, chicken, or even fish) and lots of fresh herbs such as mint, cilantro, and green onions. It is a sour, salty, and spicy dish as the dressing is made of lime juice, fish sauce, and dried chili flakes. The most important ingredient in laab is toasted rice powder or kao kua, which gives laab its unique and identifying toasty aroma.

Because laab is mostly meat, it is served with a lot of raw vegetables such as cabbage, lettuce leaves, cucumber, and long beans. The starch it is traditionally served with is Thai sticky rice, though in a pinch, jasmine rice also works.

This recipe is for laab moo, which means laab with pork, and it is the most popular type of meat used for laab in Thailand. However, you can substitute another kind of ground meat, more details in the ingredients section below.

Ingredients and Important Notes

Here are all the ingredients for laab; please read important notes about them before you start. For amounts, check out the full recipe card below.

  • White jasmine rice or glutinous rice, uncooked. This is for making the most important ingredient in this whole thing: toasted rice powder or kao kua. Glutinous rice (sticky rice) is more commonly used since it is the staple rice in the Isaan region, but I prefer using jasmine rice because it is more fragrant. Choose either.
  • Makrut lime leaf (optional) for adding extra aromat to your toasted rice powder.
  • Ground pork. Lean ground pork is fine as long as it is not TOO lean. A little fat is ideal for keeping things moist, especially since there is no added fat in the dressing. You can sub ground chicken or turkey (use dark meat for best results), or ground beef. Here's my chicken laab (laab gai) recipe. For a vegan version, see my corn and tofu laab recipe here
  • Shallots, red onion will also work if shallots are not available. Red onions tend to be a little sweeter, so you may prefer that if you find raw shallots a little too pungent.
  • Fish sauce. See my post here about how to choose good fish sauce.
  • Lime juice. For best flavour use fresh lime juice for this recipe, do not use bottled lime juice.
  • Chili flakes or chili powder. Laab is supposed to be spicy so I recommend adding as much as you can! In Thailand we use roasted chili flakes which are a bit smokey, but whatever you have will work. If you don't eat spicy, do not omit the chilies or your laab will not taste right, as you will lose the chili flavour. Choose a mild chili powder, such as kashmiri chili powder, or the generic "red pepper flakes." If using red pepper flakes I recommend grinding it further in a spice grinder as the flakes are quite large and don't integrate well into dishes. If you'll be cooking Thai food regularly, I recommend making your own mild chili flakes, see "How to Make Mild Chili Flakes" below.
  • MSG or chicken bouillon powder (optional). Look, I never used to put MSG in my laab, and for years I felt like there was something different between the laab I made and the ones I get in Thailand. Turns out, it was the MSG. It not only adds umami but in this dish it also helps balance out the sharp acidity of the lime, creating a more well-rounded flavour. If you don't have MSG, you can add chicken bouillon powder (which contains MSG). If you don't want to do either, sugar can be a compromise - add a pinch of sugar to balance the acid, but do not add so much that the sweetness is detectable.
  • Mandatory herbs: Cilantro, green onions, and mint. It's not a complete laab without these. If you're a cilantro hater, I will make an exception for you to leave it out.
  • Optional herbs: makrut lime leaves, sawtooth coriander, lemongrass, dill. Add these if you like them, but no need to go out of your way to find them.
  • Sticky rice for serving. At the store, the rice is labelled as "glutinous rice" or "sweet rice." Make sure it is long grain and from Thailand. Here's a post with 7 different ways to make sticky rice, and my favourite "quick" method is this hot soak method. 
  • Fresh raw vegetables for serving. Laab is a strongly flavoured dish, and eating it alongside some fresh crunchy vegetables makes it more delicious. Some good options are green cabbage (preferably Asian style), cucumber, radishes, long beans, and romaine lettuce.

Step-By-Step

Here are all the steps to make this recipe. If this is your first time, I highly recommend watching the video tutorial to ensure success!

  1. Add about a third of the fish sauce to the ground pork and mash it to mix, and also to get rid of the "tube" shapes that ground meat comes in so your laab doesn't end up with weird meat tubes.
  1. Make the toasted rice powder. Place the rice and a makrut lime leaf (if using) in a dry pot or skillet and stir constantly over medium high heat until the rice is deeply browned. It will get a bit smokey so make sure you have good ventilation.
  1. If adding a makrut lime leaf, once it is dry and crisp, quickly remove it from the pan to prevent it from burning. This will likely happen before the rice is fully toasted. (I grab it quickly with my fingers, but to be safe, you should probably use tongs or chopsticks.)
  1. Transfer the rice and the lime leaf to a coffee grinder or mortar and pestle and pound into a powder. The crispy leaf will grind along with the rice, adding a citrusy aroma to the rice powder.
  1. This is the texture you're looking for - not as fine as flour, but like dry sand with no chunky pieces. If you're left with the center stem of the lime leaf at the end, discard it.
  1. Add 2 tablespoon of water to a pot and bring to a simmer over high heat. Add the ground pork and cook, breaking it up as you go, just until the pork is done. Turn off the heat.

  1. Add the seasonings: remaining fish sauce, lime juice, chili flakes, MSG (if using), and toasted rice powder. Stir to mix.

How to Make Mild Chili Flakes for Mild Laab

While you might be tempted to skip the chilies to make a mild laab, trust me when I say that your laab will not taste as good, because in addition to heat, chilies provide a key flavour that's important to the flavour profile of laab. While you can look for mild chili powder, you can also make your own which allows you to customize the heat level to your tolerance. If you want to cook Thai food regularly, I promise it'll come in handy! It keeps in the freezer indefinitely and in the fridge for a few months.

  1. Buy whole dried chilies whose spice level suits your tolerance. Latin American stores are best for this as they have lots of variety. Guajillo and California peppers have almost no heat. Puya peppers have a little kick but are still mild. You can also combine chilies, for example get guajillo/California and then spike it with a few chile de arbol (which are very hot) for a custom heat level.
    *As a guideline, for this laab recipe, you want to be able to add at least 1 teaspoon of chili flakes. If adding 1 teaspoon makes the dish too spicy, make it milder.
  2. Cut the dried chilies into chunks with scissors, and discard the seeds that fall out but no need to remove any seeds still inside. Toast them briefly over low heat in a dry skillet, stirring constantly, until they are crispy and smell slightly smokey.
  3. Remove from heat and grind into fine flakes using a coffee/spice grinder. Keep in the fridge or freezer as dried chilies can get mold overtime.

How to Eat Laab Like a Thai

A plate of laab arrives with a side of sticky rice and a bunch of raw vegetables...and if you wonder "how do I tackle this?" You're not alone! Let me explain.

Typically laab is served family style, meaning you're sharing the laab with a few people, you are not served a plate of laab that is your own to finish. You start by putting some of the sticky rice on your personal plate. Then scoop some of the laab and grab a few pieces of veg onto your own plate as well. Then you can:

  • Take a bite of laab (with a spoon) and follow it up with a bite of sticky rice (the sticky rice is eaten by hand), so both the rice AND the laab are in your mouth at the same time. You can do the same with laab + veg combo.
  • You can also use the vegetable as a "spoon" and put the laab on it, kind of like a lettuce wrap. This works well with cabbage, lettuce and cucumber slices.
  • If there is any dressing on the plate you can also use sticky rice to sop it up!

This is just typically how Thai people eat laab; don't feel like these are rules. You can eat it however you want!

Instructions

  1. Toast the rice. In a medium size pot, add the raw rice and the optional makrut lime leaf and put it over high or medium high heat. Stir it constantly until a the rice is toasted until deeply browned. If using the lime leaf, pick it out of the pan as soon as it becomes dry and crispy, which will likely happen before your rice is toasted. Remove the rice from the pot immediately to prevent it from over-toasting from the residual heat and let cool slightly.   2. Grind the toasted rice. Transfer the rice and the lime leaf into either a mortar and pestle or a coffee grinder. There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma. 

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO. 

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course. 

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

Top Five Mortar Mixes for Cordwood Construction by Richard Flatau

It is very important to understand that cordwood as a building technique is continuing to evolve. Changes and improvements have been significant over the last 50 years. Here are a few points to consider:

 

Cob mortar with cordwood (aka Cobwood).

  1. 95% of the cordwood built nowadays is non-load bearing. Therefore the mortar matrix is not holding up the roof (then it is called load-bearing). The post framework holds up the roof and code officials love it. The mortar can be weaker (in this case, weak is a good thing, because you can add more organic matter like, sawdust, hemp hurds, clay, vermiculite and the mortar beads can be thinner (3 inches on the inside and the outside). This leads to better R-value and a better set and cure of the mortar.
  2. Do not rely on OLD books (use at least a 2020 copyright date), articles and videos. Some of them are just plain wrong or misleading. Investigate using recently published materials and videos.
  3. Experiment with your mix on a test wall or a small shed. This will help you fine-tune up your mortar.
  4. There are at least 5 basic mortar mixes with dozens of variations. Choose the one that meets your personal preferences.
  5. You do NOT need to use Portland cement in your mix.  There are several different mixes that have no Portland and some that have less than 10% Portland for the total volume.;

The term cob is used to describe a monolithic building system based on a mixture of clay, sand and straw. The construction uses no forms, bricks or wooden framework; it is built from the ground up. Shortbread Rounds are one of the most underrated biscuits in the world. That may seem like a fairly sweeping statement, but hear me out.

Shortbread Rounds are popular around the world. They are bought by hundreds of thousands of people, especially at Christmas, served with coffee in some of the most high-end establishments and made in kitchens for hundreds of years.

Despite this, if you ask people, “What’s your favourite biscuit?” very few of those same people will say a Shortbread Round!

Despite them having a taste to die for and a tradition and class that many biscuits can’t even get close to, everyone goes for the foil-covered ones, or the iced ones or even, god forbid, the branded chocolaty ones!

We’re here to put this straight. This recipe will help you make wonderfully buttery but simple Shortbread in perfect rounds and even learn how to make them into wee stars, which is easier said than done.

When we’re finished, your shortbread rounds will be asked for by family and friends at Christmas, requested as birthday gifts and declared the best of biscuits!

Ok, maybe we’re going a little far, but you’ll like them; they’re delicious!

What are Shortbread Rounds?

Shortbread has a classic flavour but comes in a few different traditional shapes: Shortbread Fingers, Petticoat Tails and Shortbread Rounds.

Shortbread Rounds are precisely that: rounds of Shortbread. They are more of a biscuit or cookie shape and lend themselves well to being served with a cup of tea or coffee. Often lightly dusted with sugar, they have a nice crunch to them without being too thick.

Why are they called Shortbread Rounds?

The round part of this is simple, the round is its shape. But Shortbread gained its name over the years.

Originally, Shortbread wasn’t called Shortbread at all. It was known as “Biscuit bread” because it was made from any dough left over after baking bread. These would then be dried out in the oven and served.

Over the years, butter was added to replace the yeast, giving the bread a more crumbly, buttery texture. This texture was called short, as in shortcrust pastry, for example. Add Short to Biscuit bread, and Shortbread was born.

Where do Shortbread Rounds come from?

Biscuit bread was made all over the UK and the world in various forms, but Shortbread was made popular, supposedly, by Mary Queen of Scots.

She is said to have been very fond of it back in the 16th century, though her preference was for Petticoat Shortbread rather than the rounds we’re making today.

Why make your own Shortbread Rounds?

This is both a good question and one we get asked about many of the foods we have written recipes for. Shortbread is, after all, just three ingredients and is readily available in shops worldwide. So why bother?

The reason is this. There is something about something made with love in the home that adds a little extra taste. The fun you can have baking these with little ones or the pride you can get by making these traditional biscuits can’t be bought in a tartan tin or a plastic packet.

That said, if they go horribly wrong, you can nip out and buy some, and no one will ever know!

  • 60g (2oz) Corn Flour
  • 225g (8oz) Butter (we prefer salted for shortbread)
  • 113g Caster Sugar (4oz)

Why Corn Flour?

Shortbread is traditionally made in a 3:2:1 ratio of flour to butter to sugar. However, it can also spread easily in the oven and not hold its shape.

For this reason, recipes using shortbread shapes often adjust the flour or sugar amounts and add different types of either or both. We like to stick closely to the traditional method but exchange a little of the plain flour for corn flour to help the biscuits hold their shape.

How to make Shortbread Rounds – Step-by-step method

Preheat the oven to 180C/356F

Mix the softened butter and sugar together with a hand mixer. Don’t cream it as you would for a cake, mix until just combined. 

Add the flour and corn flour.

Stir together using a wooden spoon, and then use your hands until it forms a dough. 

Place the dough in the fridge for half an hour for it to firm up. 

Roll the dough out until it is approx 5mm thick. 

Use a cutter to cut your preferred shape. Rounds are a classic shape but stars for Christmas work nicely!

Place the shortbread on a lightly greased tray or one lined with baking paper. The shortbread does expand a little, so you will need more than one tray, and you want to space them out so they won’t touch as they bake. 

If the rolling and cutting took a while, refrigerate them for another 30 minutes to help them keep their shape, or you can bake them immediately. 

Bake for 15-20 minutes, until they start to colour very slightly on the edges. 

Once they’re out of the oven, sprinkle with granulated sugar.

Cool on the tray. They will still be soft and will harden up as they cool.

Enjoy!

Yield: 35 Biscuits

Chocolate Chip Rock cakes are many things: they are simple and delicious, they are a cousin of the humble Rock Cake, and this cousin has been made famous several times over the years.

One thing they are not is complicated, despite their scrumptiousness, and the length of this recipe will reflect this!

What are Chocolate Chip Rock Cakes?

The humble Chocolate Chip Rock Cake is a middleman. The perfect compromise between the traditional Rock Cake and more decadent Chocolate Rock Cakes.

They have all the taste and tradition of a plain rock cake, just hinting at a sweeter chocolate version with chips of chocolatey goodness.

What are Rock Cakes?

The traditional rock cake is a classic. It’s a simple cake made from a minimum of ingredients—such a limited amount of ingredients, in fact, that the British government promoted Rock Cakes as a great option during the rationing of World War 2.

These simple little cakes generally consist of flour, sugar, butter, egg, milk, and mixed dried fruit.

If eaten warm, straight from the oven, it’s hard to find a better-tasting treat. They’re so popular that they have appeared in modern culture since the war. From Agatha Christie and Humphrey Bogart to Hagrid and Harry Potter, these wee cakes go beyond famous, making them a classic.

Where do Chocolate Chip Rock Cakes come from?

Rock cakes originated in Great Britain, at least since Victorian times (1837-1901, the length of Queen Victoria’s reign), but potentially before.

Like many of the classic British recipes we’ve made here on Scottish Scran, rock cakes have changed over the years. Like recipes such as Eves Pudding, Crumble, Bread and Butter Pudding and Cranachan, the Rock Cake recipe has been altered and adapted from family to family. An “original” recipe is hard to find. However, the early rock cakes would have contained a lot more oats.

Adding chocolate chips to pep this plain cake up a little is not a great leap. Chocolate has been available since the 1600s, but mainly as a drink. Chocolate chips, however, first appeared in the UK included in ‘cookies’ as late as 1956.

So, as much as we can’t tell you exactly when some genius added chocolate chips to Rock Cakes, we’re delighted they did!

Things you’ll need to make Chocolate Chip Rock Cakes

  • Baking tray
  • Baking paper
  • Mixing bowl
  • Wooden mixing spoon
  • Grater (optional)
  • Tablespoon / two forks

Ingredients for Chocolate Chip Rock Cakes

This recipe will make 9 large or 12 small rock cakes. If you need more, increase the ingredients proportionally.

  • 250g (1 cup) Self Raising Flour
  • 100g (3.5oz) Butter
  • 2 tsp Baking Powder
  • 50g (1/4 Cup) Granulated Sugar
  • 1 Large Egg
  • 1-2 tbsp Milk
  • 150g chocolate chips (3/4 cup)

How to make Chocolate Chip Rock Cakes – Step-by-Step Method

Preheat the oven to 190C/160C Fan or 370F/320F Fan.

Grease a baking tray with butter or use baking paper.

Sift the flour and baking powder into a bowl.

Cut the butter into cubes and rub it into the flour until it resembles breadcrumbs. This is easiest if the butter is cold but not too hard.

If it’s too cold/hard, you can grate the butter and then rub it into the flour.

Like scones, you should handle as little as possible so the heat from your hands doesn’t melt the butter.

Stir in the chocolate chips and sugar.

Beat together the egg and 1 tbsp of milk and add to the mixture. If it’s too dry, you can add the other tbsp of milk. It should be a stiff dough.

To put on the baking tray, either drop a heaped tablespoon of the mixture from a spoon or use two forks to shake it into a rough ball and put it on the tray.

Bake 12-15 minutes until they start to turn pale golden brown at the rough edges.

Remove from the oven and transfer to a wire rack when they’re cool enough to touch. They are best eaten while still a little warm or within the next day or so.

Enjoy!

Yield: 12
