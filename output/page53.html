Pastina Soup (Italian Penicillin)

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

4.9 from 13 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 45 minutes
  • Yield: 6–8 servings 1x
Print Pin

Description

This soup has become my go-to recipe when my family is sick. The broth is amazing – simple, light, flavorful, and nourishing! It’s absolute comfort food. 

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Broth:

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 large carrots, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 2 stalks of celery, cut into large chunks
  • half of 1 yellow onion, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 4 cloves garlic, peeled and smashed
  • 10 cups chicken or vegetable broth (I use 10 cups water plus 2 tablespoons Better Than Bouillon chicken base)
  • 1 teaspoon salt

For Serving: 

  • 1 1/2 cups pastina (I use Stelline from the Good and Gather brand at Target)
  • freshly grated Parmesan cheese for topping
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Sauté Veggies: Heat oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the chunky carrots, celery, onion, and garlic to the pot. Sauté for 5 minutes. 
  2. Simmer: Add broth and salt. Simmer for 30 minutes, until the vegetables are very tender.
  3. Remove Veggies: Use a slotted spoon to transfer the vegetable chunks to a bowl. Set aside to cool for a few minutes (you don’t want to add scorching hot veggies to your plastic blender). 
  4. Blend: When the veggies have cooled a bit, blend them with 1-2 cups of broth until very smooth. Add the blended mixture back to the pot; stir to incorporate.
  5. Pastina: Cook pasta according to package directions. Drain and toss with olive oil and a pinch of salt. 
  6. Serve: Put a scoop of pastina in a shallow bowl. Ladle the steaming-hot broth over the top, then top each bowl with freshly grated Parmesan.

  • Cook Time: 40 minutes

Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Subscribe now

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Let’s dive right in.

Where Do Colostrum and Collagen Benefits Overlap?

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Can You Take Them Together?

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

When Is One More Useful Than the Other?

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Here’s How I Break It Down:

Who should take collagen?

Everyone.

Who should take colostrum?

  • Athletes with iron balance issues.

  • Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

  • Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Final Thoughts on Colostrum vs. Collagen

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.

Are cooking skills the missing ingredient in sports nutrition?

(Image: My Fitness Pal)

Sports nutrition has advanced considerably in recent years, and athletes now routinely receive expert guidance to support training and performance. However, a new review highlights an often-overlooked question: do athletes know how to prepare and cook their food?

The review examined previous interventions designed to improve athletes’ food preparation and cooking skills. Despite extensive research on sports nutrition, the researchers found only 10 studies that combined hands-on food preparation with nutrition education. None measured whether athletes’ practical cooking skills improved.

The review therefore highlights a gap between sports nutrition knowledge and practical application. For example, a field-sport athlete may know to consume sufficient carbohydrate before training and combine carbohydrate with high-quality protein post-training. Yet this knowledge has limited value if they lack the confidence or skills to shop for, prepare, and cook suitable meals.

The review raises an important question for coaches and sports nutritionists: are we spending too much time telling athletes what to eat and too little teaching them how to prepare it?

New research challenges osteoarthritis exercise advice

(Image: Chris Bailey Orthopaedics)

A massive longitudinal study has challenged conventional medical advice for people with knee or hip osteoarthritis. Traditionally, these patients have been encouraged to choose low-impact activities such as cycling or swimming to help protect their joints. However, this large-scale longitudinal study analysed more than 17,000 patients and produced some very interesting findings.

The researchers found that participating in high-impact activities was not associated with an increased risk of knee replacement. In fact, those who regularly engaged in high-impact activities had a substantially lower risk of requiring a knee replacement than those who did not. Therefore, if an individual’s capacity allows, people with knee or hip osteoarthritis should not fear high-impact activities. Instead, they should embrace them, as this longitudinal study suggests they may help support long-term joint health.

New research on HYROX injuries

(Image: Chilitri)

As HYROX continues to grow rapidly in popularity, the first prospective study tracking injuries and health problems among HYROX athletes has now been published. The study followed 89 athletes over a 12-week period, providing new insight into the types of injuries occurring and the areas of the body most commonly affected.

The researchers recorded 14.4 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and competition. Notably, 73% of injuries were classified as overuse rather than acute injuries, while the knee was the most commonly affected area, accounting for 22% of all injuries. Running was also the most frequently reported injury mechanism. Interestingly, shoulder injuries were almost non-existent, despite shoulder problems being relatively common in other high-intensity training environments.

While considerably more research is needed, these early findings highlight the substantial running demands associated with HYROX and suggest that managing running load may be particularly important. For coaches and athletes, gradually building running capacity and carefully monitoring training load could therefore help reduce the risk of overuse injuries.

Apple Crisp

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

5 from 18 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 1 hour
  • Yield: 6-8 servings
Print Pin

Description

My one-and-only apple crisp recipe! Tender jammy honeycrisp apples under a blanket of buttery brown sugar oat topping. The perfect fall treat!

Ingredients

Apples:

  • 6 cups diced apples (I use 4-5 large Honeycrisp, unpeeled)
  • 1/2 cup granulated sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour

Topping:

  • 1/2 cup salted butter, melted (1 stick)
  • 1 cup brown sugar, lightly packed
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 cup rolled oats
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Preheat Oven: Preheat the oven to 350 degrees.
  2. Mix Apple Filling: Toss diced apples with the sugar, cinnamon, and flour in a large bowl until well-coated. Transfer to a 9×9 baking dish or a cast-iron skillet.
  3. Mix Topping: Mix melted butter, brown sugar, flour, oats, vanilla, and salt in a large bowl. Stir together with a spoon or fork. If needed, use your hands to bring the mixture together into a crumble; distribute evenly over the apples in the pan.
  4. Bake: Bake for 40-45 minutes, until the apples are jammy-looking around the edges, and the topping is golden brown.
  5. Rest and Serve: Let stand for 10-15 minutes, so the apple layer can cool and thicken slightly. Serve with vanilla ice cream.

Notes

This can work with or without peeling the apples. Personally, I don’t peel the apples – I am lazy, and I just can’t be bothered to mess with what is already a perfectly good fruit. The peels cook down the same way the apples do, so they become soft and unobtrusive in the apple crisp.

Homemade Italian Pan di Stelle Cookies. These iconic Italian breakfast cookies are made with chocolate, hazelnuts and honey and decorated with white stars. They’re super easy to make at home and are absolutely delicious!

Recipe overview

  • The recipe: Classic Italian chocolate and hazelnut breakfast cookies topped with white stars.
  • Texture and taste: Chocolatey with a subtle hazelnut flavour. They’re crisp and crumbly and perfect for dunking in milk or coffee.
  • Makes: 30 cookies
  • Perfect for: An Italian-style breakfast or snack!
jump to recipe

We have always loved Mulino Bianco breakfast cookies and love choosing from all the different varieties when we’re in Italy. After recreating their abbracci cookies (hugs), I knew the next one on my list had to be Pan di Stelle!

Pan di Stelle (meaning ‘bread of stars’) are one of Mulino Bianco’s most popular breakfast cookies. They’re made with chocolate, hazelnuts and honey and are decorated with iconic white stars on top.

I found small white star sprinkles online that were perfect for decorating these cookies; they make them look so cute and give them such a realistic Pan di Stelle look.

The cookies themselves are rich and chocolatey with a delicious crisp and crumbly texture, making them perfect for dunking in your morning cappuccino!

Ingredient notes

  • Italian 00 flour: This is my go-to flour for most of my Italian baking, but all-purpose flour (plain flour UK) will work well.
  • Chopped hazelnuts: you can buy these finely chopped or weigh the same amount and blitz them in a food processor until finely chopped.
  • Cocoa powder and dark chocolate: the original cookies use both cocoa and chocolate, which helps give them a deeper, richer chocolate flavour.
  • Butter: use unsalted butter softened at room temperature. If you use salted butter, omit the added salt from the dough.
  • Powdered sugar: helps give the cookies a crisp and crumbly texture.
  • Caster sugar (known as superfine sugar in the US): I love using caster sugar because its fine texture incorporates well into the cookie dough. If you can’t find it, then you can use granulated instead.
  • Star sprinkles: I found small white star sprinkles on Amazon. They help give the cookies a realistic Pan di Stelle look, which I love.

My top tips for making Pan di Stelle

Use a kitchen scale: although I have measured all the ingredients myself using US cups, weighing out your ingredients with a kitchen scale is much more accurate and can make a big difference to the end result.

Measure all ingredients before starting: the recipe flows so much better if all the ingredients are weighed out beforehand. Also make sure to melt the chocolate before starting so it’s not too hot when added.

Adding the star sprinkles: I find that gently pressing the stars into the cookies is all you need. I have tested dabbing the cookies with a wet finger to place the stars on top, which also works, but I find it easier to gently press them in.

Don’t have a cookie cutter? A glass will work just fine to cut out the cookies if you don’t have a cookie cutter.

How long do they last?

The cookies stay crisp if stored in an airtight container and will keep well for up to 2 weeks.

Pan di Stelle (Italian chocolate cookies)

Use the camera icon to toggle the step by step photos on and off. Homemade Pan di Stelle Italian cookies. Made with chocolate and hazelnuts and topped with white star sprinkles. They're perfect for dunking in your morning coffee. Course cookies Cuisine Italian Prep Time 30 minutes minutes Cook Time 15 minutes minutes cooling time 1 hour hour Total Time 1 hour hour 45 minutes minutes Servings 30 cookies Calories 73kcal Author Emily Wyper

Equipment

  • Stand mixer with paddle attachment (see notes if making by hand)
  • Baking tray (make sure it’ll fit in your fridge)
  • Parchment paper
  • Sieve
  • Plastic wrap (cling film)
  • Rolling pin
  • 2 1/2 inch (6.5cm) Round cutter

Ingredients

Dry ingredients

  • 245 g (1¾ cups) 00 flour (can also use all-purpose flour)
  • 30 g (¼ cup plus 1 tablespoon) unsweetened cocoa powder
  • 30 g (¼ cup) finely chopped hazelnuts (see notes)
  • 60 g (½ cup) powdered sugar (icing sugar)
  • 50 g (¼ cup) caster sugar (superfine sugar) can also use granulated
  • ½ teaspoon baking powder
  • ¼ teaspoon fine salt

Wet ingredients

  • 115 g (1 stick) unsalted butter softened at room temperature
  • 30 g (1 oz) dark chocolate (70%+ cocoa) melted and cooled slightly
  • 1 large egg yolk
  • 20 g (1 tablespoon) honey (runny not solid)
  • 15 ml (1 tablespoon) whole milk
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

Topping

  • White sugar stars for decorating
  • Granulated sugar for sprinkling (optional)

Instructions

Prep

  • Measure out all the ingredients and set aside. Melt the dark chocolate in a small pan over a low heat for around 1 minute, until melted. Turn off the heat and set aside.
  • Line a baking tray (one that will fit in your fridge) with parchment paper and set aside.

To make the dough

  • Sift the flour, cocoa powder and powdered sugar into the mixing bowl of your stand mixer, then add the caster (superfine) sugar, baking powder, salt and chopped hazelnuts. Mix everything for a few seconds until combined.
  • With the paddle attachment running on medium speed, add the butter a little at a time. Mix for a few minutes until the mixture looks dry and crumbly.
  • Next, add the honey, melted chocolate, large egg yolk, milk and vanilla. Mix again with the paddle attachment for around 2-3 minutes until the dough comes together. It will look dry at first but will soon come together.
  • Bring the dough together and shape it into a flat disc. Wrap in plastic wrap and place in the fridge for 30-45 minutes.
  • Remove the dough from the fridge and cut it in half (keep the other half wrapped up). Lightly dust your work surface with flour and roll out your piece of cookie dough to about ¼ inch thick (6- 7 mm).
  • Use your round cookie cutter to cut out as many cookies as you can and place them on the lined baking tray. Bring the dough scraps together and roll it out again to cut out more cookies until your tray is filled.
  • Place some white star sprinkles over the top of each cookie and press them in gently. An optional step is to sprinkle a small pinch of granulated sugar on top too.
  • Place your tray of cookies in the fridge for about 15 minutes while you preheat the oven. Keep any remaining dough wrapped in the fridge until you are ready to roll it out.

Bake

  • Preheat your oven to 180C/350F conventional.
  • Bake the cookies for 15-16 minutes. Let them sit for 5 minutes before transferring them to a cooling rack. Repeat the process with the remaining dough. You can bring the scraps together and re-roll as many times as you need to cut out more cookies. You should get around 30 cookies total.

Notes

  • Chopped hazelnuts: If you can’t buy finely chopped hazelnuts, you can weigh the same amount (skins on are fine) and blitz them until finely chopped in a food processor.
  • Dough consistency: If your dough isn’t coming together after around 3-4 minutes of mixing, you can add another ½ to 1 tablespoon of milk.
  • Refrigerating the dough: This stops the dough from spreading too much in the oven, but if it’s too cold, it can tear when you’re rolling it out. If this happens, just bring the dough back together and let it sit at room temperature for 5-10 minutes, then roll it out again.
  • No stand mixer? You can mix the dough by hand. Add all the dry ingredients to your mixing bowl, then add the softened butter. Use your fingertips to crumble the butter and flour together until it resembles large breadcrumbs. Add the wet ingredients and mix everything with a wooden spoon until combined. Use your hands to bring the dough together and shape it into a flat disc. If the dough isn’t coming together, add another ½ to 1 tablespoon of milk.

This Cordwood Workshop was built with thoughtful precision which produced beautiful results.

From Cheryl and Steve King:

“Thought I would share photos of our 20′ X 40′ shop build. We used 18″ western red cedar logs in a pole-construction frame (6″ X 10” treated “poles”).
Started installing logs & mortar in June of 2025 & set the last log Sept. 1, 2026.” We have had a ball.”

In our area, engineered plans were required to obtain a building permit. So, that was a hurdle to find an engineer willing to take it on. We found one in another state who also had credentials in Washington State. 

We hired a Builder to do the site prep, frame the pole building, and the cement work. After the roof was on, my wife & I took over, and progress slowed waaaay down. :-).
The cordwood portion of the build has rather tedious, especially the higher the walls got, but the sense of accomplishment at the end of each day was worth it.

 One thing I’m glad we did was to attend Charlie Yeager’s weekend seminar 3 summers ago. That gave us the confidence we needed to go for it.

Scaffolding, a ladder, eye protection, and pvc gloves are all part of having a safe working environment. 

The blue color on the horizontal logs is painter’s tape.  It keeps the post from accidental staining from the mortar.
