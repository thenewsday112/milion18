Pan-Fried Potatoes with Pancetta and Sage. Golden and crispy on the outside with a soft and fluffy middle. Full of flavour from salty pancetta and fragrant sage, they make such a delicious side dish to meat or fish.

These pan-fried potatoes are such a delicious side dish that goes with almost anything, and there are two things you need to know to get them perfect.

First is parboiling, which gives them a soft and fluffy middle and reduces the frying time. Second, and probably the hardest, is to leave them alone! Resisting the urge to toss them in the pan is my biggest tip, as it’s what gives them a delicious golden and crispy crust.

I absolutely love cooking potatoes this way, especially during the colder months when I’m craving cosy comfort food, and they can be served with anything from roast chicken to pork or fish.

See the full recipe below with step-by-step photos in the recipe card, plus my top tips and delicious serving suggestions. Enjoy!

Ingredient notes and substitutions

  • Potatoes: I like to use a floury potato so they get nice and crispy on the outside with a soft and fluffy middle. Maris Piper or Russet are good options.
  • Sage: I love the flavour of sage, and it works so well with these crispy potatoes. Other herbs work great too, such as rosemary, thyme or parsley (see adding herbs section below).
  • Pancetta: you can use pancetta or guanciale here. Fry it first so it releases some fat into the pan; this helps add flavour to the potatoes and gives them a delicious crispy texture.

Adding herbs and garlic

Adding herbs: If you are adding other fresh herbs like rosemary and thyme, finely chop them and add them about 10 minutes before the end of cooking. Add more delicate herbs such as parsley right at the end of cooking.

Adding garlic: There are two ways to add garlic. The first option is to add a whole, peeled clove to the pan while frying the pancetta, then remove it. This will infuse the oil with a subtle garlic flavour. For a stronger garlic flavour, you can finely chop it and add it to the potatoes about 5 minutes before the end of cooking. Don’t add it first, as it’ll burn by the time the potatoes are crispy.

My top tips for making crispy pan-fried potatoes

  • Frying pancetta: If your pancetta isn’t particularly fatty, add 1 tablespoon of olive oil to the pan to help it fry.
  • Parboiling time: When cut into medium-sized cubes, the potatoes should be parboiled for around 5-7 minutes. They should be fork-tender and not too fluffy; otherwise, they will break up too much in the pan.
  • Let them steam dry: After parboiling, drain the potatoes and let them sit and steam dry for 5-10 minutes before frying. This lets the excess moisture evaporate, which helps them crisp up when frying.
  • Don’t overcrowd the pan: Make sure you use a pan big enough so the potatoes can cook in an even layer. I used an 11 1/2 inch (29cm) pan.
  • Don’t move them too much: When you first add the potatoes to the pan, don’t touch them for the first 5-7 minutes, as it helps give them a nice crispy crust. If you are using a stainless steel pan (like I do), don’t flip them unless it’s easy to do so without much force, or they’ll stick to the pan. Once they have formed a nice crust, they should be easy to turn.
Can I make these without pancetta?

Yes, simply leave out the pancetta and add more olive oil (about 1-2 tablespoons) to replace the fat. You can opt to add in some chopped olives or capers at the end of cooking, although they are delicious just as they are.

Can I prepare these in advance?

Yes, you can cook the whole dish a few hours in advance, then crisp them back up in the pan for a few minutes before serving.

Serving suggestions

These pan-fried potatoes make such a delicious side to meat or fish. I love to serve them with pork chops, grilled or roasted chicken, salmon or white fish alongside green veg such as broccolini, green beans or arugula salad. Crispy Pan Fried Potatoes with Pancetta and Sage

Delicious crispy pan-fried potatoes made with pancetta and sage. These potatoes are full of flavour and make such a delicious side dish to meat or fish. Course Side Dish Cuisine Modern Italian Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 55 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 5 minutes minutes Servings 4 servings Calories 370kcal Author Emily Wyper

Ingredients

  • 900 g (2 lbs) potatoes (such as maris piper or russet)
  • 100 g (3.5oz) pancetta
  • Sage leaves (about 10)
  • Olive oil
  • Salt

Instructions

  • Bring a large pot of water to a boil and salt it well.
  • Peel the potatoes and cut them into medium-sized cubes. Add them to the boiling water and parboil them for 7 minutes. Drain them and set aside.
  • Meanwhile, add the pancetta to a large cold pan and fry until golden and crispy. If your pancetta doesn't have much fat, add around 1 tablespoon of olive oil to help it fry. Remove the pancetta to a bowl with a slotted spoon and set aside.
  • Add 1 tablespoon of olive oil to the same pan and add the sage leaves. Fry them for around 30 seconds on each side then transfer them to a plate lined with kitchen paper.
  • Add another 2-3 tablespoons of olive oil to the same pan over medium-high heat and add the potatoes. Quickly shake the pan for a second to arrange them in an even layer, sprinkle them with a pinch of salt, then let them fry for 7 minutes, untouched. Don't move the potatoes, as this helps them crisp up.
  • After 7 minutes, use a fish slice or spatula to toss the potatoes and give the pan a shake to spread them in an even layer. Add another drizzle of olive oil if needed, then fry for another 15 minutes, turning them over every 2-3 minutes until they're crispy and golden all over (around 20-25 minutes total).
    • Turn the potatoes gently so they don't break up too much!
  • Once cooked and crispy, add the pancetta and sage back to the pan and give it a toss to combine. Transfer to a bowl and serve.

Notes

Storage: the potatoes are at their best (and crispiest) served immediately, but they can be reheated in the pan if made in advance.

En Charente, un chemin de fer à voie étroite a été utilisé par la population rurale et citadine pendant quatre décennies jusqu’à la fin des années 1940. Paul Mairat, député et patron de presse, mena un combat acharné contre les grandes compagnies ferroviaires de l’époque.

Résultat, l’exploitation de chemin de fer d’intérêt local a pu s’opérer sur sept lignes différentes, irriguant ainsi la campagne charentaise.

Qui se souvient encore du « petit Mairat », ce train à voie étroite de la Compagnie des Chemins de fer de Charente ? C’est tout à l’honneur du musée installé dans l’ancienne gare de Gourville, en Charente, de ressusciter ce tortillard qui a rythmé la vie de la campagne charentaise pendant pas moins de quatre décennies. Patron de presse, député, maire, Paul Mairat (1865-1924) est resté attaché à ce réseau d’intérêt local qui s’étendait sur 343 kilomètres.

Après quinze années de lutte contre les grandes compagnies ferroviaires, ce fervent partisan du chemin de fer a eu la satisfaction, à partir de 1910, d’assister à la naissance des sept lignes qui irrigueront le pays charentais. L’une d’elles, qui reliait Segonzac à Saint-Angeau (77 km), traversait le village de Gourville par la grand-rue avant de passer devant l’Hôtel de la Gare, voisin de la gare. Cette longue artère porte aujourd’hui le nom du père du tortillard, Paul Mairat. Bon nombre de communes du département ont fait de même, histoire de ne pas effacer son nom de la mémoire des lieux.

Gourville est la seule gare rescapée de cette page d’histoire ferroviaire locale

Gourville, l’unique gare conservée en l’état, mérite un détour. Le petit bâtiment lui-même, fief du chef de gare, est rempli de lampes et d’outils d’époque. À côté, le musée recèle, lui aussi, de véritables trésors issus de l’univers du chemin de fer : un atelier de cloutier, une forge, des mannequins costumés, des cartes, des photos… 250 objets de collection sont à découvrir. Au centre, trône une locomotive à vapeur construite en 1910 et remise en état en 1974. C’est une « cousine » des Corpet-Louvet 030 T qui sillonnaient autrefois villes et campagnes.

Le Tacot a circulé pendant une quarantaine d’années jusqu’en 1949

À l’extérieur, on peut voir un wagon plat stationné sur un tronçon de rails non déferré et, dans la cour, la citerne à eau qui était utilisée par les cheminots pour faire « le plein » d’eau pour leurs machines à vapeur.

Un petit train qui ne roulait jamais à plus de 20 km/h…

Le Tacot était aussi l’autre surnom de ce petit train, « en raison de sa lenteur qui n’excédait pas 20 km/h », comme explique Christelle, chargée du Patrimoine à l’Office de tourisme. « Quant à l’appellation de “tortillard”, souvent employée, elle était due à sa similitude avec le tramway, qui s’immisçait au cœur des villes en effectuant des circonvolutions sur la ligne. » La lenteur du train, l’absence de confort à bord et la concurrence féroce de l’automobile auront raison peu à peu de ce moyen de transport.

Segonzac, Jarnac, Rouillac, Villejésus, Marcillac-Lanville, Malanville-Fouqueure, Luxe, Saint-Angeau, Mansle, Aigre… Le petit Mairat comptait sept lignes qui irriguaient la campagne charentaise. © François-Xavier Point

Avec la disparition de la dernière ligne, l’année 1949 a sonné le glas pour le petit Mairat. Lors d’une balade ou d’une randonnée, le promeneur peut seulement évoquer ce chemin de fer pas comme les autres. Car de nos jours, c’est le chant des oiseaux qui a remplacé à jamais celui du sifflet des locomotives à vapeur…

The sourdough category has officially left its flannel-shirt-and-farmers-market era and entered the functional nutrition main stage. Consumers are no longer buying sourdough simply because it feels artisanal or nostalgic. They are buying it because fermentation has become synonymous with:

  • digestive wellness
  • cleaner labels
  • lower glycemic response
  • microbiome support
  • authenticity
  • satiety
  • ingredient transparency

Sourdough has become the vinyl record of the bakery aisle. Consumers believe it is more “real,” less processed, and metabolically friendlier than conventional bread. In many cases, they are right.

The global sourdough market is projected to continue aggressive growth through the next decade, fueled by premium bakery expansion, digestive wellness trends, clean-label positioning, and consumer fascination with fermented foods. Artisan bakery sales continue to outperform many conventional bread segments, particularly among health-conscious and younger consumers seeking minimally processed foods. But underneath the crackling crust and chewy crumb lies something much more important: Sourdough fermentation is becoming a highly sophisticated biochemical platform for fiber transformation and microbiome modulation. And that changes everything for formulators.

Sourdough Is a Living Fermentation System

Traditional breadmaking is largely a rapid gas-production process driven by baker’s yeast. Sourdough is different. Sourdough ecosystems contain:

  • wild yeasts
  • lactic acid bacteria (LAB)
  • endogenous cereal enzymes
  • organic acid production pathways
  • extended fermentation cycles

These systems dynamically alter:

  • starches
  • proteins
  • polyphenols (see my paper on polyphenols)
  • sugars
  • and importantly… fibers.

The fermentation process literally remodels fiber structures during proofing and fermentation. This is not passive nutrition fortification. This is active biochemical transformation. Recent research has shown sourdough fermentation significantly alters arabinoxylan structure, molecular weight distribution, fermentability, and water extractability in wheat systems. That means fibers directly influence:

  • dough rheology
  • gas retention
  • viscosity
  • crumb structure
  • fermentation kinetics
  • moisture migration
  • shelf life
  • digestibility
  • glycemic response
  • downstream short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production

The bakery aisle suddenly starts looking suspiciously like a gut health aisle wearing a beret.

Fibers Are No Longer “Add-Ons”

For years, fiber fortification in bakery applications often resembled culinary dodgeball, throw enough fiber into the formula and hope the dough survives impact. That approach does not work in sourdough systems. In fermented dough systems, fibers become active participants in:

  • hydration dynamics
  • microbial metabolism
  • acidification behavior
  • enzymatic interactions
  • texture development
  • fermentation efficiency

Choosing the wrong fiber can:

  • tighten dough
  • suppress fermentation
  • create gummy crumb
  • increase density
  • destabilize gas cells
  • accelerate staling

Choosing the right fiber system can dramatically improve:

  • softness
  • moisture retention
  • fermentation activity
  • shelf life
  • digestive positioning
  • glycemic performance
  • clean-label functionality

This is where formulation becomes less baking, and more orchestra conducting a symphony of alchemy.

The Most Important Fibers in Sourdough Systems

Inulin and Fructooligosaccharides (FOS)

Inulin and FOS are among the most fermentation-compatible fibers available for sourdough systems.

Sources include:

  • chicory root
  • Jerusalem artichoke
  • Agave
  • enzymatically produced FOS systems

Functional Benefits

  • Improve moisture retention
  • Enhance softness
  • Support fermentation activity
  • Contribute mild sweetness
  • Improve mouthfeel
  • Support SCFA production downstream
  • Help reduce water activity migration

Typical Usage Levels

Application Usage Level Artisan sourdough breads 2% to 6% High-fiber bakery 5% to 12% Soft sourdough sandwich bread 3% to 8% Hybrid functional breads 6% to 15%

At moderate levels, inulin can improve softness and shelf stability without significantly disrupting dough structure. At higher levels, formulators must carefully manage:

  • Hydration
  • fermentation timing
  • dough strength
  • acidification rate

Because inulin is highly fermentable, excessive levels may accelerate acid production and alter proofing dynamics. That is the bakery equivalent of adding nitrous oxide to a street car. Exciting right until the rods shoot through the hood.

Soluble Tapioca Fiber

Soluble tapioca fiber systems bring unique advantages to sourdough applications due to:

  • clean flavor
  • high digestive tolerance
  • excellent water binding
  • low flavor interference
  • humectancy properties

Functional Benefits

  • Extends shelf life
  • Reduces staling
  • Enhances freeze-thaw stability
  • Improves machinability
  • Supports moisture retention

Typical Usage Levels

Application Usage Level Artisan sourdough 2% to 5% Soft sandwich sourdough 4% to 10% High-fiber formulations 8% to 15%

Soluble tapioca fiber performs particularly well in hybrid sourdough systems where bakers want:

  • artisan texture
  • softer crumb
  • longer shelf life
  • improved slicing performance

without excessive gumminess.

Acacia fiber behaves differently than many highly viscous fibers. It provides:

  • low viscosity
  • excellent digestive tolerance
  • microbiome support
  • clean neutral flavor profile
  • processing stability

Acacia works exceptionally well when layered with inulin systems to create more balanced fermentation behavior and improved digestive tolerance.

Cooking polenta in modern times doesn’t have much in common with the way it was prepared for centuries in the regions that now make up Northern Italy. Polenta was traditionally cooked on a grand scale in copper cauldrons over open fire. There was a lot of stirring, ladling, and creative, resourceful uses of leftovers. Historically it was considered one of the humblest foods and when I came across this line about polenta in Honey From a Weed by Patience Gray (1986), it stuck with me. “The idea of polenta, planted firmly in the north Italian imagination, is the antithesis of modern aspirations. It therefore intrudes on modern life like a vague emotional regret.”

But, polenta is wonderful. And similar to other foods that were cast off in exchange for industrialized, refined counterparts – brown rice, naturally leavened breads, etc. — many people find their way back to the “old ways” eventually.

What is Polenta?

Think of polenta as a cornmeal porridge of sorts. In The Classic Italian Cook Book (1973), Marcella Hazan asserts the importance of polenta by saying, “…to call polenta a cornmeal mush is a most indelicate use of language. In country kitchens, polenta was more than food, it was a rite. It was made daily in an unlined copper kettle, the paiolo, which was always kept hanging at the ready on a hook in the center of the fireplace.” She goes on to describe a wonderful scene where, “…there was a moment of joy as it [polenta] was poured out in a steaming golden circle on the beechwood top of the madia…”

About This Polenta Recipe

Polenta is a regular preparation in my kitchen, and it can be cooked a number of ways. Not just in a cauldron lol.  I jump back and forth between stovetop polenta and oven-baked polenta depending on a number of factors. Today I’m going to talk through my technique for making beautifully creamy polenta on the stovetop. We’ll get to the oven-baked version in a future post. That one is great if you just want to combine ingredients in a baking dish, push it into the oven, and forget about it until the timer goes off. But it’s not as creamy. In either case, the ingredient list is short, and most of your attention should be focused on sourcing some great cornmeal / polenta. Beyond that, a bit of patience, and technique is all you need. I love it served topped generously with this mushroom ragù as you’ll see down below. 

Which Polenta (a.k.a. cornmeal or grits) Should I Use?

Look for stone-ground, whole grain, whole kernel polenta without any additives – preferably organic. Store-bought, quick-cooking, refined polenta is the starchy endosperm of the corn kernel, and much of the flavor from the bran and germ is stripped out in the milling process. These are a few favorites to look for.

  • Hayden Flour Mills Yellow Corn Polenta:  My local market carries Hayden and this is the polenta I purchase most often. It is coarse grind, so it takes a bit longer to cook, but the flavor and texture is wonderful. (Pictured above right)
  • Bob’s Red Mill Organic Medium Grind Cornmeal: Stone-ground, whole grain. Readily available and a solid choice. If I’m looking for a polenta that will cook a bit faster, this is the one to grab because the corn is medium coarse and a bit finer. (Pictured above left)
  • Rancho Gordo Fine Yellow Polenta:  If you like a finer grind, this is your choice. Organic, heirloom corn grown & ground by Anson Mills for RG. IYKYK.

Whole polenta is quite perishable. Use it quickly or store it in the freezer.

Do I Have to Stir Polenta with a Wooden Spoon?

If you read through old polenta recipes, many will mandate stirring with a wooden spoon. It’s a good suggestion, but your polenta will be just fine if you use a stainless steel spoon instead. That said, wood spoons are kinder to your pots, less likely to scratch (especially if your pots have any sort of coating). And, some metal spoons (especially very old ones) can react to acidic ingredients resulting in a metallic taste. If you’re making tomato sauce or sourdough, for example.

Polenta Variations

Knowing how to make a great pot of polenta sets you up with a foundation to keep it simple and straight-forward (like the creamy polenta recipe below), or to add your own twists and flavors. Here are a few ways you can switch things up.

  • Use broth: Instead of water as the base liquid in your polenta experiment with broths, teas, or other infusions. I often make mine with a favorite mushroom tea (topping the polenta with these baked mushrooms.
  • Make it herby: Polenta has a real affinity for certain herbs. My favorite is chives. Double down with a couple handfuls of snipped chives folded in along with some chive compound butter, near the end of cooking so the chives retain their brightness.

What to Do with Leftover Polenta

There are many things to do with leftover polenta. Patience Gray includes this wonderful paragraph (Honey From A Weed, 1986) that exemplifies the cycle of a pot of polenta, “…made one day to serve hot, what is left is sliced and fried in hot oil till golden (polenta fritta), or brushed with oil and grilled (polenta ai ferri), to eat the next day. If chopped salame, parmesan cheese, butter and egg yolks are added to the polenta once it is stiff, and, when it has cooled, the beaten egg whites are folded in, and the preparation put into an oiled oven dish, what emerges after 45 minutes in a hot oven is a torta di polenta.”

  • How quickly sweetness arrives.

The Right Formulation Sequence

The fastest route to a bad outcome is adding R10 to a broken base formula and expecting it to become a superhero. Build the formula in this order.

Step 2: Set Acid and Salt Balance

Acid and salt do not merely change flavor. They change how sweetness is perceived. Before optimizing R10:

  • Finalize pH.
  • Confirm titratable acidity.
  • Establish the target acid blend.
  • Evaluate sodium and mineral contribution.
  • Confirm flavor loading.

A bright lemon beverage with too much citric acid may not need more modulation. It may need a smarter acid system. Formulation is often less about adding more players and more about getting the existing team to stop tripping over one another.

Step 3: Add R10 Late in Development

Once the formula is 80 to 90% right, screen R10 at controlled levels. Evaluate more than initial sweetness:

  • Sweetness onset
  • Peak sweetness
  • Mid-palate continuity
  • Bitterness
  • Metallic notes
  • Flavor identity
  • Flavor persistence
  • Aftertaste quality at 15, 30, and 60 seconds

If R10 improves flavor carry, the existing flavor may suddenly become too loud. This is often a win. You may be able to reduce flavor load slightly while improving balance and reducing cost. A product can taste more natural not because you added more flavor, but because the flavor now has a better place to land.

Step 5: Validate After Process and Through Shelf Life

Bench perfection is halftime. Taste the product:

  • Before processing
  • After processing
  • At pilot scale
  • After packaging
  • At realistic shelf-life intervals

Test after pasteurization, UHT, baking, extrusion, freezing, retort, or whatever cage match the product must survive. Reduced-sugar systems drift. Protein systems drift. Flavors drift. The calendar is undefeated.

Processing and Quality-Control Guardrails

Addition Method

At ppm levels, uniform distribution is the whole ballgame. For dry systems:

  • Create a validated geometric premix with a compatible dry ingredient.
  • Add the premix early enough to achieve uniform distribution.
  • Confirm mixer performance and blend time.

For liquid systems:

  • Prepare a compatible pre-dispersion according to the product specification.
  • Add with sufficient mixing.
  • Avoid adding at a point where the system is already too viscous for distribution.

For viscous systems:

  • Use a slurry or preblend.
  • Add before viscosity climbs high enough to sabotage dispersion.
  • Confirm distribution through retained samples and sensory comparison.

For plant scale:

  • Verify actual addition through batch records.
  • Use a gold-standard retain.
  • Compare pilot and production sensory results.
  • Do not assume “we added it” means “we distributed it.”

Heat and pH

The exact operating window must follow the current R10 technical documentation and be verified in the finished product. The practical rule is straightforward: Test R10 before and after the real process, at the product’s actual pH, solids, packaging conditions, and intended shelf life.

Do not casually assume that a clean bench sample will survive UHT, retort, high shear, a bakery oven, or six months in a warehouse with questionable air conditioning.

If tagatose acts as a prebiotic, does it produce the same digestive effects associated with other prebiotic ingredients? The answer is yes, but not exactly. And understanding why helps formulators create products consumers love instead of products consumers remember for the wrong reasons.

What Makes Something a Prebiotic?

The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) defines a prebiotic as: A substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms, conferring a health benefit. That’s an elegant scientific definition. Translated into plain English, a prebiotic is food for your beneficial gut bacteria. Those bacteria ferment the ingredient and produce compounds known as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) including:

  • Butyrate
  • Acetate
  • Propionate

These metabolites help support:

  • Colon health
  • Gut barrier integrity (mucosa)
  • Immune modulation
  • Mineral absorption
  • Healthy glucose metabolism
  • Satiety signaling

The take home word here is fermentation. And fermentation creates gases.

Why Prebiotics Can Cause Digestive Symptoms

Many formulators have worked with ingredients like:

  • Inulin
  • Chicory Root Fiber
  • Fructooligosaccharides
  • Galactooligosaccharides
  • Gum Acacia
  • Resistant starch
  • Partially Hydrolyzed guar gum

They’re fantastic ingredients. They’re also famous for producing customer reviews that include words like: “I felt bloated.” “My stomach sounded like a drum solo.” “Great product…don’t eat it before a meeting.” That’s because most traditional prebiotics arrive almost completely intact into the large intestine. Gut microbes suddenly receive a massive buffet. The bacteria celebrate. Your customers sometimes do not. Rapid fermentation creates:

  • Hydrogen
  • Carbon dioxide
  • Methane (in some individuals)

Along with:

  • Bloating
  • Gas kinetics
  • Abdominal discomfort
  • Increased bowel movements
  • Disaster pants

These are not safety concerns. They’re simply normal consequences of fermentation.

Tagatose Is Different

Tagatose doesn’t behave exactly like a dietary fiber. Instead, it follows a more interesting path. A significant portion of ingested tagatose is absorbed in the small intestine. The remainder travels into the colon where beneficial bacteria ferment it. That means the microbial benefits remain. But the fermentation load is generally smaller than with many traditional fibers. Think of it as feeding your microbiome, without throwing it an all-you-can-eat buffet.

What the Research Shows

Multiple human studies have demonstrated that tagatose can:

  • Increase beneficial Bifidobacteria
  • Increase Lactobacillus populations
  • Produce beneficial short-chain fatty acids
  • Support a healthier gut microbiome

At the same time, gastrointestinal tolerance has generally been shown to be favorable at typical food-use levels. Like every fermentable carbohydrate, tolerance is highly individual.

Dose Matters

Here’s where formulation becomes important. Small amounts incorporated throughout the day are generally well tolerated by most consumers. Very large single doses, >100 grams, are another story. As intake increases, more tagatose escapes digestion and becomes available for microbial fermentation.

That can increase:

  • Gas
  • Bloating
  • Loose stools
  • Gastrointestinal discomfort

This isn’t unique to tagatose. It’s true of virtually every prebiotic ingredient. Even apples can do this. It’s simply the biology of fermentation.

Think of It Like Exercise

Nobody walks into a gym after years on the couch and deadlifts 500 pounds. Your gut microbiome isn’t much different. When consumers gradually increase prebiotic intake, the microbial ecosystem often adapts. Tolerance frequently improves over time. This is one reason many nutrition professionals recommend introducing prebiotics gradually.

A product containing:

  • 8 g tagatose
  • Protein
  • healthy fats
  • fiber

will often behave differently than, 50 grams dissolved in a beverage consumed on an empty stomach. Matrix matters. Serving size matters. Consumption pattern matters. Everything is dose dependent.

This review contains spoilers for the film You Can See Everything.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better. 

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them? 

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests. 

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic. 

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario. 

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things. 

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

Could weight-loss drugs become the next major controversy in sport?

(Image: ChatGPT)

The Washington Post recently published a fascinating article examining the growing interest among athletes in weight-loss drugs, particularly GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound. Although these medications were developed to treat conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity, their ability to suppress appetite and promote weight loss has raised important questions about their potential use in competitive sport. This is particularly relevant to athletes in weight-category sports such as boxing, MMA, wrestling and weightlifting, where even small reductions in body weight can be significant. However, losing weight does not necessarily lead to improved athletic performance.

The article also highlights that the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) has been monitoring certain weight-loss medications since 2024 because of concerns about their potential performance-enhancing effects. In September, WADA confirmed that these medications would remain permitted in competitive sport throughout 2027, although research into their potential misuse is ongoing.

The article also referenced a recent study of sports medicine physicians, which found that 12% of those surveyed were aware of elite athletes using GLP-1 medications without a medical need. Another notable development is that tennis player Serena Williams has publicly discussed using Zepbound for weight loss and improved mobility, bringing even greater mainstream attention to the debate.

Although WADA currently considers the evidence insufficient to justify banning these medications, their potential use in competitive sport raises important questions. The next generation of weight-loss drugs could complicate matters further, particularly if new treatments become more effective at reducing body fat while preserving muscle mass. With these medications set to remain permitted throughout 2027, could weight-loss drugs become the next major controversy in sport?

Santos FC introduces robotic exoskeletons for training and recovery

Hypershell Exoskeleton (Image: Fox News)

An intriguing story has recently emerged from Brazilian football club Santos FC (see here), which has introduced robotic exoskeleton technology into its S&C department. The club announced that it had received seven exoskeletons from technology company Hypershell.

These lightweight, battery-powered wearable devices attach around the hips and legs. By combining electric motors, movement sensors and AI-based motion control, the technology claims to reduce the muscular effort required during activities such as walking and running.

According to reports, the club’s medical and performance staff are exploring several potential applications for the devices. One is recovery, where exoskeletons could reduce physical effort during low-intensity movement after matches or demanding training sessions. The club is also investigating their use in strength and endurance training, including mechanical assistance or resistance to adjust training demands. In addition, the technology could support rehabilitation and return-to-play protocols by allowing athletes to progress gradually towards normal physical workloads.

It will be particularly interesting to see whether these devices can genuinely improve recovery between demanding training sessions and matches, enhance athletic performance, or provide meaningful benefits during rehabilitation and return-to-play programmes. For now, however, their effectiveness in these areas remains unproven.

Overall, this fascinating development in sports science and technology raises an intriguing question: how long before robotic exoskeletons become a regular feature in the S&C departments of professional football clubs?

Is isometric calf strength testing an effective predictor of athletic performance?

(Image: Instagram)

S&C expert Professor Stephen Patterson recently shared an interesting LinkedIn post highlighting new research that questions the effectiveness of isometric calf strength testing as a predictor of athletic performance.

The research found that isometric calf strength explained approximately 9% of drop jump performance and just 2% of jump height and sprint speed, with none of these links reaching statistical significance. Patterson highlighted a key limitation of conventional isometric calf strength testing: the lack of specificity to the mechanical demands of sprinting and jumping. Many isometric calf strength assessments are performed with the ankle positioned close to neutral, at approximately 2.5° of dorsiflexion. In contrast, during sprinting and jumping, peak ankle torque occurs at approximately 16–21° of dorsiflexion, under substantially higher movement velocities and within the stretch-shortening cycle. Consequently, these conventional isometric assessments may fail to adequately replicate the joint angles, contraction velocities, and neuromuscular demands associated with explosive athletic movements.

Based on these findings, Patterson suggested that if the primary purpose of isometric calf strength testing is to predict sprinting or jumping performance, he would stop using it. Importantly, this does not necessarily invalidate isometric calf strength testing as a measure of calf strength but rather questions its usefulness as a standalone predictor of dynamic athletic performance.
