From the 1937 Archives: How a Forgotten Railway Station Became Will Hay’s Buggleskelly

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In October 1937, The Railway Magazine reported how Cliddesden station on the abandoned Basingstoke and Alton line had been used in Will Hay’s film Oh, Mr. Porter!. The contemporary account introduces the fictional Buggleskelly station and the elderly locomotive Gladstone, before recounting the film’s story.

“Oh, Mr. Porter”

Cliddesden becomes Buggleskelly

CLIDDESDEN station on the abandoned Basingstoke and Alton line of the Southern Railway has been enjoying a fresh lease of life. Flourishing under the assumed name of “Buggleskelly” it was the scene of action of Will Hay’s new film “Oh, Mr. Porter” lately filmed by Gainsborough Pictures (1928) Limited. The cast includes William Porter (Will Hay), Jeremiah Harbottle, Albert, and the redoubtable Gladstone, an ancient but stout-hearted engine (ex Kent & East Sussex Northiam).

The celebrated “Gladstone” and part of cast

William Porter takes charge

The story is briefly as follows: William Porter has been responsible for dozens of mishaps but the Superintendent dare not fire him—though he has longed to for twenty years—because William is related by marriage to the Managing Director. His wife has threatened to take him to live with her relatives if he loses his job on the railway. Eventually the Superintendent, in despair, offers him a small position at Aberdeen or Inverness, but William is determined to be a station-master. The Managing Director and the Superintendent find a vacancy at Buggleskelly, a small market town on the Southern Railway of Northern Ireland.

A supposedly haunted railway station

The superstitious Irish believe that Buggleskelly is haunted. The sole staff of the derelict station are the ancient Jeremiah Harbottle and his assistant Albert. These two have not been paid for months, but live quite comfortably by stealing and by paying for goods with tickets belonging to the company.

The mysterious excursion train

William tries to modernise Buggleskelly, and arranges an excursion. A one-eyed man buys all the tickets for the football team and William sees them off at 6 a.m. while Harbottle and Albert are sleeping off the effects of a brawl at the inn. Harbottle and Albert refuse to believe Will has really sent out an excursion, and tell the signalman to let the express through. To William’s surprise there is no crash; his train seems to have disappeared, and there is consternation on the line.

Gladstone and the missing train

The villagers declare Will’s one-eyed Irishman was a ghost, but he does not believe it and sets off with Harbottle and Albert in an ancient engine—Gladstone—to see whether the train is in a tunnel on a disused line. They find the train, and the one-eyed Irishman and his men loading it up with cases of arms. William realises they are gun-runners.

“Buggleskelly”, repainted and en fête

A dramatic escape

The gun-runners chase William, Harbottle, and Albert up the hill and to the top of a windmill, where they escape by climbing out on to the sails. They uncouple the excursion engine, at the same time attaching Gladstone to the other end of the train. Then they dash along the line with the gun-runners aboard, not daring to stop lest the men should get out. Harbottle suggests throwing out a message in a bottle, to warn the police.

Gladstone’s final journey

Unfortunately Gladstone is now out of hand, and dashes into the terminus. William is congratulated by the Superintendent for capturing a notorious gang and modestly passes the laurels on to the old engine Gladstone, but alas! the strain has been too much for its inside—and it bursts and blows to bits!

Mexican Burrito with Chicken & Avocado

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Why buy burritos when they’re so simple to make? Save time with precooked chicken (you can also use leftovers!) and budget-friendly staples like canned beans, shredded cheese, salsa and frozen corn. Then, step up your burrito game by adding creamy avocado and fresh lime juice. Wrap in foil, and pack for a homemade lunch.

Active time: 15 minutes | Total time: 20 minutes

Mexican Burrito with Chicken & Avocado

Ingredients

  • 8 oz (227g) boneless, skinless chicken breasts, cooked 
  • 1/4 cup (38g) fresh or frozen corn (thawed)
  • 1/4 cup (43g) no salt added black beans, drained and rinsed
  • 2 tbsp finely chopped cilantro, divided (plus more for optional garnish)
  • 1/2 cup (56g) shredded reduced-sodium Cheddar cheese
  • 1 small avocado (150g), diced and divided
  • 1/4 cup (60g) low-sodium tomatillo salsa, divided (plus more for optional garnish)
  • 1 tsp fresh lime juice
  • 1/4 tsp salt (or to taste)
  • 1/4 tsp pepper (or to taste)
  • 3 medium (8-inch/45g each) whole-wheat flour tortillas
  • Diced tomatoes (optional garnish)

Directions

Warm the chicken (if desired) and chop into bite-size pieces.

In a small bowl, combine the chicken, corn, black beans, 1 tbsp cilantro, cheese, 1/2 avocado and 2 tbsp salsa. Mix well to combine. Add salt and pepper. In a blender or food processor, combine the lime juice and the remaining avocado, salsa and cilantro. Blend until creamy. Taste for seasoning, adding more lime juice or salt as needed.

Spread 1/3 of the chicken filling into the center of a flour tortilla. Fold in the sides, and bring up the bottom of the tortilla, tucking in the ends. Keep rolling, pressing and creasing the end of the tortilla as you finish rolling it up. Top with avocado cream, and, if desired, more salsa, cilantro and tomatoes.

Serves: 3 | Serving Size: 1 burrito

Nutrition (per serving): Calories: 456; Total Fat: 20g; Saturated Fat: 7g; Monounsaturated Fat: 8g; Polyunsaturated Fat: 3g; Cholesterol: 83mg; Sodium: 566mg; Carbohydrate: 34g; Dietary Fiber: 8g; Sugar: 4g; Protein: 35g

Nutrition Bonus: Potassium: 535mg; Iron: 20%; Vitamin A: 10%; Vitamin C: 25%; Calcium: 25%

An analysis of tightening guar supply, rising raw material costs, and the implications for food and beverage manufacturers using partially hydrolyzed guar gum as a soluble dietary fiber.

A tightening guar market creates new challenges for PHGG

The global guar market is experiencing significant price pressure, and manufacturers incorporating partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG) into their formulations should be paying attention.

According to an October 9, 2026 supplier market update, guar splits prices have increased approximately 30% since April, including a nearly 5% increase in November futures on October 8 alone.

While much of the market discussion has focused on conventional guar gum, the implications extend to PHGG, a specialized, enzymatically hydrolyzed derivative of guar gum used primarily as a soluble dietary fiber.

PHGG occupies a very different functional position from conventional guar gum. Rather than providing substantial viscosity or thickening, PHGG is manufactured to deliver soluble fiber with relatively low viscosity, making it particularly useful in beverages, nutritional products, powdered supplements, and other applications where manufacturers want to increase fiber content without dramatically changing texture.

That distinction is important because PHGG manufacturers depend on the same underlying agricultural supply chain as conventional guar gum producers.

When guar seed and guar splits become more expensive or less available, PHGG producers face increased raw material costs, even though the final ingredient undergoes additional specialized processing.

For food and beverage manufacturers, the concern is not simply whether PHGG prices will rise. It is whether raw material volatility could affect the cost, availability, and continuity of supply of an increasingly important functional fiber.

Understanding the connection between guar splits and PHGG

PHGG begins with guar seed, primarily cultivated in India. The seed is processed to separate the endosperm, which contains the galactomannan polysaccharides responsible for guar gum’s functional properties.

The manufacturing process generally follows this sequence: Guar seed → Guar splits → Guar gum → Controlled enzymatic hydrolysis → Purification and drying → PHGG

Conventional guar gum consists of high-molecular-weight galactomannans capable of producing significant viscosity in aqueous systems. 

During PHGG production, controlled enzymatic hydrolysis reduces the molecular weight of these polysaccharides, producing a soluble fiber ingredient with substantially lower viscosity. The resulting material retains much of the underlying carbohydrate structure while exhibiting different rheological and functional characteristics.

This additional processing creates a more specialized ingredient, but it does not eliminate dependence on the original guar crop. A disruption at the agricultural level can therefore propagate through the entire value chain, from guar splits to finished PHGG.

However, a 30% increase in guar splits prices does not automatically translate into a 30% increase in PHGG prices. Finished PHGG costs also reflect processing yields, enzyme treatment, purification, drying, quality assurance, transportation, and producer margins.

The extent of price transmission will depend on the manufacturer’s cost structure, raw material inventory, and contractual commitments.

The 2026 supply picture

Preliminary estimates cited in the October supplier update suggest that India’s 2026 guar harvest may be limited to approximately six million bags, representing around 600,000 metric tons of guar seed.

Based on an estimated 28% recovery rate, that crop could yield approximately 168,000 metric tons of guar splits.

The supplier also reports combined guar gum and guar splits exports averaging approximately 25,000 metric tons monthly over the previous year.

These figures suggest a potentially tight raw material environment, although they should not be interpreted as a precise supply deficit. Finished guar gum and guar splits represent different stages of processing, and the market’s actual balance depends on conversion yields, domestic consumption, carryover inventories, and available stocks. Nevertheless, several reported developments deserve attention.

Farmers are reportedly withholding guar seed in anticipation of higher prices. Speculative traders are purchasing guar splits, potentially reducing the quantities immediately available to industrial processors. Meanwhile, uncertainty surrounding the final crop size makes forward procurement more difficult.

For PHGG producers, this combination can create two overlapping challenges: rising input costs and increased competition for suitable raw material.

Unlike conventional guar gum, PHGG is not necessarily a commodity that manufacturers can replace quickly with another supplier’s material. Differences in molecular weight distribution, fiber concentration, residual viscosity, solubility, sensory characteristics, and regulatory documentation can make supplier qualification more complicated. That increases the importance of securing consistent, specification-compliant supply.

Why PHGG manufacturers face a different risk profile

The market for PHGG is more specialized than the market for conventional guar gum. PHGG production requires controlled enzymatic processing and additional quality controls to achieve the desired molecular weight distribution, solubility, low viscosity, and dietary fiber specifications.

These characteristics are particularly important for manufacturers using PHGG in clear or lightly flavored beverages, nutritional powders, functional foods, and digestive wellness products.

PHGG is valued because it can deliver meaningful quantities of soluble fiber without the substantial thickening associated with conventional guar gum. Published research also describes its suitability for certain acidic and thermally processed food systems, although stability must be confirmed under the specific formulation and processing conditions. 

This specialization introduces additional supply-chain considerations. A producer facing higher guar splits costs cannot necessarily switch to a different raw material without evaluating its impact on processing efficiency and finished-product specifications. Likewise, a food manufacturer purchasing PHGG cannot assume that all commercially available grades will behave identically.

For manufacturers developing fiber-fortified products, a supply disruption could therefore mean more than paying a higher price. It could require requalification of an ingredient that plays an important nutritional and functional role in the formulation.

What the latest market evidence tells us

Independent industry reporting reinforces the concern surrounding guar prices. A September market analysis documented substantial increases in Indian guar seed and guar gum futures, citing lower acreage, weak monsoon conditions, and restricted supply.  More recently, an October 7 report from Rantec Corporation identified continued price pressure associated with dry growing conditions and reduced planting acreage. 

These developments support the broader conclusion that PHGG’s agricultural feedstock is experiencing upward price pressure. However, there is an important limitation: publicly reported guar futures prices do not establish the actual price increase for finished food-grade PHGG.

PHGG contract pricing may lag raw material markets because of existing inventory, forward purchasing arrangements, and processing economics. 

Manufacturers should request current PHGG quotations and supplier-specific forecasts rather than applying commodity price increases directly to their finished ingredient budgets.

Implications for food and beverage formulators

PHGG has gained attention as a soluble dietary fiber for applications in which manufacturers want to improve nutritional composition while maintaining acceptable sensory properties.

Its relatively low viscosity allows formulators to incorporate fiber without necessarily producing the thick or gummy mouthfeel associated with some high-molecular-weight hydrocolloids.

That functionality is particularly valuable in ready-to-drink beverages, powdered drink mixes, nutritional shakes, meal replacements, and selected dairy and plant-based applications.

For manufacturers targeting a declared dietary fiber level, the relevant calculation is not simply the amount of PHGG added. It is the quantity of qualifying dietary fiber delivered by the ingredient.

Recommended procurement strategy for Q4 2026 and H1 2027

For manufacturers currently using PHGG, the most appropriate response is to review purchasing coverage before raw material volatility creates delivery problems.

Near-term requirements through December 2026 should be evaluated against existing inventory, open purchase orders, and confirmed supplier allocations. Manufacturers with uncovered requirements should seek firm delivery commitments rather than relying exclusively on historical lead times.

First-quarter 2027 requirements warrant early supplier discussions. Where production forecasts are sufficiently reliable, staged forward purchasing may reduce exposure to additional price increases while avoiding unnecessary inventory accumulation.

Second-quarter 2027 requirements should remain part of the planning conversation, particularly for manufacturers with high PHGG consumption or limited alternative sources.

Supply-chain teams should request visibility into supplier raw material coverage, manufacturing lead times, minimum order quantities, price-validity periods, and potential allocation policies.

For manufacturers using PHGG as a principal fiber source, dual-source qualification may be worth evaluating. However, any alternative should be assessed for dietary fiber content, molecular weight distribution, viscosity, solubility, sensory performance, regulatory status, and processing stability before commercial substitution. A lower quotation offers little benefit if the alternative cannot reliably deliver the intended nutritional or functional performance.

Looking ahead: PHGG supply through 2027

The outlook for PHGG will depend on several interconnected factors. The first is the actual size of India’s 2026 guar harvest. Preliminary estimates indicate a smaller crop, but final production figures and carryover inventory remain uncertain.

The second is competition for guar splits among industrial processors, including conventional guar gum producers and manufacturers serving food, pharmaceutical, and other markets. The third is the extent to which raw material inflation reaches finished PHGG pricing.

Because PHGG involves additional processing and specialized manufacturing, price transmission may be delayed or moderated. Nevertheless, prolonged increases in guar splits costs would be expected to place pressure on PHGG production economics.

A fourth factor is the ability of manufacturers to maintain consistent supply. If raw material availability becomes constrained, suppliers may prioritize existing contractual commitments or strategic customers.

For food and beverage manufacturers, these conditions justify proactive planning without assuming that a shortage or a particular PHGG price increase is inevitable.

Final assessment

Partially hydrolyzed guar gum remains a technically valuable ingredient for manufacturers seeking soluble dietary fiber with minimal impact on viscosity and sensory properties. Its advantages in beverage and nutritional applications are not diminished by current agricultural market conditions.

However, the approximately 30% increase in reported guar splits prices since April 2026 highlights a developing vulnerability in PHGG’s upstream supply chain. Manufacturers should recognize that the raw material economics supporting PHGG production are changing, even if those changes have not yet appeared fully in finished ingredient quotations.

The appropriate response is to review near-term requirements, engage suppliers early, evaluate forward coverage, and maintain clear visibility into ingredient specifications and inventory positions.

The central message is straightforward: PHGG remains a strong formulation choice, but its agricultural supply chain deserves closer attention as manufacturers plan for 2027.

I’ve been wanting to make Rasta Pasta for a while because I’m a BIG fan of bold, spicy flavors. This Jamaican-inspired dish brings all that flavor to the table with tender jerk-seasoned chicken, sweet bell peppers, and penne coated in a creamy Parmesan sauce. The peppers and creamy sauce help balance the warmth of the jerk seasoning, and everything cooks in one skillet for less cleanup! It’s colorful, hearty, genuinely delicious, and ready in 45 minutes. Better yet, at just $2.80 per serving, it’s a fun way to switch up pasta night without spending a fortune!

A Spiced Chicken and Peppers Pasta

If you’ve never heard of Rasta pasta, here’s a little background! It’s believed to have originated in Negril, Jamaica, in 1985, when chef Lorraine Washington served homemade fettuccine with tomato sauce and ackee (Jamaica’s national fruit) to a group of construction workers. The red, gold (yellow), and green colors associated with the Rastafari movement inspired one of the workers to name it Rasta pasta. And you’ll spot those same colors in the three bell peppers I use in my recipe, too! There are plenty of versions of this dish today, but the one I’m most familiar with is this creamy, jerk-seasoned pasta. My recipe is a simple, budget-friendly take on this dish, rather than a recreation of the original.

For my version, I’ve kept things easy on both the cook AND the grocery budget! I sear the jerk-seasoned chicken first, then cook the colorful peppers in the same skillet so none of those delicious browned bits go to waste. I also skip boiling the penne separately and simmer it right in the chicken broth, where it absorbs all that savory seasoning and releases starch to help thicken the sauce. Half-and-half keeps things creamy without the extra cost of heavy cream, and I stir in Parmesan off the heat for a smooth finish. It’s a similar approach to our creamy Cajun chicken pasta, but with the bold flavor of jerk seasoning and plenty of colorful peppers.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Cut the chicken into thin cutlets. I slice each chicken breast horizontally in half before seasoning. This gives me four thinner pieces that cook more evenly and quickly, without the outside drying out before the center is done. Aim for 3-4 minutes per side, but always check the thickest part reaches 165°F with a meat thermometer. And here’s a little budget win: I only need 1 lb. of chicken for four hearty servings, thanks to all those peppers and pasta!
  2. Adjust the jerk seasoning to your spice tolerance. Jerk blends can vary a LOT in heat and saltiness. For a milder pasta, I’d choose a mild jerk blend rather than leaving out most of the seasoning. That way, you still get those lovely aromatic flavors from spices like allspice and thyme. The broth and Parmesan add salt, too, so give the finished sauce a taste before adding any more.
  3. Keep an eye on the pasta and the liquid. Unlike some of our other one-pot pasta recipes, I simmer this one uncovered. This lets some of the broth evaporate as the penne releases starch, creating a flavorful, slightly thickened base for our creamy sauce. Keep the broth at a steady simmer and stir often. The penne should still have a little firmness in the center, with most of the broth absorbed. It’ll finish cooking once you add the half-and-half. If the pan looks dry before the pasta is ready, add a splash of hot broth or water. Still looking too soupy? Give it another minute or two to reduce before adding the dairy. Different pans and stovetops can make a difference here!
  4. Be gentle with the creamy sauce. Half-and-half is my budget-friendly alternative to heavy cream, but its lower fat content makes it more likely to separate if you boil it too hard. Once you add it, keep the heat low and let the sauce thicken around the pasta. Then turn off the heat before stirring in the Parmesan to help prevent clumping. I also save the fresh lime for serving rather than adding it directly to the hot dairy sauce. Everyone can add a squeeze to their own bowl, and there’s less risk of the acidity making the sauce grainy.

Meal Prep it!

I’m all for getting an extra lunch out of dinner, and this Rasta pasta is PERFECT for meal prepping! Whenever I’m serving veggies on the side, I’ll usually make a little extra and pack them alongside the pasta (like the broccoli and green beans shown here). I like using divided meal prep containers to keep the veggies from sitting in the creamy sauce. Then, once the pasta is cool, I’ll just pop the lid on and store everything in the fridge for up to 4 days. The pasta does soak up some of the sauce as it sits, so I add a little splash of water or broth when reheating in the microwave to bring back its saucy consistency.

If you know you’ll be reheating the pasta throughout the week, cook it just until tender rather than letting it get overly soft in the pot.

how to make Rasta Pasta Step-by-Step Photos

Gather your ingredients: Before getting started, gather and measure everything you’ll need for this pasta. I recommend having your jerk seasoning, chicken broth, half-and-half, and Parmesan ready to go so you can focus on cooking once the skillet gets hot. You’ll also need a large, deep skillet or Dutch oven with enough room to stir the pasta as it cooks.

Prep the vegetables: Dice 1 yellow onion, slice 1 red, 1 green, and 1 yellow bell pepper into strips, and mince 3 cloves of garlic. Try to keep the pepper slices roughly the same thickness so they soften evenly in the skillet.

Prepare and season the chicken: Carefully slice 2 large boneless, skinless chicken breasts (about 1 lb. total) horizontally through the middle to make 4 thinner cutlets. Sprinkle about 1 Tbsp of jerk seasoning over both sides, making sure each cutlet is evenly coated.

Cook the chicken: Heat 1 Tbsp cooking oil in a large Dutch oven or deep skillet over medium-high heat. Once the oil is hot and shimmering, add the seasoned chicken. You should hear a nice sizzle! Cook for 3-4 minutes per side, until the outside is nicely browned and the thickest part reaches an internal temperature of 165°F. Transfer the chicken to a plate and let it rest while you prepare the pasta. Don’t wash the skillet! Those browned bits left behind will add flavor to the sauce.

Soften the vegetables: Add the diced onion and sliced bell peppers to the same skillet. Cook for 4-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion begins to turn translucent and the peppers start to soften but still have a little bite. As you stir, gently scrape up the flavorful browned bits left behind by the chicken.

Add the garlic and seasoning: Stir in the 3 minced garlic cloves and the remaining ½ Tbsp jerk seasoning. Cook for 1 minute, stirring constantly, until the garlic smells fragrant and the seasoning is evenly distributed. Keep everything moving so the garlic and spices don’t burn.

Cook the pasta in the broth: Add 8 oz. penne pasta and 2 cups chicken broth to the skillet. Stir well, making sure the pasta is coated in the seasoned broth, then bring everything to a simmer. Cook uncovered for 8-10 minutes, stirring frequently and scraping along the bottom to prevent sticking. The pasta should be nearly tender, with most of the broth absorbed and a little starchy liquid remaining. Don’t worry if the pasta isn’t completely cooked yet…it’ll finish cooking in the next step.

Make it creamy: Pour in 1 cup half-and-half and stir to combine. Continue cooking for 3-4 minutes, stirring frequently, until the penne is tender and the sauce has thickened. Keep it at a gentle simmer, not a vigorous boil. The sauce should look creamy and lightly coat the pasta.

Finish with Parmesan: Remove the skillet from the heat and stir in ½ cup (45g) finely grated Parmesan cheese. Keep stirring until the cheese melts into the warm, starchy sauce and everything looks smooth and creamy. If the sauce seems a little too thick, stir in a splash of water or chicken broth until it reaches your preferred consistency.

Slice the chicken: Once the chicken has rested, slice each cutlet into thin strips, cutting across the direction of the muscle fibers (against the grain). This shortens the fibers, making the chicken more tender and easier to chew.

Garnish and serve: Arrange the sliced jerk-seasoned chicken over the creamy pasta. Finish with 2 sliced green onions and a squeeze of fresh lime from 1 lime cut into wedges, if desired. (However, I highly recommend the lime! Its fresh acidity adds a lovely finishing touch to the warmly spiced sauce). Serve warm and enjoy!

Storage & Reheating

Let any leftovers cool, then transfer it to an airtight container and refrigerate for up to 3-4 days. I recommend storing any extra green onions and lime wedges separately so you can add them fresh when serving.

Reheating

The pasta will absorb some of the sauce as it sits, so I like adding a splash of water, chicken broth, or milk when reheating. You have two options:

  • Stovetop: Add the pasta to a skillet with a splash of liquid and warm over medium-low heat, stirring occasionally until heated through.
  • Microwave: Place a portion in a microwave-safe bowl with a little water or broth. Cover loosely and heat in 30-second intervals, stirring between each, until heated through.

Whichever method you use, reheat the chicken and pasta to 165°F throughout. Keep the heat gentle to help prevent the creamy sauce from separating.

Freezing

I don’t recommend freezing this Rasta pasta. The creamy sauce can separate once thawed, and the penne can become overly soft when reheated. It’s definitely best enjoyed fresh or refrigerated for a few days.

Butternut Squash Gnocchi with Taleggio Cream

Gnocchi in a roasted butternut squash sauce served with taleggio cream and crispy sage. Cosy, comforting, and full of flavour! Course gnocchi Cuisine Italian Prep Time 15 minutes minutes Cook Time 50 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 5 minutes minutes Servings 4 servings Calories 466kcal Author Emily Wyper

Ingredients

  • 500 g (1.1 lb) gnocchi
  • 1 small butternut squash (1½ lbs/680g)
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 2 sprigs rosemary
  • 20 g (½ cup) Parmigiano Reggiano finely grated
  • 60 ml (¼ cup) heavy cream (double cream UK)
  • 12 sage leaves for serving
  • Olive oil
  • Salt and pepper

Taleggio cream

  • 80 ml (1/3 cup) heavy cream (double cream UK)
  • 100 g (3.5 oz) taleggio cheese rind removed

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 180C/350F fan or 200C/400F conventional.
  • Cut the squash in half and remove the seeds. Cut the squash into quarters and trim off the skin using a knife. Chop the squash into medium-sized cubes and place in a baking tray.
  • Add the 2 garlic cloves (skins on), remove the rosemary from the sprigs and add it to the tray with a good pinch of salt and pepper. Drizzle over 2-3 tablespoons of olive oil and toss so everything is coated. Roast in the oven for around 35-40 minutes, until cooked through.
  • While the squash is cooking, make the crispy sage leaves.
  • Heat 1 tablespoon of olive oil in a pan. Once hot, add the sage leaves and fry them for 1-2 minutes on each side. Transfer them to a plate lined with kitchen paper to drain. Set aside.
  • Once cooked, blitz the squash with garlic (skins removed), rosemary, heavy cream and Parmigiano cheese until smooth using a food processor or blender (I did this in a bowl with an immersion blender). Taste for seasoning and add more salt if needed.
  • Transfer the squash sauce to a large pan and bring a large pot of water to a boil.
  • Meanwhile, add the heavy cream and taleggio to a separate saucepan over medium-low heat until the cheese has melted and the cream has thickened slightly. Keep the taleggio sauce on a low heat, stirring occasionally while you cook the gnocchi.
  • Add the gnocchi to the boiling water and cook until they float to the surface. At the same time, heat the squash sauce over medium heat and add around ½ cup/120ml of pasta water to loosen it to a saucy consistency,
  • Transfer the gnocchi to the sauce and stir until well coated. Add more pasta water if needed.
  • Serve the gnocchi in bowls, drizzle over the taleggio cream, then top with crispy sage and serve.

Notes

  • Prep in advance: you can prepare the butternut squash sauce in advance and store it in the fridge for up to 3 days. Add it to a pan and loosen it with pasta water when ready to serve. The taleggio cream is best served immediately.
