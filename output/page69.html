Punch.ev Achieves 5-Star Bharat-NCAP Safety Rating

📅 Jun 13, 2024  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“We welcome stricter government safety standards and are proud to be the first manufacturer to have sent vehicles and lead the Bharat-NCAP protocol with superlative results. We are delighted to produce India’s safest vehicle – an EV – in the Punch.ev, while the Nexon.ev continues its legacy of safety with its 5-star rating.” Shailesh Chandra, MD, Tata Passenger Electric Mobility and Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles

Tata Passenger Electric Mobility Ltd (TPEM), a subsidiary of Tata Motors has declared that the Punch.ev and the Nexon.ev have achieved the 5-Star Bharat-NCAP safety rating. While the Punch.ev set a major milestone by receiving the highest ever scores achieved by any vehicle till date – 31.46/32 and 45/49 points for adult occupant protection (AOP) and child occupant protection (COP) respectively, the Nexon.ev scored 29.86/32 and 44.95/49 points for AOP and COP respectively. With this, Tata Motors is now the only OEM with the safest range of SUV portfolio scoring 5-stars across Bharat-NCAP and Global-NCAP tests.

Nitin Gadkari, Minister of Road Transport and Highways of India, expressed, “My heartfelt congratulations to Tata Motors on this significant milestone as the Nexon.ev and Punch.ev receive a 5-star rating, under Bharat-NCAP. The certification aligns with the Indian government’s vision for safer vehicles in the country and emphasizes the role of Bharat-NCAP in making India’s automobile industry ‘aatmanirbhar’. The Bharat-NCAP car safety standard is pivotal in realizing the government’s vision to make India a global automobile hub and increase its export worthiness in international markets.”

Commenting on this extraordinary achievement, Shailesh Chandra, MD, Tata Passenger Electric Mobility and Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles, said, “Safety, once less discussed, is now a top priority for the Indian car buyer. At Tata Motors, safety is engrained in our DNA, which has made us an industry benchmark. We have continued to be the pioneers in driving the safety conversation – ensuring that our commitment reflects in every vehicle we manufacture, regardless of price point. We welcome stricter government safety standards and are proud to be the first manufacturer to have sent vehicles and lead the Bharat-NCAP protocol with superlative results. We are delighted to produce India’s safest vehicle – an EV – in the Punch.ev, while the Nexon.ev continues its legacy of safety with its 5-star rating. Together, all four of our SUVs tested under the Bharat-NCAP have achieved 5-star ratings, setting a benchmark for all passenger vehicles to follow. Looking ahead, our proactive stance on safety will continue, backed by extensive R&D to help us evolve and enable us in leading the way to create a safer future for every road user.”

The Punch.ev, since its launch, has been quite an attraction for EV enthusiasts and first-time buyers, with over 35% owners from the rural markets. With its combination of long range, exalting performance, cutting-edge technology, and features that are generally found in segments 2 – 3 above its own, the Punch.ev has attracted over 10,000 proud members to its family. It’s not only a truly electric SUV; it’s a bold step into the future of mobility. The Punch.ev also scores mighty high on convenience and punches above its weight, offering a premium experience that is accessible to customers.

Zone Fun Training

📅 May 15, 2024  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There’s lots of talk about Zone 1, 2, 3, and 4 cardio, with each “zone” based on the level of aerobic or anaerobic energy you’re utilizing. The lower the zone, the more aerobic you are and the more fat you’re burning as percentage of total energy. The higher the zone, the more anaerobic you are the more glucose and glycogen you’re burning. Lower zones are easier and slower. Higher zones are harder and faster. 

This is a sound way to think about “cardio,” but it doesn’t cover every zone. There’s one big training zone missing from the conversation: Zone Fun.

Subscribe now

In Zone Fun, intensity doesn’t matter. It can be high or low. You can go fast or slow. Intense or easy. Zone Fun oscillates across all the heart rate zones. Because the prime concern and determinant is whether or not you’re having fun, the other stuff melts away. Not in importance—all the aerobic zones still matter, intensity still matters—but in what you have to consciously pay attention to.

You can just keep going as long as you’re having fun and moving your body.

I’d go so far as to say that hitting Zone Fun is the most important metric for your cardiovascular training. If you reach Zone Fun, if you’re moving fast and slow and medium and moving with intensity and then slowing things down, you are doing the right thing. You are improving your health and fitness. Having fun is a good barometer for doing the right thing health wise. Your body intrinsically “knows” when you’re improving skills, increasing VO2max, building an aerobic base and your ability to tolerate anaerobic discomfort, strengthening muscle—fun is the tell. 

We have evidence that Zone Fun training is even more effective than other kinds. When you subject rodents in the lab to forced exercise—sticking them on a rodent wheel, making them tread water, tying weights to their tails and making them drag them and climb ladders—you see moderately beneficial effects, but sometimes negative effects. When you give rodents the opportunity to engage in voluntary exercise like free access to a wheel, and they choose to do it, the benefits are greater. For instance, one study found that mice doing a ten week voluntary running program had more favorable shifts in muscle tissue fiber development than mice doing ten weeks of forced running.1 Another even found that mice with colitis had higher mortality and inflammation when they were forced to exercise and lower inflammation and mortality when they chose to exercise.2

Okay, so what are some ways to practice Zone Fun training?

Ultimate Frisbee

My favorite way to get into Zone Fun is with the greatest sport of all time: Ultimate Frisbee. Sadly, I don’t get to do this as often as I’d like to. But when I lived in Malibu, I had a weekly game set aside with the same group of guys. It was a mix of running, jumping, throwing, jostling for positioning, sprinting, and jogging. Most importantly, it was fun. It was a game. It was a game requiring strategy, planning, and intense concentration. Because when you’re concentrating or when you’re thinking ahead and thinking in the moment, you’re having fun. You’re not thinking about the hard work that you’re doing. You don’t even realize that you’re breathing hard, that you’re starting to get sore. You don’t notice the burning. And therein lies the beauty of Zone Fun.

Fat tire biking

I’ve taken fitness influencers out on the sand that are half my age and they always tell me it’s the hardest workout of their lives and can barely keep up. No joke. But the thing is, you keep going because riding through sand next to the beautiful water, seeing all the lovely sights that one sees, invigorates and makes the work worth it.

Standup paddling

Paddling is my other method of Zone Fun training. Up on the paddleboard, I get into my rhythm and flow, and the hours just melt away. I'm never quite breathing hard. My serratus anterior muscles are getting an incredible workout (there's no better ab exercise). My heart is working, the blood is flowing, and the fat is burning. This is a gentle form of training.

Share

Play sports

The perfect way to access Zone Fun training is to play sports.

Pick a sport that you used to really enjoy when you were younger. The trick to training is that it shouldn’t feel like work. You also shouldn’t feel like you’re learning to play. You should come into it fairly naturally. So, if you’re playing basketball, you should be able to dribble the ball, make a layup, and generally feel comfortable with the flow of the game. If you’re trying to pick up a game of soccer, make sure that you’re comfortable handling the ball, passing, shooting, and generally move with the way the game moves. And that’s true for anything. You don’t want to be learning the fundamentals when you’re trying to engage in Zone Fun. You don’t have to be an all-star, but you shouldn’t be a total beginner.

Make the boring interesting

Another way to use Zone Fun training is to take what might normally be a boring form of movement and make it more interesting and fun. For example, instead of running on a treadmill, go for a trail run. Changing topography, textural shifts, rising and climbing the trail, plus all the plants and animals and beautiful smells around you, will make what’s normally a boring plod around the track much more interesting and fun. Deep into the endurance world, I did most of my training on trails and mountain passes just because it was so much more interesting than just running around the track or on a treadmill. It was the only way I could make the incredibly uncomfortable training bearable.

Another substitution might be mountain biking instead of road biking. Insead of zoning out on a road, you have to constantly stay alert for roots, rocks, jumps, drops, and trees. You have to stay immersed in the moment—you can’t just bear down and suffer like you can on the road. This gives you just as good a workout without feeling like it.

Rock scrambling

Rock scrambling is my absolute favorite way to traverse the wild. I grew up in a small fishing town in Maine, scrambling all over the rocks that lined the coast. I think this was how I got most of my movement and training foundation. There's something so satisfying about scrambling on all fours up extremely steep rock faces. It’s just pure fun and I think it triggers something primordial within us.

Loaded partner carries

Here's what I mean by loaded partner carries:

Get a group of at least two people together. Choose a walking path, ideally with an incline. Up a mountain is best. Then, pick a heavy object, at least one per person. If you have two people, choose 1-2 objects. If you're a group of three, choose 2-3 objects. And then you go on the walk while carrying the objects and switching off with each other.

A kettlebell and a weighted sandbag. A big log and a heavy rock. A pair of dumbbells. A barbell and a rucksack. You get the idea. Just go on the walk with a friend or friends, taking turns carrying the objects you brought. This is very hard work, but because you're working together, it's fun. You have each other to lean on and nobody wants to let the other people down. Turns out that camaraderie is extremely motivating.

Those are my favorite ways to train Zone Fun. What about yours? How will you incorporate more Zone Fun training into your regimen?

📅 Apr 15, 2024  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Victoria Quirk (Courtesy Turkey & The Wolf Icehouse)

Nashville has long been a haven for aspiring country stars and songwriters, but these days, my hometown is also experiencing a surge in culinary talent. A decade or two ago, finding hand rolls made with fish from Tokyo or natural wine by the glass was a challenge. Now, we’re in business, with more options coming down the pike at a rapid pace.

Born and raised in Nashville, I spent my childhood digging into plates of fried chicken with sides of squash casserole and turnip greens at Elliston Place Soda Shop and meandering out to Highway 100 for a plate of biscuits at The Loveless Cafe—establishments that have fed this city for generations. We’ve always eaten well here, but as the city has changed, so have its tastes, expanding beyond the down-home cooking visitors might expect. 

That’s a good thing. Ours is a vibrant dining scene that caters to all tastes and budgets, from experimental fine dining to tried-and-true lunch counters. Use this as your primer to getting a taste of Music City. Whether you’re craving a plate of hot chicken from Prince’s or a piece of freshly butchered bluefin tuna, Nashville has it.

Bar Roza

211 Arcade Alley

Victoria Quirk (Courtesy Bar Roza)

Tucked into an alley attached to the Nashville Arcade lies one of Nashville’s dreamiest new bars, an oasis of blush Italian marble and cocktails that defy convention. The product of chef Julia Jaksic and beverage director Owen Gibler, it offers downtown a much-needed respite from honky-tonks and domestic beers. Gibler uses digestifs and aperitifs to create cocktails that have exceptionally aromatic qualities on the nose and on the palate. Jaksic’s menu of bites is designed to accompany cocktails and conversation. Think gorgeous little snacks like cheesy gougères with spicy ’nduja and fluffy focaccia topped with rich stracciatella and mortadella. The bar has quickly amassed a crowd of regulars and industry pros. Come for the drinks, stay for the impeccable vibes and tunes on an analog reel-to-reel tape player. 

Kase x Noko

707 Porter Road
(615) 806-0649

Mick Jacob (Courtesy Kase x Noko)

Visiting Nashville? Step one: Book your reservation at Kase x Noko, the tiny omakase spot that’s connected to uber-popular Noko. Step two: Book everything else on your trip around that reservation. That’s because this 14-seat sushi counter is one of the hottest tickets in town, offering a 14-course omakase at an unheard of $75 price point. There are ways to ratchet up the bill, of course, with add-ons like “steak and eggs”—nigiri stacked with A5 Wagyu, uni, and caviar—or flame-seared yellowtail belly, expertly formed and presented by chef and co-owner Dung “Junior” Vo and his crew. The playlist is curated by partners Wilson Brannock and Jon Murray, leaning heavily on early-2000s hits, an excellent background for cocktails starring flavors such as shiso, yuzu, and pandan. 

Maíz de la Vida

606 8th Avenue South
(629) 288-8459

Courtesy Maiz de la Vida

What started as a pandemic-era tortilla pop-up quickly blossomed into a very popular food truck, followed by a tortillería, and now a full-service restaurant in the Gulch, serving chef Julio Hernandez’s Tennessee-Mexican dishes. Hernandez, who grew up among Tlaxcala’s corn and cooked his way through New York and Nashville kitchens, serves dishes such as grilled chorizo with heirloom beans, served with collard greens with smoked ham hock, salsa verde, and tortillas, and Tennessee trout with popcorn grits. The heart of it all is the masa that’s nixtamalized in-house using corn from Mexico and, more recently, Tennessee farms. Mezcal and tequila cocktails are thoughtful and plentiful.

Turkey and the Wolf Icehouse

800 Meridian Street
(833) 420-4236

Victoria Quirk (Courtesy Turkey & The Wolf Icehouse)

Turkey and the Wolf was born in New Orleans, where it became an instant hit for its irreverent vibes and gonzo takes on Southern favorites. Luckily for Nashville, chef-owners Mason Hereford and Lauren Agudo were lured here by its friendly restaurant community, and they teamed up with chef-partner Will Mondros to open the Icehouse in a cinder-block building and covered patio on a scruffy corner lot in East Nashville that was once a dog park. Now, the yard is filled with thrifted tables, an Airstream that serves as the office, and sculptures including a brontosaurus and a life-size rearing horse. The menu includes hits that helped launch Turkey and the Wolf to fame, like the collard melt oozing with slow-cooked greens, Swiss cheese, and Russian dressing, and the towering bologna sandwich stacked with thick slices of fried bologna and a healthy handful of vinegar potato chips. But the Icehouse has its own personality and surprises, including a buttery, flaky roti full of juicy lamb and topped with handfuls of herbs. Specials are constant and often experimental, the beers and cocktails are ice-cold, as the name implies, and there’s often live music in the yard. There’s always a party going on at the Icehouse.

888

800 Clark Place
(888) 383-8610

Courtesy 888

Even if you read the directions closely—“follow the red light”—it may still take you a moment to find the entrance to 888, the Japanese listening room located below the towering JW Marriott, just around the corner from the main hotel entrance. Once inside, the vibes are groovy and the fish is fresh. In fact, diners visiting on the second Saturday of the month will witness master sushi chef Kiichi Okabe breaking down an enormous bluefin tuna, which is then served for immediate, luxurious consumption. Beyond Okabe’s sushi menu, try larger plates like a koji-marinated rib eye cooked over binchōtan charcoal, or the donabe with chicken thighs, mushrooms, and cured egg yolk. The restaurant is styled after a kissa, a Japanese vinyl listening room, and it delivers heavily on that promise with rotating DJs who play soul, funk, disco, and more, as well as full album parties that require silence from the room. Don’t miss out on the Silken Spirit Martini, a tableside service that includes the use of a hand-cranked mechanical shaker to mix Japanese vodka or gin with sake and orange bitters.

Elliston Place Soda Shop

2105 Elliston Place, Nashville, TN 37203
(615) 219-2704

Courtesy Elliston Place Soda Shop

Aside from hot chicken, Nashville’s other hometown food is the meat ’n’ three, a plate lunch consisting of a protein and three sides. The Southern classic is a specialty of Elliston Place Soda Shop. Opened in 1935, it is one of the few remaining (and best examples) of the genre. Generations of Nashvillians have graced the red leather booths and black-and-white-tiled floors over the years, tended to by a staff that feels like it turns over about once a century. (Ms. Linda, “the pie lady,” still makes her beloved coconut meringue pies every morning.) Though Elliston Place moved to a newer space next door a few years ago, it still features the soda counter and pressed-tin ceiling, which makes the milkshakes and sundaes taste like the good old days.

International Market

2013 Belmont Boulevard
(615) 297-4453

Mary Craven (Courtesy International Market)

International Market introduced Nashvillians to Thai food in the 1970s, when it opened on Belmont Boulevard serving an exceptionally affordable steam table lunch of Thai and Chinese dishes. Now, with a new location (across the street) and new ownership (Anna and Arnold Myint, children of the original owners), the restaurant incorporates high-quality local meats and produce in an updated setting—though thankfully the original mustard-and-orange bench seating came along for the ride. The kitchen is now a proving ground for chef Arnold Myint, a James Beard semifinalist. Menu items venture beyond the typical selections of curries with five-spice duck lo mein, shrimp and pineapple curry, and red curry rice ball wraps. At lunch, diners can choose from the classic steam table or order from the menu; nighttime brings table service with Singhas and craft cocktails.

Locust

2305 12th Avenue South

Courtesy Locust

At Locust, chef Trevor Moran created his own culinary genre blending influences from Japan to his native Ireland. I’ve eaten briny Belon oysters that tasted—pleasantly—like licking a battery; a roast sole served with morels and uni; and a bowl of tiny sea snails served with toothpicks and spicy salt. The DIY beef tartare hand rolls—freshly ground bottom round, fluffy rice, and smoky pickled egg cream, plus freeze-dried capers and nori for rolling—are a must, as is the tuna crisp, featuring house-cured slices of tuna loin and belly atop a fried wonton crisp slathered with horseradish. The chefs deliver these dishes to the tables themselves to an eclectic soundtrack featuring everything from Meat Loaf to old-school hip-hop. The drink list is heavy on natural wines, though my go-to is the Toki Highball cocktail or a can of sake. In homage to Moran’s birthplace, there’s also Guinness by the pint, and Irish gin and tonics compete with Orion beer and sake cups.

Kisser

747 Douglas Avenue

Camille Tambunting

The diminutive Kisser, modeled after a Japanese kissaten, a neighborhood spot serving comfort food where locals can eat, drink, and hang out, has been a smash hit since opening in 2023. After spending years in some of the best kitchens in L.A. and Nashville, co-owner couple Brian Lea and Leina Horii decided to focus on their own vision. Inspired in part by Horii’s family sushi restaurant in California, the menu blends traditional Japanese cuisine and their fine-dining techniques with dishes like onigiri stuffed with snow crab and avocado, a perfectly crispy chicken katsu sandwich on fluffy housemade milk bread, and bowls of udon in delicate broth. It’s one of the best places in town to enjoy fresh fish, particularly the chirashi bowl brimming with pristine sashimi and glistening salmon roe. Make sure to end your meal with a green melon cream soda or a crackly miso crème brûlée.

Folk

823 Meridian Street
(615) 610-2595

Courtesy Folk

Folk is a neighborhood gem that Nashville needs now more than ever as hotels and chains threaten to take hold of the dining scene. The restaurant is perched on a quiet corner in East Nashville’s McFerrin Park and welcomes diners with wood-fired sourdough-crust pizzas—my favorite is adorned with littleneck clams, parsley, bonito, and lemon—and rustic Italian-leaning dishes like Marcella beans with spicy pepper relish, bouncy focaccia with whipped ricotta, and more. From the high-ceilinged dining room, adorned with paper lanterns and exposed-brick walls, you can glimpse the bustling kitchen and blazing pizza oven. Natural wines and classic cocktails are the move at the bar, where the afternoon light streams in during the daily apéro hour. The crowd of shockingly healthy houseplants are as well-nourished as the patrons.

Redheaded Stranger

305 Arrington Street
(615) 544-8226

Courtesy Redheaded Stranger

Not far from Folk stands a Tex-Mex taco shop with a sense of humor where you can gobble down one chef’s highly agreeable take on Taco Bell’s Crunchwrap. To make it, chef Bryan Lee Weaver (also of Butcher & Bee) fills his fluffy handmade tortillas with Texas red chili, pork green chili, or both when you order it “Xmas-style.” If tacos are more your speed, you’ll find toppings like brisket, tater tots, and freshly roasted Hatch chiles. To me, the sleeper hit is the vegetarian taco with charred poblano, whipped feta, and crispy rice, thanks to its variety of textures and smoky heat. The queso dip comes with more of those warm, velvety tortillas (never chips!), and the frozen margaritas are tart and free of sickly-sweet sour mix. The interior offers retro-diner vibes, with a Formica bar and red leather booths, but there’s nothing like grabbing a patio seat in the summertime to let those margs work their Texas magic.

Henrietta Red

1200 4th Avenue North
(615) 490-8042

Emily Dorio ( Courtesy Henrietta Red)

Lucky for Nashvillians, former Per Se chef and Nashville native Julia Sullivan came home to open her first solo restaurant in Henrietta Red. This airy, tiled Germantown spot boasts an oyster bar glistening with mollusks, including Maine’s salty Mookie Blues and Alabama’s buttery Murder Points. Despite Nashville’s landlocked status (the Cumberland River doesn’t count), Henrietta Red deftly combines coastal ingredients with Southern flavors in dishes like oysters roasted with ‘nduja butter and whole trout with dandelion greens. Wood-fired seafood is the focus here, but the fresh pastas and vegetables—particularly the beet salad with pistachio and mint—stand out too. Brunch brings its own reason to visit, with dishes like shrimp toast with beets and tahini, and smoked fish cakes with lemon-fennel aïoli.

Prince’s Hot Chicken

5814 Nolensville Pike
(615) 810-9388

Despite the ever-widening swath of restaurants serving Prince’s signature dish around the globe, you have to visit the original purveyor to get real-deal Nashville hot chicken. As the story goes, the ultra-spicy sandwich was created as punishment by a jilted lover of Thornton Prince; but, instead of catching on fire, Prince loved the spice so much that he’d go on to perfect the recipe that launched his business. Today, Prince’s remains the best in class, serving up fried chicken spiced with cayenne pepper oil atop a slice of white bread with a pickle (a foil to all that cayenne). If you’re sticking closer to downtown, swing by Prince’s outpost in the Assembly Food hall to get your hot chicken fix. 

Wendell Smith’s

407 53rd Avenue North
(615) 383-7114

Courtesy Wendell Smith’s

Wood-paneled walls line the dining room of this classic meat ’n’ three restaurant that’s been serving up Southern soul since 1952. Every day, the fourth-generation owners welcome a regular crowd hungry for roast beef, baked ham, and pit barbecue, plus daily specials like fried catfish, chicken and dumplings, and throwback fixins like candied yams, turnip greens, baked apples, fried corn, and creamed potatoes. Wendell Smith’s is a slice of Nashville that has hung on to its corner lot despite unchecked development all around it, still sharing space with the adjacent liquor store of the same name that’s been operating just as long. 

Loveless Cafe

8400 Highway 100
(615) 646-9700 

Courtesy Loveless Cafe

What began 70 years ago as a humble motel and café at the start of the Natchez Trace Parkway has been thriving from the moment Lon and Annie Love began serving biscuits and fried chicken to travelers out of their home kitchen. After their continuing success, the couple turned their home into a restaurant and opened 14 rooms as a motel to accompany their new dining destination. The motel went out of operation years ago, and its rooms are now home to retail space, with smoked hams, jams, and biscuit mix on offer. Though there is now expanded outdoor seating and an outdoor bar serving bloody Marys when the wait is long, there’s nothing better than grabbing a seat in the tiny dining room that still feels like home.  

Sperry’s Restaurant

5109 Harding Pike
(615) 353-0809

Courtesy Sperry’s Restaurant

An institution since 1974, this legendary West Nashville steakhouse is a windowless dining room featuring backlit stained glass, carpeting, a stone fireplace with a roaring fire (most nights), and sporty portraits of the hounds and horses that once lived on the grounds of the abutting Belle Meade Mansion. Martinis and Manhattans pair perfectly with Sperry’s steaks and seafood, from a filet Oscar smothered in crabmeat and béarnaise to king crab legs served with drawn butter. Nashville’s very first salad bar remains gloriously intact here, and diners can pile their plate with neon-green goddess and bacon crumbles (though the plated iceberg wedge salad topped with bacon and black olives is my choice). Bananas Foster, another highlight, is served tableside in a plume of booze-soaked flames and cinnamon-fueled sparks. The doors open at four for happy hour, when sausage-stuffed mushrooms and discounted cocktails draw a steady stream of locals each day.
