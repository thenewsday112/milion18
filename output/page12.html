Everyone Is Running a Con in You Can See Everything

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Full spoilers follow for You Can See Everything, which is in theaters now.

“The greatest trick the devil ever pulled was convincing the world he didn’t exist.” — The Usual Suspects

You Can See Everything is, deservedly, one of the most-talked-about films of the year, and is sailing past box office records for documentaries. The filmmaking itself is impressive, often surprising, and effective. But the movie’s staying power is as a social experiment about whether any of us truly know, care about, or communicate what’s “real” outside of our own interests. Truth exists on a spectrum in the world of this film, and it often feels like a game of narcissistic whack-a-mole.

Liar, or Wired Differently?

We understand that co-directors Nathan Fielder (Nathan for You) and Lance Oppenheim (Primetime) haven’t delivered a definitive take on whether Elizabeth Holmes is a liar; according to the legal system and whistleblowers, she is, after her company Theranos was revealed to be, essentially, hawking a blood-testing machine that didn’t actually work. What the film is asking is whether Holmes understands that she is lying, or if she can discern the difference between what she wishes were so, and what is. It’s like crafting a “not guilty by reason of insanity” defense for fraud.

For years, pundits and armchair psychiatrists have speculated that Holmes has Narcissistic Personality Disorder (NPD) and sociopathic or psychopathic tendencies, though she has no public diagnosis. Yet, in You Can See Everything, there are moments where Holmes appears to be neurodivergent in a way that reframes her entire story. That is to say nothing of the fact that her partner, Billy Evans, is (on screen, at least) wildly controlling, and the relationship often feels abusive. In the world of the film, Holmes sometimes appears as a victim and the most sympathetic character. So the questions become: was she manipulated by her original romantic and business partner, Ramesh “Sunny” Balwani, whom Holmes met when she was 18 and he 37? Is that pattern now playing out with Evans? Is she a narcissistic sociopath, or is she on the spectrum in such a way that inhibits her from reading social cues and understanding language nuances to a degree that she can lie, and not know that she is?

The first real jaw-dropping moment in You Can See Everything is when Fielder shows Holmes and Evans the famous Abbott and Costello sketch “Who’s on First?” She finds it funny, yet insists that she didn’t understand that the first baseman’s name was “Who,” which is the entire crux of the joke. As Fielder presses, she becomes visibly upset by her own confusion, and Evans sends her to bed like a child. Again and again, the film suggests that her discernment is inhibited, and she isn’t using language the way the rest of us do. Evans claims her brain simply works differently.

Fielder runs this experiment because a question arose earlier around Holmes’ wording in a clip he plays for her, in which she says the Theranos blood-testing machine “can” run the tests without clarifying that it couldn’t yet. Holmes and Evans bring the machine out of storage to demonstrate what she’d meant: it was possible for the machine itself to work, but the testing had not caught up with its capabilities. The implication is that what was seen as a lie was really Holmes’ failure to understand linguistic nuance. Could it be, we wonder, that this woman did terrible things, but also, that her actual neurological conditions have been misunderstood in such a way that her sentence isn’t commensurate with the reality?

Theranos whistleblower Tyler Shultz wants to disabuse us of that speculation. Talking to CBS, he said he almost jumped out of his seat during the sequence featuring the Theranos device because, according to him, that was not the machine they were using on patients, but a future version that was never completed. In his view, the film re-litigates Theranos just enough to sow doubt in the audience’s mind. This brings us to a broader point, one Shultz makes: this isn’t a documentary. What this is, is a very well-made film and a cleverly told story, one that, in ways the filmmakers are most certainly aware of, and perhaps some they are not, holds up a funhouse mirror to truth, and to all of us.

Showmen, Not Documentarians

The question of who is manipulating whom starts with the film itself. Holmes and Evans pitched a “documentary” to tell their version of the story, and the directors used the same unregulated label to tell theirs. Nobody had to meet a real, standardized legal bar to capture the “truth.” Broadly, when it comes to documentaries and entertainment, the courts seem to rest on an imagined “reasonable viewer” who recognizes dramatization and has enough discernment to tell entertainment from reality. When Olivia de Havilland sued FX over the series Feud, the court didn’t seem to believe that an audience would interpret a docudrama as fact. This defense can hold up even on a news network. When Fox News was sued over Tucker Carlson’s show, Fox’s lawyers argued that no reasonable viewer would believe what he was saying was factually accurate. The judge agreed, writing that given Carlson’s reputation, his viewers arrive “with an appropriate amount of skepticism,” and dismissed the case.

The question of who is manipulating whom starts with the film itself.

But research suggests that real viewers simply aren’t making these distinctions. A Washington University study found that when a film contradicted an accurate text, students recalled the film’s misinformation as much as half the time. Perhaps more saliently, a general warning that films can be inaccurate didn’t change the outcome; only a specific warning about that particular error had an impact. According to the Telegraph, the share of UK viewers who believe The Crown is an accurate account of history rose from 19% to 27% between 2022 and 2023. Worrisome, perhaps, but understandable; they call it suspension of disbelief for a reason. Basically, we’re not very good at parsing fact from fiction even in the best circumstances.

Given that, if accuracy is the goal, then it’s notable that Fielder and Oppenheim were tapped for this project. This isn’t Ken Burns or Errol Morris. These are showmen whose work has, for years, seemed to blur the line between performance and reality. There are layers of irony here. Oppenheim just released Primetime, a film about the ways in which To Catch a Predator host Chris Hansen was willing to use and manipulate subjects to get what he needed to make a great story. Meanwhile, Hansen calls the film a “complete work of fiction” and says it exploited his name and image for commercial gain. And now Oppenheim has co-directed this film, about a known liar, that may be playing with ideas of truth itself. For his part, Fielder has faced questions about whether the way participants were portrayed in both Nathan for You and The Rehearsal skirted ethical lines. To be clear, they are very good at what they do, and this isn’t to say they’re unethical. One lens on the film is the idea that they’re purposefully using their own reputations to ask some very interesting questions. Another is that they framed pieces of Holmes in or out, depending on what was convenient for their narrative.

Enter Amanda Seyfried

Certainly, there are points where they know they’re doing sleight of hand, and as viewers, we’re left wondering if they’re challenging the audience to catch them. One of the film’s great twists comes just as you think the story is over: Holmes has gone to prison, and you can almost feel yourself in the editing room with Fielder and Oppenheim as they realize that what they have is interesting, but not quite enough to truly break through. You can nearly hear one of them say: “Let’s turn this on its head. We had Liz watch the fictional version of herself; let’s do the reverse with Amanda.” But we don’t know what truly happened. We do know that watching Amanda Seyfried and Elizabeth Holmes watch each other brings a bit of voyeuristic glee.

Years ago, I heard a story about a filmmaker who shot a brutal car crash scene based on a deeply traumatic incident a friend had told him about, without her permission. When the film premiered, he invited her to sit with him, without telling her what he’d done, and then, as her story was brought to life on screen, he just… stared at her, waiting to see her reaction. There’s something chilling about that. The sequences of Fielder showing The Dropout to Holmes (the Hulu series where Seyfried played Holmes), and his film to Seyfried, reminded me of that incident. To be clear, I’m not suggesting Fielder is that man. But the impulse feels related. It’s like, “I want to see what it looks like when you are faced with shame, pain, or heartbreak… I want to watch you as that happens inside of you.” And in the case of You Can See Everything: I then want to show the world what that looks like. It’s a voyeuristic impulse many have, and another way the film challenges the audience to confront the spectrum of acceptable versus verboten acts, and what it is to use others and present an image to the world for your own purposes.

(Here’s the thing: that story is hearsay. I can’t say with 100% certainty it’s true. But I told it, you believed it, and now that I’ve said it may not be, you don’t know what to believe. Welcome to You Can See Everything.)

Upon watching Holmes and Fielder’s time together unfold, Seyfried immediately slips into the fantasy of playing her again.

Seyfried is a wonderful actress and, by all accounts, a genuinely good human (she has a farm filled with rescue animals). She even cares enough about Holmes, without being a fan, to tell Fielder she hated to see the other woman in a toxic relationship with Evans. And yet she, too, has a stake in a particular interpretation of Holmes, winning an Emmy for her portrayal in The Dropout. This is not a condemnation of Seyfried, who wrestles with the idea that her project may have had some responsibility for negative outcomes in Holmes’ life. Yet upon watching Holmes and Fielder’s time together unfold, she immediately slips into the fantasy of playing her again.

In a brilliant bit of filmmaking that captures nearly all of the film’s themes at once, Seyfried reenacts a visit between Fielder and Holmes in prison, since he couldn’t film there. In it, Holmes claims that the guards and others treat her terribly because they believe the version of her that The Dropout presents is real. At one point, Fielder second-guesses his own memory of her wording, prompting Seyfried to wonder aloud if he’s being fully accurate or protecting his subject. Neither can be sure that “their” version of what happened is real. Notably, the exchange underscores how readily audiences believe the version of a person a film or series presents, until they’re shown another. Like this one.

The Many Faces of Liz

For large chunks of You Can See Everything, we are asked to explore the idea that Holmes’ image has been used: by Balwani and Holmes herself, by The Dropout, now by Fielder and Oppenheim, and most grotesquely by Evans. He reportedly wants to make an AI version of her available to the public, letting anyone put words in her mouth. He posts from her social media account, as her, apparently without her knowledge, including genuinely offensive comments that she, not he, will be judged for. Her face, her body, her voice: everyone is cherry-picking and using her image for their own ends. And we’re right there with them: the film spends its first stretch awkwardly making jokes at her expense, and most of the audience is laughing. The documentary has a thread about Evans wanting to benefit financially from the film, and ultimately the couple relinquished their stake. But the movie seems fine with everyone else benefiting from her image: it is making a great deal of money itself, and The Dropout before it earned acclaim and awards.

And all of these ideas around “using” her image are set against real violation: two relationships that read as abusive, one with Balwani, whom she testified abused her (he denies it), and one with Evans, at least as the film presents him. And, crucially, according to her father, there was also a sexual assault that changed her forever. This is a reference made quickly that the film never follows up on. Maybe that assault is what set her on this path of creating characters she felt safe hiding behind. Whether there is any sustaining “Liz” beneath the characters that she or others have crafted is unknowable. But the film seems to be saying: Holmes is many things, and among them, she is an object that has been used by others for years, and here we are, doing it again. To be clear, the intent isn’t to create false equivalencies. Holmes is the deepest narcissistic rabbit hole of them all; it’s just that, to lesser or greater degrees, everyone is using the same kind of selective truth and storytelling to get what they want.

There’s a moment in the film when a man involved in Holmes and Evans’ new venture (what some have dubbed Theranos 2.0) says, in essence, that all CEOs lie. The comment raises an interesting question: how different is Holmes, in terms of apparent sociopathic tendencies, from the lion’s share of tech CEOs? Elon Musk settled with the SEC over a “funding secured” tweet that a judge ruled was untrue and reckless, yet he was never criminally charged. Nikola founder Trevor Milton was convicted of lying to investors, then pardoned. Perhaps this is another question the film raises: when does a lie matter, and when doesn’t it?

In Holmes’ case, there is one distinction: Theranos deployed its machines, and real people got inaccurate medical results. The company voided two years of results from its Edison devices, and misdiagnosis caused patients real distress. Yet the film spends almost no time contextualizing what actually happened with Theranos, so Holmes’ victims are entirely unseen and unaccounted for. Perhaps that’s because Holmes was acquitted on the patient-fraud charges and convicted only of defrauding investors. Or perhaps because that piece of the story was inconvenient to the narrative. But the danger is that the film presents a far more sympathetic version of “Liz” than, say, The Dropout, and that neither version is fully true.

The Ultimate Magic Trick

At one point, Fielder talks about illusionist David Blaine, describing a trick in which the magician appears to shove an ice pick through his hand. But the reality is, Fielder says, it’s not an illusion; he is literally driving an ice pick through his hand. And the analogy that the whistleblower, Shultz, sees in this moment is that Holmes really is in an abusive relationship, and so you have empathy for her. But she’s doing the real thing, enduring actual pain, for the sake of the magic trick. From his point of view, she is a victim, but she is so by design. Shultz maintains that the woman he saw in that film is unrecognizable from the Elizabeth Holmes that he knew, who commanded any room. Perhaps Holmes really is an immaterial non-entity, a chameleon, twisting herself to the needs of the moment.

So, what is the “truth”? In reality, Oppenheim and Fielder likely know both that Holmes is a liar and that she knows she is one. But simply owning that isn’t much of a movie. Evans, for his part, knew he wasn’t inviting traditional documentarians into his world. Did he and Holmes think they could play players? Did it work? Or is the film playing us? Maybe it’s all of the above. You Can See Everything is manipulative, and it captures something essentially human. Those two things aren’t necessarily in conflict. This is a film about how we use each other, how we are used, the images we present to the world, and how, perhaps, no one ever really knows anyone, often including ourselves.

“You can see everything” is a promise Holmes makes to Fielder early in the documentary. But it also points to the filmmakers’ understanding of what they are doing here. At the end of the day, we don’t see, or know, anything definitively. We are left unmoored, possibly gaslit, as clinical psychologist Dr. Ramani Durvasula points out, feeling like victims of narcissistic abuse so often do.

And that’s the ultimate magic trick of the movie: putting the viewer in the position of being had by Holmes, and, perhaps, by the filmmakers themselves. In a sense, it weaponizes empathy. It’s as if it’s saying, if you don’t feel a little taken in, you may be on the more sociopathic end of the spectrum the film presents (least: Seyfried; most, as the film portrays them, a tie between Evans and Holmes). And if you do feel taken in, you are likely a sucker, ripe for the taking.

Quick bites from around the food safety arena

  • Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced the first update of infant formula nutrient requirements in 30 years. However, the announcement did not identify any new standards for infant formula, nor did it address dozens of cases of cronobacter and botulism that have affected babies since 2021. Although the FDA is accepting public comments on a new proposed rule, it has not yet made that rule available to the public.
  • Thirteen cases of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli may be linked to exposure at a pumpkin patch in California’s San Luis Obispo County. Brookshire Farms has closed while public health officials complete an investigation of the source of the infections. Meanwhile, the petting zoo at another pumpkin farm in Homer Glen, IL, is closed after five children were hospitalized with E. coli infections. All five had visited the farm before becoming ill.
  • The FDA has declared an outbreak of E coli associated with chicken wraps is over. Although the agency did not name Trader Joe’s as the source of the wraps, the USDA’s Food Safety and Inspection Service did confirm that connection to USA Today. The FDA has not completed its investigation as it continues to trace ingredients and inspect sites potentially involved in the outbreak.
  • A second Italian region has barred the serving of uncooked tomatoes after reports of Salmonella infections. Authorities in Emilia-Romagna ordered all public and private food service providers in schools, care facilities, hospitals and nursing homes to avoid the use of uncooked cherry tomatoes. A national task force has been convened to investigate multiple Salmonella outbreaks.
  • A researcher at Cornell University is developing a computerized model aimed at standardizing the way food safety risks are described and assessed during the “last mile” of the supply chain. Cornell University professor and principal investigator Martin Wiedmann built a QMRA, or quantitative microbial risk assessment, tool to improve identification of food safety risks. The tool puts physical damage or defects into the assessment along with other food safety risks. One goal of the research, Wiedmann says, is to get people “more committed to doing more standardized quality assessments.”

Futura 2 a Milano: non avrete altra pizza romana migliore di questa

Seconda nuova apertura a Milano nello stesso giorno: dopo Unica, la stessa sera inaugurazione anche per la (seconda) Futura Pizzeria Romana di Luca Pezzetta. Due punti di partenza diversi, ma coincidenti. 

Le due aperture concomitanti sono anch’esse una coincidenza. Sta di fatto che ho dovuto correre da Porta Romana, dove ha aperto Unica, a Porta Genova, dove c’è la seconda pizzeria Futura di Pezzetta.

Un aggiornamento sugli sviluppi dell’ondata di ristorazione romana che ha investito Milano. Ristoranti, trattorie e pizzerie romani ovunque, e sembrano tutti locali di successo – l’unico format che non sembra avere attecchito è quello della pinsa. I locali dedicati alla pinsa romana a Milano si contano sulle dita di una mano.

Cosa ho mangiato dalla nuova Futura 

La nuova pizzeria di Luca Pezzetta a Milano presenta lo stesso menù di quella in Isola. Con gli stessi diciamo antipasti, fritti e non, e le stesse pizze. 

Ho scelto due antipasti, che Luca ha fatto precedere da un assaggio del famoso, e ottimo, Pain Suisse al vitello tonnato (14 €). 

Io invece ho scelto un Tramezzino fritto (7 €), buono, idea originale la frittura, fatta peraltro bene. 

E uno sfogliato, il Croissant uovo e tartufo (14 euro: a occhio, diversi prezzi sono rimasti quelli di un anno fa). Molto molto buono, sia la parte croissant, smashata sulla piastra, che la parte uovo + tartufo (grattugiato a mano al tavolo). 

Due antipasti importanti, e impegnativi – ma ne vale la pena.

Agriturismo, ristorante, rivendita di prodotti, Ferdy Wild ha mandato da Pezzetta a Milano una selezione dei suoi formaggi, che Luca usa per reinterpretare le pizze. 

Su tutti, il Granùn 24 mesi, un formaggio a pasta dura cotta a grana. Notevole, su una semplice pizza con pomodoro olio evo e basilico. Anche se magari qualche pezzetto intero di Granùn sulla superficie avrebbe dato un tocco in più. Questa pizza si chiama Cosacca Ferdy e costa 18 euro.

Gli antipasti e i dolci

La pizzeria Futura di Luca Pezzetta a Milano si segnala anche per i suoi fritti, per gli sfogliati e i supplì – insomma la sezione antipasti. Che sono decisamente originali e memorabili, a partire da quelli che ho assaggiato.

Ci sono i supplì, preparati con riso Carnaroli, pasta secca di grano duro, carne e verdure da filiera controllata. Supplì al telefono (5 €), Cacio e pepe (7 €), all’ossobuco, in omaggio a Milano, Boscaiolo e Pulled pork, tutti a 9 €. 

Tra i fritti, oltre al tramezzino, segnaliamo la crocchetta di fassona (9 €) e il baccalà croccante (14 €).

Piatti signature di Pezzetta a Milano, il pain suisse e il croissant. Ma segnaliamo anche le polpette al sugo di nonna (12 €) e la Pizza “Pan con tomte e pata negra” a 28 euro.

Altrettanto originale è la carta dei dolci, che unisce all’iperclassico Tiramisù a 7 € proposte meno scontate come il Pain au chocolat con gelato allo zabaione (9 €), o la Focaccia al sale, gelato allo zabaione e cioccolato fondente (12 €).

C’è un menù dedicato al senza glutine, con una proposta numericamente ridotta.

Dove si trova Futura 2, nuova pizzeria di Luca Pezzetta a Milano

La seconda Futura si trova in via Gaudenzio Ferrari, ultima traversa di corso Genova, praticamente a due passi dalla zona dei Navigli. Pezzetta ha scelto una delle zone più frequentate di Milano, così come ha fatto un anno fa per la sua prima pizzeria milanese, aperta in Isola. Potete leggere la mia recensione in coda a questo articolo.

Locale più grande e mosso del primo, la pizzeria di Luca Pezzetta a Milano Porta Genova può contare su 72 posti a sedere. Suddivisi in varie aree: si entra e c’è un lungo bancone bar, con una dozzina di sgabelli, e una manciata di tavolini. A destra, una saletta, a sinistra, la cucina e un’altra sala più grande. La cucina è divisa in due aree separate fisicamente, la più grande affacciata su strada, l’altra destinata alle pizze senza glutine, per evitare contaminazioni. È una delle novità diciamo ambientali della nuova pizzeria di Pezzetta a Milano. L’altra è la sezione del cocktail bar, con un banco più ampio del bar di Futura in Isola. 

Tutto l’aspetto generale di questa seconda pizzeria Futura è diverso da quello scelto da Pezzetta per quella in Isola. L’ambiente è più movimentato, luci e colori richiamano un po’ quelli di una discoteca anni Ottanta. E c’è forse un po’ troppo rumore di fondo. Ma il tutto in chiave giovanile. 

Insomma, l’avventura imprenditoriale di Luca Pezzetta, partita da Fiumicino per arrivare a Milano, prosegue. Oltre all’annunciata terza apertura meneghina, prevista per l’inizio del prossimo anno in Montenapoleone, Pezzetta lo avete visto a Fiumicino con la nuova Casa Clementina.

Pezzetta a Milano. Come sono le pizze romane di Futura: ottime

9 novembre 2025 – di Emanuele Bonati

Nel mare magnum di nuove aperture di pizzerie con serata di inaugurazione, pizze gratis e file interminabili, Luca Pezzetta da Roma Fiumicino ha aperto la sua nuova pizzeria Futura a Milano con una serata normale. Nessuna inaugurazione ufficiale, niente code in paziente attesa di una fetta di pizza da mangiare all’impiedi. Solo la gente, tanta gente, e un locale pieno fino a tarda sera, in pratica un doppio turno.

Foto di Giulio Paternò.

E quindi eccomi al tavolo per la mia personale inaugurazione della pizzeria romana in Isola (siamo in via Borsieri) che, spoiler, darà filo da torcere alle pizzerie già aperte e a quelle di prossima apertura. A proposito, non dimenticatevi di Sasà Martucci, finalista del nostro Campionato in Campania e in procinto di aprire la sua nuova pizzeria in zona Solari.

La pizzeria Futura di Luca Pezzetta a Milano è bella. Non grandissima ma dagli spazi ben organizzati, la cucina all’ingresso, una quarantina di posti, il bancone del ba. E un dehors su Borsieri di una trentina di posti, che verranno probabilmente incrementati. ricoriamo che la via è una successione di bar locali ristoranti pizzerie (ci sono anche Sorbillo e Cpuano’s).

Ma passiamo al vero motivo per cui sono qui. Rispondere alla vostra domanda “come sono le pizze di Luca Pezzetta a Milano”. Ottime – e non solo le pizze.

Cosa mangiate da Luca Pezzetta a Milano

Supplì all’ossobuco come apertura. All’ossobuco di vitello, un immancabile e doveroso “omaggio” a Milano, panatura artigianale, e con una gremolata originale, molto verde (9 euro).

Pain Suisse al vitello tonnato: girello di vitellone, tonno, capperi, alici, origano, aceto di vino, brodo di verdure e maionese (12 €). Altra strizzatina d’occhio di Pezzetta a Milano con una farcitura nordica come il vitello tonnato in un ottimo sfogliato. Un unico appunto: non sentivo proprio i capperi in polvere. Aggiungerei qualche cappero vero.

Naturalmente, Pzzetta ha portato con sè a Milano anche la sua Marinara – pomodoro, origano fresco e secco, battuto di prezzemolo, aglio e basilico (8 €). Sottile sottile, la fetta regge, buonissimo l’insieme.

Il panettone di Luca Pezzetta per il Natale

Una delle preparazionio per cui è famoso Pezzetta è anche la sua abilità come panificatore lievitista. Che a Milano si traduci nel pane per le bruschette e nel panettone. purtroppo mi è toccata in sorte una fetta che al centro non era cotta perfettamente – ma l’assaggio è bastato a classificarlo fra i panettoni molto buoni di questa stagione 2025.

Quanto costano le pizze da Futura a Milano

La pizza Capricciosa di Mare. Foto di Slevin.

Luca Pezzetta apre la carta della sua pizzeria a Milano all’insegna della romanità. E non poteva essere altrimenti: supplì e un paio di fritti. Quelli al telefono – classici romani – vengono via a 3,50 euro.

Altro classico è la crocchetta di patate con provola, mortadella e arancia (7 euro).

Regina delle pizze – lo sappiamo – è la Margherita rigorosamente con il fiordilatte (8 euro). E c’è anche la declinazione con il prosciutto cotto (14 euro). Altro classico napoletano ma da Pezzetta a Milano in versione romanesca, la Cosacca con il Parmigiano 60 mesi (15 €).

Romanissima la pizza Porchetta e patate (16 euro) e “internazionale” la Prosciutto e funghi (16 euro).

La pizzeria Clementina (qui la recensione) di Luca Pezzetta è a Roma, rectius, a Fiumicino e dunque sul mare. E il mare arriva anche a Milano; impossibile non menzionare la Capricciosa di Mare (19 euro) e la pizza con i Gamberetti (20 euro)
