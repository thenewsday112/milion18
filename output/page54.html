A beautifully preserved fossil from Mongolia’s Gobi Desert has solved a mystery that puzzled paleontologists for nearly 40 years. Tamirkhan balcarceli shows that zhelestids were not early relatives of modern placental mammals, despite having teeth that seemed to suggest they were. Instead, the rabbit-like animal belonged to a separate lineage of small Cretaceous mammals.

Antecedentes y Objetivos: Los hongos secuestrados forman un grupo artificial caracterizado por su hábito hipógeo; se ubican en un grupo determinado de Ascomycota y Basidiomycota. Recientemente se erigió la familia Wynneaceae, que incluye los géneros Geodina y Wynnea. Este último se caracteriza por formar apotecios espatuliformes, auriculiformes, otidioides a esparasoides, con ascosporas elipsoides a naviculares con crestas longitudinales conspicuas. En México se han citado W. americana, W. gigantea y W. macrotis. Los análisis moleculares y morfológicos muestran que los especímenes constituyen una especie nueva hipogea, aquí denominada Wynnea hypogaea.
Métodos: Los ejemplares se recolectaron en 2022 en un bosque mesófilo de montaña del municipio de Coscomatepec, Veracruz, México. La descripción y estudios morfológicos se realizaron bajo los protocolos adecuados. Se realizaron los estudios moleculares con ITS, nrLSU, 18S, rpb2 y tef-1α para delimitar las especies. El material estudiado está depositado en el Herbario Micológico José Castillo Tovar del Instituto Tecnológico de Ciudad Victoria (ITCV) y Herbario de la Escuela Nacional de Ciencias Biológicas (ENCB) del Instituto Politécnico Nacional.
Resultados clave: Wynnea hypogaea se describe e ilustra. Por sus características morfológicas, moleculares y ecológicas, se distingue claramente de otros taxones del género. El taxón nuevo se caracteriza por los ascomas hipogeos con cámaras glebales irregulares, peridio marrón rojizo y ascosporas de 35-40 × 15-20(-22) µm, estriadas longitudinalmente. Crece bajo Quercus lancifolia en bosque mesófilo de montaña.
Conclusiones: Wynnea hypogaea es la primera especie del género con hábito hipogeo y sésil. Filogenéticamente es cercana a W. macrotis y W. sparassoides; no obstante, ambas forman un clado distinto del descrito. En algunos ascomicetos Pezizales, se puede observar la transición de epígeos a hipógeos. Por lo cual, la exploración y estudio de estos organismos deben continuar.

Yat gaw mein, also known as Baltimore yak or dirty yak, is Baltimore carryout comfort food at its finest. A Chinese American remix of Beijing’s zha jiang mian, the dish dates back to the late 19th century, when Chinese immigrants started settling in the city. Setting up shop in traditionally Black and working-class neighborhoods, Chinese cooks adapted their recipes to locally available ingredients, swapping fermented pastes for a simpler, soy-based gravy. Over time, something new emerged: savory wheat noodles, stir-fried with shrimp, beef, and vegetables and punctuated with a hard-boiled egg. Today you can find the beloved dish at counter spots around town like Jimmy’s Carryout, Danny’s, and Pimlico Chinese Carry Out.

In this at-home version from Steve Chu, the chef behind Baltimore’s Ekiben, smoky wok-charred noodles are tossed with umami-packed soy sauce and oyster sauce, rich fat, and sweet caramelized onions. What makes the dish “dirty” is the flavor that develops when soy sauce, sugar, and aromatics scorch against the hot metal of the wok, creating deep savory notes balanced by subtle sweetness. Be sure to keep the wok on high heat and stir frequently, since this allows the wok hei—which translates to “the breath of a wok” and refers to that elusive smokiness—to work its magic. The finishing squeeze of ketchup might sound unusual, but it delivers the acidic punch that makes the dish complete.

Makes: 2–4 Time: 25 minutes

Ingredients

  • Kosher salt
  • 1 lb. fresh wheat noodles, such as lo mein or udon
  • ¼ cup duck fat, chicken fat, vegetable oil, or other cooking fat
  • 1½ cups bean sprouts
  • 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
  • 1 medium scallion, thinly sliced
  • ¼ cup dark soy sauce, plus more if needed
  • 2 Tbsp. light soy sauce
  • 2 Tbsp. oyster sauce
  • 1 Tbsp. MSG or chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 Tbsp. sugar
  • 1–2 tsp. Tianjin chile flakes or crushed red chile flakes
  • Steamed or poached shrimp or medium-rare seared beef slices, for topping
  • 1 hard-boiled egg, peeled, for topping
  • Ketchup and hot sauce, for serving

Instructions

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the noodles and cook for 1 minute less than the package instructions so they remain slightly undercooked. Drain, then rinse briefly under cold water. Set aside to cool. 
  2. Heat a wok over high until lightly smoking. Add the duck fat, followed by the bean sprouts, onion, and scallion. Stir-fry until the mixture begins to brown around the edges, about 1 minute. Add the noodles, both soy sauces, the oyster sauce, MSG, sugar, and chile flakes. Stir-fry, tossing continuously so the sauce coats the noodles evenly and begins to brown against the wok, 1–4 minutes. (If a darker color is desired, add a little more dark soy sauce.)
  3. Add 1½ cups of water to the wok, scraping up the browned bits, and simmer until the noodles absorb some of the liquid, about 1 minute. (If you prefer a saucier dish, add a little more water.) Add the shrimp and hard-boiled egg, tossing gently so the egg warms through and takes on some of the dark soy color without breaking apart. 
  4. Serve immediately, with ketchup and hot sauce on the side.

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

Other single-subject cookbooks showcase pantry ­staples that are rarely in the spotlight. Anna Hezel’s Little Book of Olives: A Guide to Cooking, Eating, and Drinking for the Brine-Inclined presents recipes including cantaloupe and crushed-olive panzanella, Castelvetrano and pistachio pesto, and a whole chapter of olive desserts, plus shopping tips and a breakdown of varieties. The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Finally, some books exist more to dazzle than to inform, as is the case with pop artist Ed Ruscha and River Café chef Ruthie Rogers’s 2025 release Squeeze Me: Lemon Recipes & Art. In the pair’s high-gloss love letter to the lemon, pop art–coded photos with cheeky captions and a suite of minimalist recipes—such as lemon–­mascarpone tart and limoncello—make this ode to the simple citrus equally at home on a coffee table or the kitchen shelf.

While styles and forms vary, these books serve a ­common goal: to illuminate the sometimes surprising pleasures of a particular fruit or vegetable. That they manage to do so in such delightful fashion is proof that, once in a while, ­working with less really is more.

Part of what gives me so much joy about eating in Asheville is recognizing that we’re all part of a very integrated ecosystem. It feels like every restaurant I love is either run by an alum of one of the places I’ve been involved in, or by people that I’ve worked side by side with at hurricane-relief events, or by someone I know I’m going to see at the farmers market. 

That’s what makes Asheville special. The folks that come in from out of town are lucky that there’s a virtuous cycle of talent here to keep the pipeline full. It’s so cool to see the evolution of this place, even if we locals don’t always realize how good we have it.

It’s almost funny how not a big deal eating out here is. It’s so laid-back. We take it for granted that it’s going to be a great restaurant—of course it is! Having said that, the sheer range of restaurants is incredible. Given how small our town actually is, I sometimes find myself thinking, How lucky are we? 

A hallmark of most of the restaurants I love is their connection to nearby farms. At my last count, there were more than a dozen farmers markets happening in Asheville, and there are probably more than twice as many local farms within an hour of here. Most people know the farmers by name, and nobody is precious about it. So when you go to a restaurant, they’ll just say, “It’s Evan’s greens tonight,” and everybody knows who Evan is.

The last thing is that whether it’s an Indian restaurant or a Filipino restaurant or Italian or Spanish, I love the Southern throughline in all of the dishes and hospitality here. That doesn’t mean there’s cornbread or okra in every dish; it’s honoring the fact that we all chose to open Indian or Italian restaurants here in Appalachia, so let’s not divorce the two from each other.

Let’s find a way to make it feel holistic. We do that really well. I was worried after the pandemic and Hurricane Helene that Asheville might not bounce back, but we have. I know that this place is going to continue to trailblaze for years to come.

Laab (a.k.a larb) is one of the most iconic dishes of Thai cuisine, beloved by both Thais and people around the world. It's such a classic that laab was the second recipe I ever posted on Hot Thai Kitchen way back in 2009! It's a warm salad of ground meat, dressed with a bright, spicy lime dressing and loaded with fresh herbs like mint and cilantro. You can enjoy it as a meal with sticky rice and fresh veggies, or serve it alongside other Thai dishes. It is also one of the easiest Thai dishes you can make at home, perfect for any Thai food beginners!

[feast_advanced_jump_to]

Cultural Context: What is laab?

Let's get the most important thing out of the way first: while many Thai restaurants write this dish on the menu as larb, the correct pronunciation and the way it should be written is laab. The R is not pronounced. (If you speak British or Australian English, this does not concern you 😉.)

Laab ลาบ is a salad that is local to Laos and the northeast region of Thailand known as Isaan. But today, it is popular in all regions of Thailand. It is typically made of ground protein (can be pork, beef, chicken, or even fish) and lots of fresh herbs such as mint, cilantro, and green onions. It is a sour, salty, and spicy dish as the dressing is made of lime juice, fish sauce, and dried chili flakes. The most important ingredient in laab is toasted rice powder or kao kua, which gives laab its unique and identifying toasty aroma.

Because laab is mostly meat, it is served with a lot of raw vegetables such as cabbage, lettuce leaves, cucumber, and long beans. The starch it is traditionally served with is Thai sticky rice, though in a pinch, jasmine rice also works.

This recipe is for laab moo, which means laab with pork, and it is the most popular type of meat used for laab in Thailand. However, you can substitute another kind of ground meat, more details in the ingredients section below.

Ingredients and Important Notes

Here are all the ingredients for laab; please read important notes about them before you start. For amounts, check out the full recipe card below.

  • White jasmine rice or glutinous rice, uncooked. This is for making the most important ingredient in this whole thing: toasted rice powder or kao kua. Glutinous rice (sticky rice) is more commonly used since it is the staple rice in the Isaan region, but I prefer using jasmine rice because it is more fragrant. Choose either.
  • Makrut lime leaf (optional) for adding extra aromat to your toasted rice powder.
  • Ground pork. Lean ground pork is fine as long as it is not TOO lean. A little fat is ideal for keeping things moist, especially since there is no added fat in the dressing. You can sub ground chicken or turkey (use dark meat for best results), or ground beef. Here's my chicken laab (laab gai) recipe. For a vegan version, see my corn and tofu laab recipe here
  • Shallots, red onion will also work if shallots are not available. Red onions tend to be a little sweeter, so you may prefer that if you find raw shallots a little too pungent.
  • Fish sauce. See my post here about how to choose good fish sauce.
  • Lime juice. For best flavour use fresh lime juice for this recipe, do not use bottled lime juice.
  • Chili flakes or chili powder. Laab is supposed to be spicy so I recommend adding as much as you can! In Thailand we use roasted chili flakes which are a bit smokey, but whatever you have will work. If you don't eat spicy, do not omit the chilies or your laab will not taste right, as you will lose the chili flavour. Choose a mild chili powder, such as kashmiri chili powder, or the generic "red pepper flakes." If using red pepper flakes I recommend grinding it further in a spice grinder as the flakes are quite large and don't integrate well into dishes. If you'll be cooking Thai food regularly, I recommend making your own mild chili flakes, see "How to Make Mild Chili Flakes" below.
  • MSG or chicken bouillon powder (optional). Look, I never used to put MSG in my laab, and for years I felt like there was something different between the laab I made and the ones I get in Thailand. Turns out, it was the MSG. It not only adds umami but in this dish it also helps balance out the sharp acidity of the lime, creating a more well-rounded flavour. If you don't have MSG, you can add chicken bouillon powder (which contains MSG). If you don't want to do either, sugar can be a compromise - add a pinch of sugar to balance the acid, but do not add so much that the sweetness is detectable.
  • Mandatory herbs: Cilantro, green onions, and mint. It's not a complete laab without these. If you're a cilantro hater, I will make an exception for you to leave it out.
  • Optional herbs: makrut lime leaves, sawtooth coriander, lemongrass, dill. Add these if you like them, but no need to go out of your way to find them.
  • Sticky rice for serving. At the store, the rice is labelled as "glutinous rice" or "sweet rice." Make sure it is long grain and from Thailand. Here's a post with 7 different ways to make sticky rice, and my favourite "quick" method is this hot soak method. 
  • Fresh raw vegetables for serving. Laab is a strongly flavoured dish, and eating it alongside some fresh crunchy vegetables makes it more delicious. Some good options are green cabbage (preferably Asian style), cucumber, radishes, long beans, and romaine lettuce.

Step-By-Step

Here are all the steps to make this recipe. If this is your first time, I highly recommend watching the video tutorial to ensure success!

  1. Add about a third of the fish sauce to the ground pork and mash it to mix, and also to get rid of the "tube" shapes that ground meat comes in so your laab doesn't end up with weird meat tubes.
  1. Make the toasted rice powder. Place the rice and a makrut lime leaf (if using) in a dry pot or skillet and stir constantly over medium high heat until the rice is deeply browned. It will get a bit smokey so make sure you have good ventilation.
  1. If adding a makrut lime leaf, once it is dry and crisp, quickly remove it from the pan to prevent it from burning. This will likely happen before the rice is fully toasted. (I grab it quickly with my fingers, but to be safe, you should probably use tongs or chopsticks.)
  1. Transfer the rice and the lime leaf to a coffee grinder or mortar and pestle and pound into a powder. The crispy leaf will grind along with the rice, adding a citrusy aroma to the rice powder.
  1. This is the texture you're looking for - not as fine as flour, but like dry sand with no chunky pieces. If you're left with the center stem of the lime leaf at the end, discard it.
  1. Add 2 tablespoon of water to a pot and bring to a simmer over high heat. Add the ground pork and cook, breaking it up as you go, just until the pork is done. Turn off the heat.
  1. While the pork is hot, add the shallots and julienned lime leaves, and any other sturdy herbs you are using such as lemongrass. Stir and let the residual heat wilt the shallots and distribute the aroma.
  1. Add the seasonings: remaining fish sauce, lime juice, chili flakes, MSG (if using), and toasted rice powder. Stir to mix.
  1. Once the laab is no longer piping hot, or just before serving, add the delicate herbs: cilantro, green onions, mint and sawtooth coriander (if using).
  1. Serve with fresh veggies and a side of Thai sticky rice. Enjoy!

How to Make Mild Chili Flakes for Mild Laab

While you might be tempted to skip the chilies to make a mild laab, trust me when I say that your laab will not taste as good, because in addition to heat, chilies provide a key flavour that's important to the flavour profile of laab. While you can look for mild chili powder, you can also make your own which allows you to customize the heat level to your tolerance. If you want to cook Thai food regularly, I promise it'll come in handy! It keeps in the freezer indefinitely and in the fridge for a few months.

  1. Buy whole dried chilies whose spice level suits your tolerance. Latin American stores are best for this as they have lots of variety. Guajillo and California peppers have almost no heat. Puya peppers have a little kick but are still mild. You can also combine chilies, for example get guajillo/California and then spike it with a few chile de arbol (which are very hot) for a custom heat level.
    *As a guideline, for this laab recipe, you want to be able to add at least 1 teaspoon of chili flakes. If adding 1 teaspoon makes the dish too spicy, make it milder.
  2. Cut the dried chilies into chunks with scissors, and discard the seeds that fall out but no need to remove any seeds still inside. Toast them briefly over low heat in a dry skillet, stirring constantly, until they are crispy and smell slightly smokey.
  3. Remove from heat and grind into fine flakes using a coffee/spice grinder. Keep in the fridge or freezer as dried chilies can get mold overtime.

How to Eat Laab Like a Thai

A plate of laab arrives with a side of sticky rice and a bunch of raw vegetables...and if you wonder "how do I tackle this?" You're not alone! Let me explain.

Typically laab is served family style, meaning you're sharing the laab with a few people, you are not served a plate of laab that is your own to finish. You start by putting some of the sticky rice on your personal plate. Then scoop some of the laab and grab a few pieces of veg onto your own plate as well. Then you can:

  • Take a bite of laab (with a spoon) and follow it up with a bite of sticky rice (the sticky rice is eaten by hand), so both the rice AND the laab are in your mouth at the same time. You can do the same with laab + veg combo.
  • You can also use the vegetable as a "spoon" and put the laab on it, kind of like a lettuce wrap. This works well with cabbage, lettuce and cucumber slices.
  • If there is any dressing on the plate you can also use sticky rice to sop it up!

This is just typically how Thai people eat laab; don't feel like these are rules. You can eat it however you want!

Toasted Rice Powder (Kao Kua)

  • 2 Tablespoons jasmine rice or Thai glutinous rice
  • 1 makrut lime leaf (optional)

Laab

  • 9 oz (250 g) ground pork (or sub ground dark meat chicken)
  • 2 Tablespoons (30 ml) fish sauce, divided
  • 2 Tbsp (30 ml) water
  • ¼ cup (25 g) thinly julienned shallot
  • 2 makrut lime leaves, finely julienned (optional)
  • 2 Tablespoons (30 ml) lime juice
  • 1-2 teaspoons chili flakes, or to taste (see note 1)
  • ⅛ - ¼ teaspoon MSG (optional, see note 2)
  • ¼ cup (5 g) mint leaves, larger leaves roughly torn
  • 8-10 sprigs cilantro, roughly chopped
  • 1 small green onion, chopped
  • 2-3 leaves sawtooth coriander, thinly sliced (optional)
  • For serving: Thai sticky rice and crunch fresh vegetables e.g. green cabbage, cucumber, long beans, romaine lettuce, or radishes.

Instructions

  1. Toast the rice. In a medium size pot, add the raw rice and the optional makrut lime leaf and put it over high or medium high heat. Stir it constantly until a the rice is toasted until deeply browned. If using the lime leaf, pick it out of the pan as soon as it becomes dry and crispy, which will likely happen before your rice is toasted. Remove the rice from the pot immediately to prevent it from over-toasting from the residual heat and let cool slightly. You can keep the toasted rice in a sealed container in the fridge for up to a month.
  2. Grind the toasted rice. Transfer the rice and the lime leaf into either a mortar and pestle or a coffee grinder. Grind into a powder, but it need not to be flour-fine, just a sand-like texture without any chunky pieces is fine. The lime leaf will grind along with the rice, but if using a mortar and pestle, you will be left with the center stem of the leaf which you can discard.
  3. Make the laab: Add roughly a third of the fish sauce (2 teaspoons) into the pork and mash it with a fork to mix and get rid of the tube shapes ground meat often comes in.
  4. In the same pot you used to toast the rice, add the water and bring to a boil over high heat. Then add the ground pork and stir to break it up until it is fully cooked. Remove from the heat immediately, do not let it over cook.
  5. Using the pot as your mixing bowl, while still hot, add the shallots and the julienned lime leaves (if using), stir to mix and wilt the shallots, breaking up the layers of the shallots as much as you can.
  6. Add the remaining fish sauce, chili flakes, MSG, toasted rice powder, and the lime juice. Mix well, taste and adjust seasoning as desired, adding more chili flakes if you like it spicier.
  7. Just before serving, add the delicate herbs: cilantro, green onion, mint, sawtooth coriander (if using). Tip: Before you add the herbs, if the pork is still quite hot, let it cool for a couple of minutes so the herbs don’t wilt as much.
  8. Serve warm or room temp with sticky rice and fresh crunchy vegetables.

Notes

1. Laab is supposed to be spicy, so add as much as you can tolerate! If you don't eat very spicy, don't omit the chilies altogether because you'll be missing the chili flavour. Instead opt for milder chili flakes such as the "red pepper flakes" sold in Western grocery stores. More on this in the ingredient section in the blog post.

2. Most laab in Thailand contains MSG; it not only adds umami but also helps balance out the acidity of the lime. You can sub ¼ teaspoon of chicken bouillon powder which contains MSG. Otherwise adding a pinch of sugar can also help balance the acid, but it won't taste as good.
