Llangollen Railway plans Remembrance Sunday Poppy Drop and free travel for veterans

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway will mark Remembrance Sunday on 8 November 2026 with a special Poppy Drop led by two Royal Navy veterans, the temporary renaming of steam locomotive No. 3802 and free afternoon travel for Armed Forces personnel, veterans and cadets.

Mandy Towers lays the wreath on behalf of Llangollen and Corwen Railway

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway has announced a programme of commemorations for Remembrance Sunday, including two symbolic journeys through the Dee Valley, station tributes, wreath-laying ceremonies and a railway-wide act of remembrance.

Royal Navy veteran Garry Smith MBE and former Women’s Royal Naval Service (WRNS) member Ruth Smith will lead a special Poppy Drop, carrying poppies on separate train journeys between Llangollen and Corwen.

For the occasion, Great Western Railway heavy freight steam locomotive No. 3802 will be temporarily renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for the day.

The railway will also offer free travel on its afternoon services to serving Armed Forces personnel, veterans and members of recognised cadet organisations.

Remembrance Sunday services at Llangollen and Corwen

The railway’s usual 10am departure from Llangollen will be cancelled to allow staff and volunteers to attend Remembrance Sunday services and parades in their own communities.

At 11am, all activity at Llangollen Station will stop for the national Two-Minute Silence, observed by staff, volunteers and visitors.

Garry Smith MBE and Ruth Smith will represent the railway at the Remembrance Service at Llangollen War Memorial on Centenary Square, laying a wreath on its behalf.

At Corwen, Wayne Ronneback and the Corwen Station Team will represent the railway at the town’s Remembrance Service.

Mandy Towers was joined by Llangollen Railway Trust Manager David Hennigan with volunteers Mandy Towers, Glyn Jones and Mike Jackson The GWR Heavy Freight 3802, will be renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for the day. Mandy Towers lays the wreath on behalf of Llangollen and Corwen Railway Garry Smith MBE and his wife Ruth Smith will be leading Remembrance Day commerations in Llangollen.

Veterans to lead special Poppy Drop through the Dee Valley

Following the morning commemorations, Garry Smith MBE will travel on the footplate of the 12.20pm service from Llangollen to Corwen, releasing poppies on the approaches to stations along the route.

At Corwen, Ruth Smith will take over the tribute, travelling on the footplate of the 1.45pm return service to Llangollen and continuing the Poppy Drop.

The railway says the tribute will be planned and carried out in accordance with its operational and safety requirements.

Garry Smith MBE, a Royal Navy veteran, said:

“Remembrance Sunday is a deeply important occasion for all of us who have served in the Armed Forces. It is a time to remember our friends, our comrades and the generations of men and women who made the ultimate sacrifice.

“To have the honour of laying a wreath at Llangollen and then travelling along our beautiful railway, releasing poppies in remembrance, will be incredibly moving.

“Every poppy represents a life, a sacrifice and a family whose world was changed forever. As we travel through the Dee Valley, we will be remembering those who never came home.

“I am immensely proud to be part of this tribute and to represent the Llangollen and Corwen Railway.”

Ruth Smith, a former member of the Women’s Royal Naval Service, said:

“Remembrance is about more than a ceremony. It is about ensuring that the sacrifices made by previous generations are never forgotten.

“There is something especially poignant about Garry taking the poppies from Llangollen to Corwen and me bringing the tribute back along the line.

“Two journeys, travelling through the communities that our Railway serves, united by one message of remembrance.

“As a former Wren, it is a real privilege to take part in this special occasion. I hope everyone who sees the trains passing will take a moment to reflect on those who served, those who suffered and those who gave their lives for our freedom.”

GWR locomotive No. 3802 to carry ‘The Remembrance’ name

The railway’s Great Western Railway heavy freight steam locomotive No. 3802 will be temporarily renamed ‘The Remembrance’ for Remembrance Sunday.

The name will be carried for the day as a tribute to the service and sacrifice of generations of Armed Forces personnel.

Remembrance displays at Glyndyfrdwy and along the railway

Volunteers at Glyndyfrdwy Railway Station have prepared a Remembrance display featuring two silhouettes representing members of the Armed Forces, accompanied by flags.

Flags and poppies will also be displayed at every station along the Llangollen and Corwen Railway.

The railway hopes these displays will encourage passengers, visitors and local residents to reflect on the sacrifices made by servicemen and women.

Free train travel for Armed Forces personnel, veterans and cadets

The railway will offer free travel on all its Remembrance Sunday afternoon services to serving Armed Forces personnel, veterans and members of recognised cadet organisations.

The offer includes personnel serving in the Royal Navy, British Army and Royal Air Force, former service personnel, and members of the Sea Cadets, Army Cadets and RAF Air Cadets, including the Air Training Corps.

Those wishing to travel free of charge must present appropriate proof of service or cadet membership at one of the railway’s ticket offices.

Accepted identification includes a military identification card, Veterans Railcard, Veterans ID Card or recognised cadet identification.

The complimentary travel offer is intended as a gesture of thanks to serving personnel, veterans and young people involved in cadet organisations.

Remembrance Sunday train timetable – 8 November 2026

The railway has confirmed the following afternoon services:

Llangollen to Corwen:

  • 12.20pm
  • 3.15pm

Corwen to Llangollen:

  • 1.45pm
  • 4.20pm

There will be no 10am departure from Llangollen, allowing railway staff and volunteers to attend local Remembrance Sunday commemorations.

The commemorations will bring together railway staff, volunteers, veterans, cadets and the communities of Llangollen and Corwen.

Through the wreath-laying ceremonies, Two-Minute Silence, special station displays, the temporary renaming of locomotive No. 3802 and the Poppy Drop journeys led by Garry and Ruth Smith, the railway aims to provide opportunities for remembrance along the heritage line.

The Llangollen and Corwen Railway hopes passengers, visitors and local residents will join in remembering those who made the ultimate sacrifice.

This morning, Ford revealed that buyers of the forthcoming Fathom—the company's sub-$30,000 electric pickup truck due next year—will get more than just a truck. Until now, vehicle-to-home technology—like the setup Ford developed for the now-discontinued F-150 Lightning—takes DC power from the electric vehicle and runs it through an inverter built into the wallbox to turn it into AC the house can use. But with the Fathom, that inverter is built into the truck from the start. So every Fathom will be able to act as a backup battery for your home.

It’s typical: You wait a decade for a cheap electric truck, and then two come along almost at once. And they’re two radically different products from two radically different automakers that I think will reveal a few preferences from the EV-buying public. One is the Blank Slate, from a brand new startup offering a bare-bones two-door that capitalizes on features the Internet says it wants, like no onboard modem, no touchscreens, and no infotainment system.

The other is the result of one of Detroit’s big three taking a long, hard look at Chinese EVs and asking itself, “How do we make a car that cheap?” Although the Ford Fathom will cost $5,000 more than a Slate, that money does buy an extra pair of doors, modern conveniences like an infotainment system, CarPlay, electric windows, and a vast dealership network to service the car. That may be helpful given how many recalls Ford issues each year.

Often overlooked, seeds are an important life stage in the growth of boreal forests. Before a tree is capable of photosynthesis, the germinating seed supports plant growth using stored reserves of carbon, starch, nitrogen, and phosphorus. But higher latitudes are known to adversely affect the deposition of these nutrients within the seed.

This is because higher latitudes have lower nitrogen and phosphorus availability, are colder, have a shorter growing season, and have an increased incidence of fire. And these limitations negatively impact seed production and germination success.

Now, you might think this negative is a negative. But a new study on the reproductive traits of black spruce (Picea mariana) across a latitudinal gradient has shown that smaller seeds and poor germination likely confer special advantages to reproduction in this species.

"In this study, we investigated the variation in cone and seed traits of black spruces growing in five natural sites along a latitudinal gradient from 48°N to 53°N in Quebec, Canada," write Karki and colleagues. Black spruce is a dominant conifer species in North American boreal forests.

This map shows where sample cones were collected from the five black spruce populations studied. SIM, Simoncouche; BER, Bernatchez; MIS, Mistassibi; DAN, Camp Daniel; MIR, Mirage. Figure 1 of Karki et al CC BY

For their study, Karki and colleagues collected 90 fully mature brown cones from each of 10-13 adult black spruce trees growing in the five independent test sites. The researchers then studied cone and seed size, seed number, nutrient composition, and germination rates, comparing the data between latitudes.

They found a clear trend along the latitudinal gradient. Black spruce trees in more southern latitudes have larger seeds, heavier seeds, more seeds per cone, wider cones, and a higher sucrose but lower starch content than more northern populations.

Black Spruce (Picea mariana) growing in Charlevoix-Est, Québec, Canada. The cones and seeds get smaller the farther north they grow. Image Credit: R. LLM via iNaturalist CC BY-NC 4.0

Karki and colleagues concluded from these data that the heavier seeds produced in southern areas are packed with nutrients that allow them to grow well in place, while lighter seeds of northern areas can be dispersed by the wind to new locations. Both confer advantages to the seed.

That's because these differences affect germination. Where its warmer, in the southern latitudes, the seed can drop, full of nutrients, and establish a new tree. But in higher latitudes, fewer nutrients in the seeds leads to lower, and delayed, germination, which may sound bad, but is really an advantage. The delay prevents seeds from germinating when there's still a risk for frost, helping to ensure the survival of the next generation. Fewer nutrients also result in a light seed that can be carried by the wind to new, favourable growing locations.

Northern black spruce cones also appear to be better suited to fire ecology; the cones need more heat to open than their southern relations. This "could reflect an adaptation to the shorter fire intervals occurring at higher latitudes" write Karki and colleagues. This is important because climate change is predicted to shorten the fire intervals in Eastern Canada. And so having cones that can withstand fire and protect their seeds helps mitigate risks to the next generation.

Together, these results paint a hopeful picture. Black spruce may have built-in resiliency along the latitudinal gradient that allows the boreal forest to adapt to changing conditions over time.

READ THE ARTICLE: Karki, D., Silvestro, R., Deslauriers, A., Mslati, M., Périé, C., Boucher, Y., and Rossi, S. (2026) Moving-or-staying dilemma: reproductive traits reveal a latitudinal trade-off in black spruce. Annals of Botany. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcag288.

A creamy and delicious tuna dip topped with briny olives, capers and marinated peppers. Full of umami-rich flavours and ready in under 10 minutes. Serve with fresh vegetables and crackers for the easiest appetiser.

Unlike most tuna dips, this one is made with no cream cheese or sour cream. It’s inspired by one of my favourite Italian dishes, Vitello Tonnato, and is bursting with umami-rich flavours.

Tuna, mayo, anchovies and capers are blitzed into an incredible, rich dip and topped with marinated peppers, salty olives and briny capers.

I love to serve it with bitter endive or chicory leaves and fresh vegetables to cut through the richness. Crackers work really well too.

It’s such a great party appetiser and takes less than 10 minutes to prep. Trust me, this dip won’t last long!

Ingredient notes

  • Tuna in olive oil: Just like Vitello Tonnato, the quality of the tuna makes a big difference here, especially texture-wise. I recommend using tuna canned in olive oil rather than water or brine, as it has a richer flavour and gives the dip a smooth texture.
  • Anchovies: Even if you’re not an anchovy fan, these really add a lot of umami flavour to the dip, so don’t skip them.
  • Capers: I always use capers jarred in brine. They add a delicious pickly flavour.
  • Mayonnaise: Use a good-quality mayo if possible.
  • Peppers: I like to use jarred, marinated peppers (they’re also perfect for sandwiches).
  • Olives: Black or green olives work well. I chose black just for a contrast of colours.

What to serve with it

I love serving this dip with endive or chicory leaves. They act as perfect serving-sized cups for scooping up the dip, and the contrast of bitter and salty flavours works perfectly. See more serving suggestions below.

  • Bell peppers: thinly sliced sticks of fresh pepper or use sweet mini peppers cut in half; they’re perfect for dipping.
  • Carrots or cucumber: these are also perfect as they can be cut into sticks for easy dipping.
  • Breadsticks or crackers: I tend to prefer crackers so it’s easy to scoop up the topping, but breadsticks or grissini are great for dipping too.

Recipe tips

Drain the tuna: Make sure to drain the tuna before adding to your food processor or the dip will be too oily.

Additions for freshness: Try adding some freshly chopped parsley or chives for a little freshness and contrast.

You shouldn’t need salt: There should be enough seasoning in each ingredient, so there’s no need for added salt here; however, if you feel it needs it, you can add it to taste.

Prepping ahead: Prep the dip ahead of time and keep it in the fridge until you are ready to serve it. I recommend chopping the toppings in advance but only add them to the dip when serving.

Before serving: If you prep this ahead of time and store it in the fridge, make sure to remove it from the fridge around 15-20 minutes before serving so it’s not too cold.

Easy Tuna Dip with Peppers, Capers and Olives (Tonnato Style)

A creamy, umami tuna dip topped with capers, peppers and olives and served with fresh vegetables and crackers. Ready in under 10 minutes. Course Appetizer Cuisine Modern Italian Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Total Time 10 minutes minutes Servings 6 – 8 servings Calories 141kcal Author Emily Wyper

Equipment

  • Food processor

Ingredients

For the dip

  • 160 g (5.6oz) tuna canned in olive oil
  • 90 g (1/2 cup) mayonnaise
  • 2 anchovy fillets
  • ½ tablespoon capers in brine

For the topping

  • 1 marinated red pepper finely chopped
  • 8 black or green olives finely chopped
  • 1 teaspoon capers in brine

For serving

  • Mini peppers cut in half with seeds removed
  • Chicory or endive leaves
  • Crackers

Instructions

  • Wash, pat dry and prep all your veg for serving, and set aside.
  • Drain the tuna then put in it a food processor with all the other dip ingredients (capers, anchovies and mayo). Blitz for a few seconds until smooth. Transfer the dip to a serving bowl.
  • Finely chop the marinated pepper and black olives and sprinkle them on top of the dip then add the capers on top.
  • Transfer the bowl to a serving platter and arrange the vegetables and crackers around it. Serve.

Notes

  • Storage: The dip will last well for up to 2 days in the fridge.
  • Prepare in advance: Prep the dip in advance and store it in the fridge. Prepare the topping ingredients and keep them in the fridge separately. Add them to the dip just before serving.

What the Winter Arc Means for GLP-1 Users

That idea can be useful. The end of the year can feel busy and unpredictable. Travel, holiday meals, colder weather, and shorter days can make routines harder to keep.

“A seasonal reset can help you decide what matters before January arrives. But there is a catch: “locking in” can easily turn into all-or-nothing thinking,” says Katherine Basbaum MS RD, MyFitnessPal dietitian.

For GLP-1 users, that can be especially tricky. Some days may feel different than others because of appetite, digestion, energy, or medication timing — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, stomach pain, fatigue, and dizziness are all things people commonly deal with. (2,6)

That does not mean you cannot build momentum. It just means your Winter Arc should have guardrails.

Think of it this way: your Winter Arc is not a punishment plan. It is a support plan.

How To Lock In Without Going Extreme

A GLP-1 Winter Arc works best when it is simple enough to repeat.

Instead of choosing 10 habits and trying to perfect all of them, choose a few habits that support your bigger goal. The best winter habits are the ones you can still do on a cold Tuesday, after a busy workday, or during a holiday week.

Start with the minimum version

A minimum version is the low-pressure version of a habit. It might be shorter, simpler, or less detailed than your ideal plan, but it still keeps you connected to the habit. This helps you keep momentum without turning one imperfect day into a reason to stop.

Build in flexible days

Some days may not be ideal for a hard workout or three balanced meals. That does not mean your plan is broken.

If you feel unusually tired, nauseated, dizzy, or under-fueled, choose the gentler version. Take a walk. Stretch. Eat one good meal and a protein-rich snack or two if that feels manageable. 

Habit 1: Make Strength Training Your Anchor

Strength training on GLP-1 does not need to mean intense gym workouts. A good rule of thumb is to work in strength training at least twice a week, hitting the major muscle groups — legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms.  (3)

This fits the Winter Arc well because strength training gives you a clear habit to repeat. You can do it at a gym or at home with simple equipment like resistance bands or dumbbells.

A beginner-friendly session might include:

  • Chair squats or sit-to-stands
  • Wall push-ups or incline push-ups
  • Resistance band rows
  • Glute bridges
  • Step-ups
  • Light carries with dumbbells or grocery bags

Start with one to three sets of each move. Keep the effort moderate. You should finish feeling like you could come back and do it again later in the week. You can connect with a personal trainer for a personalized routine.

This is where the muscle-loss conversation naturally comes in, but it’s not the whole story. “Choosing strength training as one of your Winter Arc goals will definitely help towards maintaining and building muscle tissue, but that’s not the only benefit,” says Basbaum. “You’ll also see gains in your functional fitness, which means lower risk of injury and improvement in quality of life.” 

Still, muscle matters. It supports strength, balance, daily movement, and feeling capable in your body.

One recent review looked at people using GLP-1 medications at weight-loss doses and found they did lose some muscle overall, but they lost proportionally more fat than muscle, which is actually a good sign for body composition. The researchers still point to nutrition and exercise as the best ways to protect muscle along the way.  (4)

The takeaway is simple: do not panic about muscle loss GLP-1 headlines, but do give your body the tools to stay strong.

Habit 2: Keep Protein Consistent

Protein intake on GLP-1 can take some planning because your meals may feel different than they used to.

Some days, a full meal may feel easy. Other days, smaller meals or snacks may feel more realistic. That is where protein anchors can help.

A protein anchor is a food or meal you can repeat often. Examples include:

  • Greek yogurt or cottage cheese at breakfast
  • Eggs, tofu, or beans in a quick lunch
  • Chicken, fish, turkey, lentils, or tempeh at dinner
  • A smoothie with protein if solid food feels hard
  • Edamame, tuna, hummus, or cheese as a snack

Getting enough protein and doing some strength training work together; one helps your body make better use of the other to build and maintain muscle. (5)

“It’s also important to note that not everyone needs the same protein target. Your needs can vary based on body size, activity level, age, and training goals,” Basbaum says. (5) 

A practical Winter Arc goal could be: “I will include protein at two meals most days.”

That is specific. It is flexible. And it is much more useful than trying to overhaul every meal at once.

Habit 3: Keep Movement Easy To Repeat

Strength training can be your anchor, but it does not have to be your only movement. Walking, cycling, dancing, swimming, yoga, or indoor treadmill time can all be part of an exercise and GLP-1 routine. 

A useful target: about 150 minutes of moderate movement a week, plus two strength sessions.  But let’s be honest, that can sound like a lot, especially in winter. So make it smaller. (3)

Try:

  • A 10-minute walk after lunch
  • A short indoor walk while listening to a podcast
  • One strength session during the week and one on the weekend
  • A stretch routine on side-effect days
  • A quick walk before the sun goes down

Your GLP-1 exercise program does not need to be perfect. It just needs to be repeatable.

Habit 4: Track What Helps, Not Everything Perfectly

Tracking can be a helpful tool on a GLP-1 journey, but it should not become another extreme rule.

The point is not to prove you had a perfect day. The point is to notice patterns.

For example, tracking can help you see:

  • Whether you are getting protein regularly
  • Which meals feel best before or after workouts
  • Whether symptoms line up with certain foods or medication timing
  • How often you are strength training
  • Where your routine tends to slip during busy weeks

Try prompts like:

  • “How am I doing on protein today?”
  • “What is an easy dinner if I am not very hungry?”
  • “What can I eat before a light strength workout?”
  • “What is one simple habit I can focus on this week?”

That is the better version of “lock in”: use your own patterns to make the next choice easier.

A Simple 4-Week GLP-1 Winter Arc Plan

You do not need to plan every day from October through December. Start with four weeks.

Week 1: Choose your three habits

Pick one habit for movement, one for protein, and one for tracking.

For example:

  • Movement: two short strength sessions
  • Protein: protein at breakfast
  • Tracking: log meals and GLP-1 dose on dose day

Keep it simple enough that you can do it during a busy week.

Week 2: Make protein easier

Choose two or three protein anchors you can repeat.

This could be Greek yogurt, eggs, a ready-to-drink protein shake, tofu, rotisserie chicken, tuna packets, lentils, or cottage cheese.

The goal is not to eat the same thing every day forever. It is to lower the number of decisions you have to make.

Week 3: Build your strength rhythm

This week, focus on making your strength routine feel familiar. Keep the moves simple, repeat the basics, and notice what feels doable.

If your energy is lower, use the lighter version. If you feel good, stick with the same routine and finish with confidence. 

“Exercise and GLP-1 habits do not need to be extreme to be useful. Consistency is the win here,” Basbaum notes.

Week 4: Holiday-proof the plan

This is where your Winter Arc becomes realistic.

Make a backup plan for cold weather, travel, parties, or low-energy days. Save a few easy high-protein meals. Keep resistance bands nearby. Pick an indoor walking option. Decide what “good enough” looks like during a holiday week.

A good plan should bend. If it breaks every time life happens, it is too rigid.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

  • Is the Winter Arc a good idea if you are on a GLP-1?

It can be, as long as you use it as a flexible habit-building period instead of an extreme challenge. The Winter Arc is generally framed as an October-through-December period of focus before the new year, but GLP-1 users may need to adjust routines around appetite, digestion, energy, side effects, and their prescriber’s guidance. (1, 2, 6)

  • How can you help prevent muscle loss on GLP-1?

You may not be able to prevent every change in muscle during weight loss. But you can support it by strength training, eating enough protein, and avoiding overly restrictive eating. Research on GLP-1 medications backs up pairing treatment with good nutrition and regular movement. (3,  4,  5)

  • What is a good GLP-1 exercise program for beginners?

A beginner-friendly GLP-1 exercise program might include two days of full-body strength training and a few short walks each week — roughly two strength sessions plus about 150 minutes of moderate movement is a solid target.  (3)

  • How should you approach protein intake on GLP-1?

Protein intake on GLP-1 should be realistic and personalized. A good starting habit is to include protein at meals or snacks throughout the day, especially if your usual intake feels lower than before. Protein and strength training work together — getting enough of one helps your body make better use of the other.  (5)

  • How can you stay consistent during the Winter Arc?

Staying consistent is easier when you track the habits that help you make better choices. That might mean logging meals, protein, movement, symptoms, or medication timing.

Bottom Line

The Winter Arc does not need to be intense to be useful.

For GLP-1 users, the best version is not about restriction. It is about choosing a few habits that help you feel supported: strength training, protein consistency, realistic movement, and tracking that helps you notice patterns.

Lock in gently. Keep the habits small enough to repeat. Let muscle support be one goal, not the whole story.
