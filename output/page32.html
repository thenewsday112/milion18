Two closely related plant species can have very different reproductive strategies. One might pollinate itself; while the other needs pollen arriving from another flower. That small difference can have strong consequences for how species diverge and remain separate. A new study recently published in AoB Plants explores how differences in pollination help keep two Erysimum species apart. 

For one species to become two, its populations have to slowly stop interbreeding. This is known as reproductive isolation, and it can happen in several ways. Plants may grow in different places, flower at different times, attract different pollinators or fail to produce healthy offspring when they cross. 

But these barriers don’t operate independently. Researchers have often studied these barriers one at a time, leaving a key question: how do they work together to keep species from interbreeding? 

Ana García-Muñoz and colleagues studied two closely related species: Erysimum incanum, which mostly pollinates itself, and Erysimum wilczekianum, which needs pollen from other plants. They checked every step of reproduction to find out what stops the two from interbreeding. 

The results reveal an interesting degree of reproductive isolation. When all barriers were considered together, the researchers calculated a total isolation value of 0.999, indicating almost complete separation. That separation doesn’t come from one big obstacle. It comes mainly from barriers that act before pollen reaches a flower. 

Pollinator behaviour seems to be particularly important. Pollinators clearly preferred E. wilczekianum, so pollen rarely made it from one species to the other. If insects keep visiting the same kind of flower, the chances of cross-fertilisation decline. 

The two species differ in floral traits, particularly flower size, which may influence which flowers pollinators visit and, ultimately, where they carry pollen. In Erysimum, flower size seems to be a characteristic that evolves in response to a species’ way of life and, at the same time, reduces mating with other populations.  

The barriers acting after pollination are more complex. When the researchers examined crosses between the species, they found an important difference in the results. Hybrid seed formation was largely prevented when E. wilczekianum was the maternal parent, whereas crosses in the opposite direction were comparatively successful. Although fewer hybrid seeds germinated, those that survived grew particularly well. The researchers also found no evidence that later generations of hybrids become less healthy or less successful at reproducing.  

Taken together, our results contribute to a broader understanding of how mating system divergence can shape the architecture of reproductive isolation in plants. Rather than acting as a single barrier, mating system differences appear to reorganize multiple components of isolation simultaneously, from pollinator-mediated processes to post-pollination compatibility.

These findings suggest that the two species are still capable of producing viable offspring. Rather than being unable to cross, they are kept apart mainly by differences in their environment and the pollinators they attract.

Ubisoft has released a fall update on the Assassin’s Creed franchise, providing news big and small on its flagship series. The news includes updates for existing games and smaller but significant insights on the various upcoming Assassin’s Creed games previously announced.

Assassin's Creed Valhalla

In terms of current games, Assassin’s Creed Valhalla is getting a long-requested New Game Plus thanks to a new update coming on November 5. This means you can start a fresh game while bringing over your progression items/level from your previous playthrough. This update will also introduce the following features:

  • An optional progression mode where Gear, Runes, and Masteries all contribute to Eivor's overall Power
  • New runes, achievements, a stash system, and new rewards, including a Master Assassin outfit inspired by Naoe's look in Assassin's Creed Shadows
  • Mithril, a new resource to upgrade Divine Gear and strengthen runes
  • Divine Gear quality for all items

This news comes a few days after New Game Plus arrived in Assassin's Creed Black Flag Resynced. 

Assassin's Creed Codename Invictis

Codname Invictis, a PvP multiplayer game, remains in development as Ubisoft shares it has incorporated feedback from players who, according to Ubisoft, “saw great potential in the foundations but wanted more of the pillars that define Assassin's Creed.” To this end, Ubisoft has spent months reworking Invictis to reflect this vague feedback and say the game is “now evolving in an exciting direction that better reflects those pillars while staying true to its multiplayer ambitions.”

Assassin's Creed Codename Hexe

As for the next single-player Assassin’s Creed, Codename Hexe, Ubisoft says it will share the next major update on this game in 2027. First announced in 2022, Codename Hexe is set during the 17th-century reign of the Holy Roman Empire, and the era's witch hunts will play a significant role.

Future Assassin's Creed Remakes 

With the release of Assassin’s Creed Black Flag Resynced earlier this year, Ubisoft has given fans what they'll likely see as a disappointing update on potential future remakes: “One thing we want to be transparent about today is that we currently have no other Assassin's Creed remake actively in development. Our teams are already focused on the next chapter of Assassin's Creed titles, including projects you haven't heard about yet, featuring new settings and stories.”

However, Ubisoft adds that this door isn’t closed for good. “Like many of you, we love the idea of revisiting our classic titles, and we'll keep exploring opportunities to bring older titles back,” says Ubisoft.

Assassin's Creed Netflix Series

Ubisoft’s next update concerns the live-action Assassin's Creed series in production for Netflix. They say the show is “nearing completion” in Rome and that award-winning composer Hannah Peel (Insomnia, Rogue Agent, Game of Thrones: The Last Watch) is composing the score. 

Lastly, Ubisoft teases big celebrations are planned for 2027, which marks the 20th anniversary of the Assassin's Creed franchise. It's keeping details to itself for now, but states it's "preparing a year of reveals, updates, rewards, and community moments. "

For more on Assassin's Creed, check out our review of Assassin's Creed Black Flag Resynced.

Full spoilers follow for South Park Season 29, Episode 4!

This season of South Park America started off well enough, but there's been a real struggle to build any real momentum over the long haul. It often feels like the writers aren't sure whether to fully commit to the idea of doing more classical standalone episodes this year or try to build an overarching narrative. And so we're left with a season that constantly reminds us that "November is coming," without anything more insightful to add than the constant allusions to Grand Theft Auto 6.

To its credit, Episode 4 tries to build on the foundation of the previous chapters and establish a more coherent conflict for the embattled South America. This time around, rising beef prices and high demand have robbed South America Elementary of its precious sloppy joes. It falls on Stan, Kyle, Cartman, and yes, Little Randy, to save the day and find a solution to the town's woes.

I will say, though, that I got a big kick out of the heated steakhouse rivalry and the conflict between Ruth's Chris, Sizzler, and Texas Roadhouse. Maybe it would have been better if the series waited a little longer before coming back.

What South Park does have right now, though, is Little Randy. I was hoping that particular twist from Episode 3 would carry over, and boy does it. Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

Make a 15-Minute Fresh Cranberry Sauce with 3 Ingredients

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Our easy three-ingredient cranberry sauce recipe (shown above) has become my go-to.

Only need a small batch of cranberry sauce? Put the rest of those fresh cranberries to good use and make my sugared cranberries. They’re a delicious, festive, budget-friendly treat that doubles as a gorgeous garnish for your turkey platter and holiday desserts.

But when we checked Walmart again on 10/26/26, they were finally in stock for just $0.76 a bag! That’s cheaper than a can of cranberry sauce AND a whopping $2.23 less than we paid at Kroger for the same size bag. Proof that a little comparison shopping can make a big difference to your holiday grocery bill.

Swap Out the Green Bean Casserole

Traditional green bean casserole often relies on canned french-cut green beans and canned cream soups…a double hit of packaging markups! Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Elevate the Bread Basket

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Bake a quick batch of warm homemade dinner rolls or a skillet of golden cornbread, and serve it alongside whipped domestic sweet cream butter drizzled with local honey and sea salt. They cost pennies per serving, actually fill people up, and feel deeply comforting. Short on time? Don’t feel bad about grabbing a bag of ready-made dinner rolls at the grocery store like we did this time. At only $1.52 for 12 dinner rolls, I have no regrets…just extra time to spend with my family!

Prep Ahead to Keep Your Sanity

Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Admittedly, scratch-cooking fresh foods takes more prep than opening a row of cans with a can opener, but that doesn’t mean you need to be glued to the stove all day. Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

1. Build an American Charcuterie Board

Spot the savings! The imported Swiss Gruyère on the right costs $7.97 for 6 oz., while the Wisconsin-made Gruyère right next to it is just $4.87 for 7 oz. That’s $3.10 less for an EXTRA ounce!

The days of carefree grazing boards might be behind us (for now…fingers crossed!), but you don’t need imported French cheeses or Italian prosciutto to put together an impressive board. The U.S. produces excellent artisanal cheeses! While we navigate this timeline with import taxes kicking us while we’re down, try a sharp, aged Wisconsin white cheddar, a log of creamy Vermont goat cheese, or an Oregon blue cheese instead. Pair your American-made cheeses with domestic cured salami, crisp sliced apples, local honey, and candied Georgia pecans. It looks gorgeous, takes no time at all, supports local dairies, and dodges hefty markups.

2. Pour West Coast Bubbles or Switch to a Festive Mocktail

If your holiday tradition calls for a celebratory toast, you might need to leave the French Champagne and Italian Prosecco behind. Luckily, California, Oregon, and Washington state produce exceptional sparkling wines at a fraction of the cost of imported bottles, giving you festive bubbles without paying international shipping and tariff penalties! If alcohol isn’t in your budget, period, swap it out and save yourself a bunch of money by making a festive mocktail instead!

3. The Real Math: Why We Retired Our Holiday Pot Roast

At Budget Bytes, we don’t typically build holiday recipes around $70 rib roasts, but a cozy beef chuck roast used to be one of our favorite reasonably-priced celebratory centerpieces. Historically, our absolute max to keep a recipe accessible on the site is staying under $3.50 per serving.

So, when we ran the numbers on our classic pot roast between 2024 and 2026 at Walmart, the math was eye-opening:

  • 2024 Beef Pot Roast (8 Servings): A 2.61 lb chuck roast ran $17.33. Combined with basic vegetables, bouillon, and seasonings, the whole pot cost $25.86, or $3.23 per serving.
  • 2026 Beef Pot Roast (8 Servings): That exact same 2.61 lb cut of chuck roast at the same store jumped to $25.02—a 44% increase on the beef alone. Factoring in slight cost increases on the rest of the ingredients, the meal totaled $33.95, or $4.24 per serving.

A 44% jump pushes an everyday pot roast right out of budget territory. Save our pot roast recipe for later, but we’re pivoting for the holidays this year.

It’s All About the Bird!

Domestic turkeys are waiting for you at the grocery store right now at drool-worthy prices because they are raised, processed, and distributed entirely within the United States. While beef prices climbed, we found a whole turkey that easily feeds 7 to 9 people for just $13.11. (I also found another 11.72-pound turkey a few days later for $9.84! Today, I found a frozen whole turkey, 13.55 lbs, for $11.52! That’s a pretty good deal at 85 cents per pound.) 

If you’re hosting a smaller gathering, a 5.5 lb bone-in turkey breast came out to $16.33. But from a budget perspective, the whole bird is easily the better value, giving you more than double the meat at a fraction of the price per pound. If your oven can’t fit a giant bird, you can always spatchcock your turkey to lay it flat, opt for an even cheaper roasted chicken, or go for the turkey breast-only option. Otherwise, go for the full bird and enjoy the leftovers if you have the oven space! (And if you ARE tackling a whole turkey this year, keep an eye out for our ultimate guide to the best Thanksgiving turkey, which is coming soon! It’ll have everything you need to know to get it just right.)

Pro Tip: Roasting a whole bird or a bone-in breast alongside cheap root vegetables like carrots, onions, and potatoes allows the vegetables to caramelize in the flavorful pan drippings, creating your main dish and side dishes all in one pan. After dinner, simmer the roasted bones with water and vegetable scraps to make rich broth, then turn the leftovers into a comforting turkey noodle soup (recipe also coming soon!) or a creamy turkey pot pie later in the week.

Why This Strategy Works

This menu works because it centers on staple proteins and produce, featuring domestically raised turkey alongside hearty crops like potatoes, carrots, onions, garlic, and cranberries. The U.S. produces massive quantities of these staples locally, so their prices aren’t tied to shipping containers, port disputes, or trade friction. The rising cost of gas and diesel does affect domestic products (and our plans to travel for the holidays!), but we’re doing the best we can.

By buying raw or frozen, domestically grown produce and relying on basic American dairy and pantry seasonings, you avoid the packaging tariffs hitting canned goods and the trade surcharges driving up imported meats and luxury items. You don’t have to sacrifice flavor, tradition, or hospitality to survive this season’s grocery landscape; you just have to shop with a plan.

To our friends outside the U.S.A.: While this breakdown is tailored to U.S. supply chains, the exact same principle applies where you are. Leaning into your domestically grown root crops, greenhouse produce, and domestic staples is your best defense against punishing import markups and currency exchange rates.

Milano, via Vasari: sono le 21,30 di un mercoledì qualunque e da Trippa è il secondo turno. Tavoli in legno scuro, tovagliette di carta paglia, pavimenti in cementine. Alle pareti, vecchie stampe pubblicitarie e un telefono a disco Siemens sembrano props di un set fotografico. Invece sono tracce tangibili di un tempo che scivola veloce come l’olio sul pane. 

Aperta nel 2015 (ne leggete a seguire) nel quartiere di Porta Romana, la creatura del cuoco Diego Rossi e dell’imprenditore Pietro Caroli affonda le radici nella tradizione popolare. Quella dei trani milanesi, antichi locali con cucina economica dedicati alla mescita, ma anche delle vecchie osterie, luoghi di incontro e condivisione, prima ancora che di ristoro. 

Dopo anni passati in brigate di alto livello – tra cui quelle del ristorante St. Hubertus di Norbert Niederkofler e delle Antiche Contrade di Cuneo – lo chef veronese ha scelto una strada controcorrente: dedicarsi alla cucina sincera e spontanea, che sa di altri tempi. Da Trippa niente giochi di prestigio né piatti architettati per essere più belli che buoni. Qui contano la sostanza, la materia prima, la veracità. Lo suggerisce già l’insegna, che allude al quinto quarto, e con esso a una cucina viscerale, concreta, senza fronzoli. E a tavola si percepisce tutta.

Ma vale davvero la pena affrontare la lista d’attesa?

Quanto costa Trippa a Milano 

Il menù (digitale) di Trippa è essenziale, tanto nella scelta dei piatti quanto nella quantità degli ingredienti. L’intento è chiaro: proporre una cucina che non si piega alle tendenze del momento né alle logiche dell’algoritmo. Una cucina che parla prima al palato e poi all’occhio. 

In carta ci sono piatti che affondano il mestolo nella tradizione popolare: trippa fritta (15 €) e lumache con panna e funghi (19 €), tortello di zucca alla mantovana burro e salvia croccante o maltagliati con fagioli e tastasal (18 €), baccalà all’olio con tarassaco ripassato e lenticchie (22 €) e quaglia ripiena di rigaglie (22 €). Anche i dessert seguono la stessa linea con la torta di nocciole, la panna cotta al vincotto di fichi, il tiramisù con mascarpone montato (8 €). I prezzi sono verificati in trattoria il 28 ottobre 2026.

Perché andare da Trippa a Milano

La domanda iniziale torna inevitabile: Trippa a Milano vale davvero l’attesa? La risposta è sì, ma con qualche riserva. I piatti sono buoni, eseguiti con precisione e cura. Non cercano l’effetto scenico, perché sono fatti per essere mangiati, non fotografati. Da Trippa si viene per staccare, chiacchierare senza fretta e godersi un piatto di pasta fumante. Sempre che si riesca a prenotare. Ed è proprio qui che la poesia rischia di incrinarsi.

La lista d’attesa, ormai quasi un rito nella Milano da mangiare, può anche alimentare il desiderio. Ma quando diventa un percorso a ostacoli, finisce per trasformare la prenotazione in una prova di resistenza. E a quel punto, più che l’attesa del piacere, resta il piacere rimandato. Come il vino dimenticato nel bicchiere e lasciato svanire. Tornerei? Per la cucina e il servizio, gentile e attento, sì. Per il sistema di prenotazione – una partita a Jumanji – purtroppo no.

Ma pensiamo alla tavola e alla cena.

Ci accomodiamo al bancone, affacciato sulla cucina. Da qui mi sento una voyeur che sbircia dal buco della serratura: uno scorcio privilegiato sul lavoro della brigata, che tra gesti collaudati e movimenti precisi prepara i piatti a una velocità impressionante. È senza dubbio il posto più interessante da cui osservare la scena.

Come si mangia da Trippa a Milano 

La cena comincia con porri di Cervere arrostiti, animelle di vitello fritte e fonduta di formaggio ubriaco (18 €). Il vegetale è saporito, anche se leggermente fibroso. Le frattaglie, morbide all’interno e croccanti all’esterno, sono la parte migliore del piatto. La fonduta, con le sue note lattiche, interrompe la conversazione. Nel complesso buono, ma senza fuochi d’artificio.

La prima portata convince decisamente di più, proprio grazie alla sua essenzialità. Le pappardelle al ragù di pecora e pecorino (19 €) sono schiette, sincere, senza bisogno di effetti speciali. La sfoglia, sottile e traslucida, si avvolge attorno alla forchetta come un nastro di seta. Il sugo è ricco e cremoso. Forse il piatto migliore della serata.

Poi arriva la trippa di vitello alla romana con pomodoro, cannella e pecorino (18 €). Il quinto quarto deve piacere (qui la ricetta della Sora Lella). La consistenza è elastica, quasi viscosa, ma seduce il morso senza difficoltà. Il formaggio dona sapidità, mentre la cannella solletica il palato con sentori speziati. Un cliché che non cerca di piacere a tutti.

La cena si chiude con il sugolo d’uva fragola, ricotta di pecora e pecorino di fossa (8 €). Un gioco di contrasti: chiaro e scuro; lucido e opaco; dolce e salato. Il budino di origine contadina incuriosisce e invoglia ad affondare il cucchiaio.

Trippa per tutti nella Trattoria che ha appena aperto a Milano

3 luglio 2015 – di Emanuele Bonati

Fra le tante teorie della critica gastronomica, c’è quella che sostiene che è meglio non andare a provare un locale appena aperto. Io invece da Trippa a Milano ci sono andato praticamente subito.

Al bando inaugurazione in sordina, rodaggi, sperimentazione e conflitti d’interesse: conosco sia Diego (Rossi) che Pietro (Caroli) da qualche anno.

L’aria hipster e malandrina e il baffo seduttivo alla belle èpoque, stemperati dai tatuaggi e dall’accento veneto è dello chef Diego Rossi. Un percorso lavorativo che lo ha portato prima a Venezia con Giovanni Ciresa, poi da Norbert Niederkofler, da Alfio Ghezzi alla Locanda Margon, e alle Antiche Contrade di Cuneo con Juri Chiotti. Un’aria piacevolmente cialtrona, e per quel che ho potuto vedere una notevole tecnica.

Pietro Caroli viene dalla blogosfera, dove con la sua compagna-presto-sposa Francesca D’Agnano (entrambi brindisini trapiantati a Milano) ha dato vita a un fortunato blog di coppia, Singerfood & Chiccherie; ora ha riversato tutte le sue conoscenze e competenze in questa Trattoria, assumendo le vesti dell’oste a metà fra l’accoglienza pugliese e l’efficienza milanese. Ma secondo me nella tasca del grembiule da trattore tiene una toque blanche, un cappello da cuoco da indossare alla prima disattenzione del suo socio Diego…

E dall’incontro nasce Trippa Trattoria Milano.

Cosa si mangia da Trippa a Milano nel 2015

Per descriverla, voglio iniziare dal menù (ancora in evoluzione, e comunque cambierà a secondo dell’estro, della disponibilità, dell’umore della cucina), che quella sera partiva da tre antipasti (10/11 €): se il baccalà mantecato è un evergreen (recuperato di recente, stava giusto ritornando in seconda linea: un semi-ritorno), la tartare di salmerino è  una variante di un pesce riscoperto di recente, mentre l’insalata d’oca tiene assieme vecchio e nuovo. Ma sono sempre presenti dei fuori carta.

Minestre (sì, minestre: all’antica) – risotto all’aceto, pepe nero e silene (un’erba spontanea, molto utilizzata nella cucina popolare), spaghetti al tonno e fave, zuppa di cicerchie e ortiche. Erbe di campo, ingredienti semplici (l’idea di fondo di Rossi è 3 o 4 ingredienti per piatto). 10 €, 12 il risotto. E ci sarà sempre un risotto in carta, per assecondare una passione dello chef (e mia e dei miei commensali: lo abbiamo preso tutti). Inutile dire che la toque di Pietro verrà buona per preparare le orecchiette (con l’aiuto di Francesca…).

I secondi

Secondi: insalata di guancia e peperoni e cipolla di Tropea, polpo arrosto crema di carote e ciliegie, diaframma tarassaco e senape antica. E pescato del giorno. Qui si arriva alla motivazione del nome del locale, ovvero alla passione che farà da filo conduttore del menù: il quinto quarto, che sarà naturalmente sempre presente in carta. E fra i fuori carta, le lumache gratinate. Da 13 a 14 €.

Verdure. Da tempo non trovavo in un menù milanese tanti nomi “desueti” in contemporanea: silene, tarassaco, cicerchie e carosello, scurie. Fra le verdure infatti, oltre a una misticanza, ci sono scurie (fagiolini lunghi) e cipolle di Tropea agrodolci, e carosello (una cucurbitacea vicina al cetriolo come sapore e consistenza ma più digeribile) olio limone e capperi. 6 €.

I dolci di Trippa a Milano

Dolci. Ritorna il carosello, in un piacevolissimo mix com fragole e spuma di yogurt; e poi ci sono un’ottima crostata di cilliegie, una mousse di ricotta e albicocche, fragole, melone (da 3,50 a 6 €).

La carta varierà spesso: punti fermi, i risotti, la matrice, la trippa, nelle loro varie variazioni e declinazioni, il vitello tonnato, la battuta di fassona della macelleria Martini. E ci saranno pressoché sempre, come detto, dei fuori carta, secondo disponibilità e fantasia – che potrebbero arrivare anche a 18/19 €.

L’altra sera poi c’era uno chef ospite Mirko Balzano, dall’Irpinia. In carta i suoi spaghetti alla scapece con zucchine e la triglia all’arrabbiata con mortadella e provolone. Avevo appena finito la crostata – ma non potevo evitare di assaggiare la triglia. Mamma mia.

Che dire della cucina? Abbiamo assaggiato un po’ di cose, io e i miei commensali. Una cucina semplice, ingredienti ricercati, anzi proprio “cercati”, come le erbe di campo, le verdure i pesci i tagli di carne “dimenticati”. I sapori, le consistenze, tutto era al suo posto e in particolare le cotture erano assolutamente perfette. Una di quelle cucine che in qualche modo ti toccano, ti accarezzano, ti confortano, ti divertono.

Carta dei vini con etichette bio, cinque o sei rossi, quattro bianchi, due spumanti, birre bibite e Coca-Cola in vetro; sik segnala la presenza al bar di bottiglie di Vermouth, un altro recupero memoriale.

Andateci, e sappiatemi dire. Noi, abbiamo concluso la serata con un bicchierino di Strega.

The iPhone 18 Pro Max Takes an Incredible Design and Just Makes It Faster

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every year there’s a new iPhone, and while the iPhone Duo is a huge departure from what Apple has done in the past, the iPhone 18 Pro Max is nearly identical to last year’s model. Apple didn’t do much to drastically change the Pro Max, but instead tweaked some things under the phone to make it a bit more useful.

The most obvious improvement is the A20 Pro, which is significantly stronger than the A19 Pro in last year’s phone. But the most useful improvement has been to Siri. The improvements Apple has made to its ‘Apple Intelligence’ has made Siri more useful than it has been since the company first tried revamping the assistant with AI back in 2024.

So, if you’ve already upgraded to the iPhone 17 Pro last year, there isn’t much reason to upgrade again. But if you’ve been on the fence and need an upgrade, you could really do a lot worse than the iPhone 18 Pro Max.

Design and Features

When I say that the iPhone 18 Pro Max looks just like the iPhone 17 Pro Max, I mean it. Even if I put the two phones on a table next to each other, you’d be hard-pressed to figure out which is which. They both measure 0.34 inches thick, even though the 18 Pro Max is a bit heavier, at 8.78 ounces, compared to the 17's 8.22 ounces. The only way I can tell my iPhone 17 Pro Max from the iPhone 18 Pro Max is that the Pro Max Apple sent me for review has the new Black colorway.

But while the phone’s chassis being unchanged might sound like a bad thing, it really is a case of not fixing something that’s not broken. The best part of the iPhone 17 Pro Max was its camera shelf, which virtually eliminates the phone wobbling when it’s put down on a table. For some reason, other phone manufacturers really haven’t adopted this design language yet, but I’ll just say every time I use an Android device lately, I miss the camera shelf, however absurd that may sound.

The rest of the iPhone 18 Pro Max’s layout is also unchanged. On the right side of the phone, you still get the power/lock button, along with the capacitive Camera Control Button. And just like on the 17 Pro Max, the camera button is extremely easy to hit on accident, so be mindful of it.

Over on the left side is the volume rocker and the Action Button, which I have set to enable or disable Do Not Disturb mode. You can go into the settings and swap it to one of 12 different actions, or just disable it entirely. But I like to keep it on DnD, so that I can make my phone stop blowing up when I’m overwhelmed.

The iPhone 18 Pro Max’s display is also unchanged, at 2868 x 1320, and with a gorgeous OLED panel. Everything I watch or play on the phone looks incredible, with absolutely vibrant colors. It’s also rated for up to 3,000 nits of brightness, which means it gets bright enough to use outside comfortably. The only other phone I’ve used that comes close is the Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra, but that shouldn’t be too surprising, given that Samsung manufactures a lot of Apple’s displays.

Apple has also shrunk the Dynamic Island on the display, so you have less of the display area blacked out at the top of the display. It is still there, and it’s still visible, but I don’t really notice the difference until I have the 18 Pro and the 17 Pro next to each other. There have been phones that have eliminated the need for a camera cutout, and the iPhone still isn’t there, but hopefully by shrinking it, Apple is getting closer to getting rid of it altogether.

Siri Is Better

I have had some problems with Siri for a few years now. For some reason, around 2022 or 2023, Apple's voice assistant stopped working for me most of the time I tried to use it. And it's not like I'm constantly trying to talk to the little robot in my phone or anything, but it got so bad that I couldn't even yell at my phone to skip a song on Apple Music reliably when I was in the shower.

But with the improvements to Apple Intelligence in iOS 27, Siri has dramatically improved. I am no longer asking it to set a timer, only to have it hang and then ask me if I want to query ChatGPT. It just sets the damn timer. I don't want to give Apple too many roses for this, because in a lot of ways, Siri has simply regained functionality that it had a decade ago.

Of course, Apple has also worked in a bunch of AI features, as well, which will let Siri scan through your messages or photos to help you plan events or find information. And, I'm sure that's useful for a lot of people, and it does appear to work well, but I'm old and too used to doing everything manually, and even this new version of 'Apple Intelligence' hasn't converted me to automating everything I do on my phone.

Camera

For the most part, the camera system in the iPhone 18 Pro Max is exactly the same as the iPhone 17 Pro Max, with the only real difference being the addition of a variable aperture. The aperture on the 18 Pro works just like it does on a regular camera, with a mechanical shutter that widens or narrows to control the amount of light in the shot.

For most people, this probably won’t make much of a difference, and the auto aperture setting is going to work fine. However, if you like to have more control of your photos, Apple has added ‘Pro controls’ to the camera app, that lets you dial in the aperture for your shot. It works extremely well, but I still don’t think I’m going to abandon my DSLR and rely on the iPhone cameras. Though, it should come in handy at events like CES, where I typically need to take photos on my phone in the interest of time.

The camera itself is still great, though, and photos look great without really needing to spend a lot of time finding the perfect shot. It’s just nice that Apple has added some finer control for anyone that wants it.

Gaming and Performance

The iPhone 18 Pro Max is powered by the A20 Pro, along with 12GB of RAM, and it is surprising just how much performance Apple was able to squeeze out of it. In every test I’ve run on the new Pro, it far surpasses its predecessor.

In GeekBench 7, the iPhone 18 Pro Max gets a single-core score of 3867 points and a multi-core score of 11,204 points, compared to the 17 Pro, which gets 2,865 and 7,120 points, respectively. That’s a massive 44% improvement to multi-core performance, and a 29% improvement to single-core performance.

Even compared to the Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra the iPhone 18 Pro Max is 24% faster, with the Android device getting 2,725 points for single-core and 8,786 points for multi-core.

When it comes to gaming, the GPU stapled to the A20 Pro is also impressive, with the 18 Pro scoring 8001 points in the Wild Life Extreme test, compared to 4795 from the 17 Pro Max and 5758 points from the Galaxy S26 Ultra. That makes the 18 Pro Max 33% faster than Samsung’s 2026 flagship.

Just like the 17 Pro, the 18 Pro Max is cooled by a vapor chamber, and it's even slightly larger this year. But, even though it has more surface area, the iPhone 18 Pro isn't more stable than last year's model, coming in at 66% in the 3DMark Steel Nomad Light stress test. That's just a bit worse than the 17 Pro Max, which got 69% in the same test last year, but with how much faster the A20 Pro is, I'll take it.

Apple also included a bigger battery this year, with 5,567mAh of capacity, compared to 5,088mAh in the iPhone 17 Pro Max. Apple claims that this battery will last through 30 hours of regular use, and while I don’t have a reliable way to benchmark battery life, I believe Apple’s numbers.

I have been pushing this phone for around three weeks now, and I usually only put it on the charger every other day, and the phone hasn’t died on me yet. Obviously, if you’re taking a lot of photos or playing a heavy game like Where Winds Meet, that number will go down, but even then I don’t imagine the phone dying in the course of a day.

Purchasing Guide

The iPhone 18 Pro Max is available today, starting at $1,299, which will get you 256GB of storage. You can also get the iPhone 18 Pro, with a smaller display, starting at $1,199.
