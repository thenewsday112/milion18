Monster Hunter Outlanders Review So Far

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Few games have changed the trajectory of their respective series quite like Monster Hunter World, one of the biggest smash hits Capcom has ever created. World was a vibrant, expansive, and ambitious leap that paid off in spades. It makes sense, then, that eight years later the series would still be chasing that high. Even with the time that's passed, I find myself amazed that Monster Hunter Outlanders, essentially, manages to cram the experience of World into a mobile game. This mobile spinoff certainly isn't attempting to reinvent the wheel, but after six hours with the game, it's proving to be a surprisingly competent package, although I have some bubbling hesitations about the grind and encroaching gacha systems.

Anytime there's a mobile spinoff of a major series, the first question that comes to mind is how that game will be compromised, or truncated, from that full console or PC experience. And so often, those mobile games are simplifications of a larger idea, like Fire Emblem Heroes or Diablo Immortal. What I've found fascinating about Monster Hunter Outlanders so far is that it doesn't really simplify the overall gameplay loop and structure. Rather, it's sort of a reconfigured Monster Hunter that strings out progression to support the typical framework of a mobile game, one filled with nearly a half-dozen different currencies to keep track of at any given time. But it also very much feels like Outlanders wants players to engage with it like a full-fledged Monster Hunter, and even includes all the story exposition you'd expect to go along with it.

Like most Monster Hunter games, Outlanders begins with a big cinematic opening where a group of adventurers from the Adventurer's Guild are striking out to an uncharted part of the world – in this case, an island called Aesoland. This island is filled with Radiant species of monsters, beasts whose abilities have been enhanced by a mineral called Radiantite. This is, ostensibly, also the game's reasoning for creating new designs of classic series monsters and tweaking their abilities to match this game specifically, like the Pukei-Pukei focusing more on head or tail attacks, which are easier to target with Outlander's more streamlined lock-on system.

But I cannot stress enough how much Outlanders banks on the same formula as World; it's almost as if the same game was transposed onto different locations and story elements. Even the first area of the game, like World, is a heavily forested area with a lot of verticality for climbing or setting ambushes; intentionally designed to show you the ropes. If you've played any Monster Hunter game, you know that core gameplay loop of what to expect – you take missions or hunts, track down monsters in an instanced map, beat them, harvest materials, and then use said materials to craft more powerful equipment.

Complementing this, you have a moderately-sized open world that you can roam around and explore to take down smaller monsters, find crafting materials, and more. Outlanders also brings back many of the elements that have become mainstays since World: scoutfly trails to guide you to objectives, buildable objects on the map like towers and ziplines, and monsters that interact and fight each other like natural predators. All these elements click together just as well as they did in World, creating a compelling loop that feels like you're constantly doing something meaningful to that overall progress. Outlanders is also impressive in terms of presentation with environments that genuinely look stunning; playing on an iPhone 14 Plus, I had a consistent framerate with very few dips.

However, the differences start to surface as you dig deeper into the minutiae of how the game actually plays and what options are available in combat scenarios. Certain aspects of the combat have been streamlined to fit the mobile experience and accommodate a touchscreen control scheme instead of a controller (although there is controller support if you want it). Movement and actions aren't as intricate, although the game sports two different control methods: Adventure Mode and Classic Mode. The former is intended for new players and streamlines things down to three buttons, Attack, React, and Burst. From there, the game contextually handles a lot of what you're doing, like the React button allowing you to dodge when a monster attacks, or block if the time is right. Classic Mode, on the other hand, gives you full manual control, including traditional button-stringed combos.

It's proving to be a surprisingly competent package, although I have bubbling hesitations about the gacha mechanics.

What I've found so far, though, is that Classic Mode proves to be unfit for just touch controls, as it's too cumbersome to use all of these different options without a controller. Adventure Mode does, generally, feel good to use, but takes some of the vibrant friction out of the experience by streamlining so much. That means the ideal way to play this, if you can, is on Classic Mode with a controller – which then ultimately raises the question of why you wouldn't just play a console Monster Hunter. There are answers to that question, of course, like if you don't own anything else to play a Monster Hunter game on.

The other two major elements where Outlanders shifts that classic experience is with your ability to build a party of adventurers and how progression works. To try and fit the typical idea of a gacha game, Outlanders creates a group of distinct characters that take part in the story who you can then recruit and use as NPC companions. Recruiting means partaking in those traditional gacha pulls using currency called Rutacoins.

Each Adventurer has their own distinct moveset that builds on one specific weapon type, meaning playing as that Adventurer is different from just slapping any other weapon on your player-created character. This does create some incentive to try and get these various characters. Of course, the real key to this system is how easy or difficult it might be to pull Adventurers and upgrade them to be useful in combat. I was able to build a full four-person party fairly easily with the currency I earned from starting the game. Overall, I like the idea of being able to build a party with these distinct characters and play solo. The option is still there to group up with other players and friends, but the Adventurers make it easy to tackle quests and Contracts on your own. But I can already see hints of it being frustrating to pull repeat characters, let alone anyone new you might want down the line.

This leads to the area I'm not as hot on: the progression system. While almost all of the currency in the game is earnable from playing, as well as spending real money, I'm finding that the classic Monster Hunter progression loop feels a bit slower here. You need Zenny to pay for items, upgrades, and services. Rutacoins are for pulling characters and sidekicks (Palicos and the like). Research Points needed for the upgradable skill tree to unlock more buildable structures, for example. And as you might expect, there are additional materials necessary for upgrading a character or sidekick's level, unlocking their level cap, upgrading weapons, crafting items, and more.

Monster Hunter has always had a lot of elements to juggle in terms of progression, which is arguably the biggest barrier to entry for some people. In many ways, the series has been about busy work, sifting through menus, and meticulously crafting sets of armor and weapons. Those are elements of Monster Hunter I've always enjoyed, but even after a half-dozen hours, I'm feeling quite fatigued by the sheer amount of stuff in Outlanders. There's a lot of currency and whatnot to earn, and equally as much to figure out in terms of how to get things done, as well as hours of tutorials.

This has, ultimately, made the opening hours a bit more of a slog than something like Monster Hunter World or Rise. And even though I'm genuinely having a good time so far, my primary concern at the moment is how all that might affect the experience as a whole, especially as you get to the more difficult and complex hunts. I've played enough gacha games in my life to know that the fun can be brought to a grinding halt pretty quickly when resources become a wall you have to overcome.

I'm feeling quite fatigued by the sheer amount of 'stuff' in Outlanders.

Of course, it'll take time to make a final judgement, but my overarching sentiment on Outlanders right now is surprise: for how it still leans into that essential component of timing in combat, and for how uncompromised the visuals and world design are. Those insidious mobile elements that too many games fall into are still nipping at the edges, but I'm hoping the core experience is able to stave it off in the long-run. I'll be pouring more time into Monster Hunter Outlanders in the coming days for the full review coming soon.

The future of protein supplementation?

(Image: ChatGPT)

PlaybookHQ, a leading YouTube channel covering sports science and technology, recently explored a potentially exciting development in protein supplementation: Space Milk, a novel yeast-derived protein supplement (see here).

The product is made from yeast protein extracted through fermentation. Space Milk describes it as the “perfect protein”, claiming that it provides a complete amino acid profile, is easy to digest and offers an alternative to conventional protein supplements.

The video highlights potential limitations of existing protein sources, including incomplete amino acid profiles in certain plant-based proteins, concerns about heavy metal contamination in some supplements and digestive issues associated with dairy-based whey protein. Space Milk claims to address these limitations and emphasises transparency through independent third-party testing and publicly available results.

Although the concept is intriguing, further independent research is needed to substantiate claims about its digestibility, protein quality and effects on muscle protein synthesis, as well as any potential advantages over established protein sources such as whey.

It will be fascinating to see whether yeast-derived protein supplements prove to be a genuine innovation in sports nutrition or simply another short-lived industry trend.

Why are FC Bayern Munich players wearing tape on their ears?

(Image: Facebook)

Recent images of FC Bayern Munich players wearing adhesive tape on their earlobes have attracted considerable attention on social media (see here), with many people wondering why the tape is there. However, the practice is not new: FC Bayern Munich players were also spotted wearing similar tape last season.

The explanation lies in the club’s sports science and performance-monitoring practices. FC Bayern Munich’s medical and sports science staff collect blood lactate samples from players’ earlobes instead of using the more traditional fingertip method. These samples allow staff to measure blood lactate concentrations and assess players’ physiological responses to training. The resulting data can help staff tailor training intensity, monitor responses to physical workloads and adjust where necessary.

So, there is a scientific explanation for the adhesive tape seen on FC Bayern Munich players’ ears. It is not a new fashion trend nor evidence that all the players suddenly decided to have their ears pierced at the same time!

Young athletes don’t develop every physical quality at the same rate

(Image: Premier League)

A recently published longitudinal meta-analysis examined patterns of physical development in male adolescent athletes. Drawing on 28 studies across nine sports, the researchers focused on three key physical qualities: lower-body power, acceleration and endurance. Lower-body power was assessed primarily through the countermovement jump, acceleration through the 10-metre sprint, and endurance through tests including the Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test, the 20-metre shuttle run, and interval shuttle runs.

Notably, improvements in lower-body power and endurance were not linear. The fastest rates of improvement occurred at approximately 13.2 years for lower-body power and 13.8 years for endurance, broadly coinciding with the typical timing of peak height velocity in male athletes. Yet acceleration followed a different pattern. Although it is tempting to assume that every physical quality improves rapidly as young athletes enter puberty, 10-metre sprint performance improved relatively steadily between the ages of 12 and 17, with no clearly identifiable surge. Acceleration, therefore, did not appear to benefit from the same obvious maturational boost as lower-body power and endurance.

The key message from this research is that young athletes do not develop every physical quality at the same rate. Around peak height velocity, qualities such as lower-body power and endurance may improve rapidly, while others, including acceleration, may develop more steadily throughout adolescence.

The danger of chasing perfection

Michael Owen & Jonny Wilkinson (Image: High Performance Podcast)

The High Performance Podcast recently featured former England footballer Michael Owen, and a clip shared on Instagram sparked a fascinating debate about the potential dangers of chasing perfection.

In the clip, Owen discussed former England rugby star Jonny Wilkinson and his relentless approach to practice. According to Owen, Wilkinson would line up 20 balls and try to kick all 20 successfully. If he missed even one, however, he would reset the 20 balls and start again from ball one. Owen admitted that Wilkinson’s mentality fascinated him because it was completely different from his own. Although he acknowledged that this mindset can certainly help an athlete reach the top, he also described it as “pretty damn dangerous.”

Although Owen was fascinated by this relentless pursuit of perfection, he knew that such a mentality would never have worked for him. Instead, he described himself as having what he called a “shit filter.” If he had a bad touch during a game, rather than dwelling on it, he would tell himself: “That’s my bad touch out the way.” This allowed him to move on immediately while continuing to believe that he could be the best player on the pitch.

This clip from The High Performance Podcast is particularly thought-provoking because it highlights two very different approaches to elite performance. Jonny Wilkinson and Michael Owen both reached the highest level of their respective sports, yet they appeared to have very different relationships with mistakes and perfection.

Social media before training: a performance killer?

(Image: Women’s Health)

Lars Johansen, a sports psychologist at Denmark’s national high-performance organisation, recently warned that using social media before exercise can cause mental fatigue and impair physical performance (see here). Johansen highlighted a study showing that participants who used social media for 30 minutes before taking a Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test performed approximately 10% worse than a control group that did not.

The study Johansen referenced examined young football players after 30 minutes of cognitively demanding smartphone use. In the test, players covered an average of 1,610 m compared to 1,780 m in the control condition—a 10.56% reduction in running distance. However, the task was not simply scrolling through Instagram or TikTok; it involved a specifically designed, cognitively demanding smartphone application.

Nevertheless, Johansen likened the physical decline from pre-exercise social media use to “smoking three to four cigarettes before you train.” While acknowledging that a total ban on social media is unrealistic, he strongly advises against using it in the hours leading up to training and competition, when peak physical and mental performance is required.

While the study Johansen cited may be more nuanced than everyday scrolling, his core message about social media’s impact on athletic performance has never been more relevant. Are female athletes underfuelled and underhydrated?

(Image: Axes Physical Theraphy)

Recent research from the Gatorade Sports Science Institute has revealed some concerning findings regarding the nutrition and hydration habits of female athletes. The research included over 500 female athletes across various sports and found that 43% of participants began their training in a dehydrated state. Additionally, most athletes failed to sufficiently replenish the sodium they lost while sweating, and over half weren’t taking in enough carbohydrates to support their performance.

These results are troubling and suggest that many female athletes may not be performing at their full potential due to poor hydration, insufficient sodium intake, and inadequate carbohydrate consumption. The researchers also recommend establishing female-specific hydration guidelines instead of relying on data primarily derived from male athletes. By developing female-specific sweat-rate classifications, awareness can be raised about the importance of individualised hydration and sodium replacement strategies for female athletes.

It is important to note that this research was conducted by the Gatorade Sports Science Institute, which may introduce some bias due to industry affiliation. However, the research highlights a significant issue and emphasises the need for greater focus on hydration and fuelling practices among female athletes.

The Modern Formulator’s Challenge

Tagatose Is More Than a Sweetener

Most formulators first discover tagatose because of its remarkable sugar-like sweetness profile. That alone gets attention. But underneath the sweetness lies something far more interesting. Are cooking skills the missing ingredient in sports nutrition?

(Image: My Fitness Pal)

Sports nutrition has advanced considerably in recent years, and athletes now routinely receive expert guidance to support training and performance. However, a new review highlights an often-overlooked question: do athletes know how to prepare and cook their food?

The review examined previous interventions designed to improve athletes’ food preparation and cooking skills. Despite extensive research on sports nutrition, the researchers found only 10 studies that combined hands-on food preparation with nutrition education. None measured whether athletes’ practical cooking skills improved.

The review therefore highlights a gap between sports nutrition knowledge and practical application. For example, a field-sport athlete may know to consume sufficient carbohydrate before training and combine carbohydrate with high-quality protein post-training. Yet this knowledge has limited value if they lack the confidence or skills to shop for, prepare, and cook suitable meals.

The review raises an important question for coaches and sports nutritionists: are we spending too much time telling athletes what to eat and too little teaching them how to prepare it?

New research challenges osteoarthritis exercise advice

(Image: Chris Bailey Orthopaedics)

A massive longitudinal study has challenged conventional medical advice for people with knee or hip osteoarthritis. Traditionally, these patients have been encouraged to choose low-impact activities such as cycling or swimming to help protect their joints. However, this large-scale longitudinal study analysed more than 17,000 patients and produced some very interesting findings.

The researchers found that participating in high-impact activities was not associated with an increased risk of knee replacement. In fact, those who regularly engaged in high-impact activities had a substantially lower risk of requiring a knee replacement than those who did not. Therefore, if an individual’s capacity allows, people with knee or hip osteoarthritis should not fear high-impact activities. Instead, they should embrace them, as this longitudinal study suggests they may help support long-term joint health.

New research on HYROX injuries

(Image: Chilitri)

As HYROX continues to grow rapidly in popularity, the first prospective study tracking injuries and health problems among HYROX athletes has now been published. The study followed 89 athletes over a 12-week period, providing new insight into the types of injuries occurring and the areas of the body most commonly affected.

The researchers recorded 14.4 injuries per 1,000 hours of training and competition. Notably, 73% of injuries were classified as overuse rather than acute injuries, while the knee was the most commonly affected area, accounting for 22% of all injuries. Running was also the most frequently reported injury mechanism. Interestingly, shoulder injuries were almost non-existent, despite shoulder problems being relatively common in other high-intensity training environments.

While considerably more research is needed, these early findings highlight the substantial running demands associated with HYROX and suggest that managing running load may be particularly important. For coaches and athletes, gradually building running capacity and carefully monitoring training load could therefore help reduce the risk of overuse injuries.

Why Mikel Arteta is deliberately disrupting Arsenal’s preparation

Arsenal F.C. manager Mikel Arteta has sparked significant online discussion after revealing an unconventional method he used during pre-season to deliberately disrupt his players’ usual preparation (see here). Arteta said he intentionally had the team arrive late to the stadium, delayed meals, made the dressing room uncomfortably hot and even reduced the number of massage beds available. These planned disruptions interfered with the players’ normal pre-match routines, with Arteta believing that exposing players and staff to these situations could help them become more adaptable when unexpected challenges arise during the season.

While this approach may seem unusual, it closely resembles a psychological technique known as stress exposure training. This involves exposing individuals to controlled and realistic stressors, so they become more accustomed to handling pressure and disruption when similar situations occur for real.

Typically, coaches strive to create the best possible environment for their athletes to prepare and perform. However, Arteta’s approach presents an interesting alternative. Occasionally introducing controlled stress and disruption into the training environment may help athletes become more adaptable and better prepared to cope when things do not go according to plan.

Coaching tips you don’t get on social media!

James de Lacey (Image: Canvas Rebel)

Our very own James de Lacey recently shared some excellent practical coaching tips on LinkedIn for delivering team training sessions more effectively.

Firstly, when addressing players, ensure that all of them are positioned in front of you rather than behind you. This means avoiding the common practice of having players form a circle around the coach. By positioning everyone in front, it becomes much easier to communicate clearly with the entire group.

Secondly, when communicating with players outdoors, take into account the position of the sun. Players should not have to look directly into the sun while receiving instructions. Instead, the coach should face the sun, even if it feels slightly uncomfortable, rather than expecting the entire team to do so. With the sun behind them, players will be in a better position to focus, listen, and absorb instructions.

Lastly, there shouldn’t be a noticeable divide between where the warm-up ends and training begins. Excessive stretching and dynamic movements should be completed by players in their own time before training, allowing the session to flow naturally into the main training activities.

Eccentric training: strength and flexibility combined?

(Image: Steel Supplements)

A very interesting and cleverly designed new study has shown that eccentric training may improve flexibility just as effectively as static stretching. Participants completed a 10-week intervention, training three times per week. The researchers used a within-subject design, whereby one leg performed eccentric-only hamstring training while the other performed conventional static hamstring stretching. This meant that each participant effectively acted as their own control.

The results showed similar improvements in joint range of motion following both interventions. However, the eccentric-trained leg also experienced significantly greater increases in hamstring strength and muscle size, highlighting an important additional benefit of eccentric training.

Therefore, while both methods appear effective for improving hamstring flexibility, eccentric training may offer coaches and athletes a more time-efficient approach by simultaneously targeting flexibility, strength and muscle size.

Zone Fun Training

📅 May 15, 2024  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There’s lots of talk about Zone 1, 2, 3, and 4 cardio, with each “zone” based on the level of aerobic or anaerobic energy you’re utilizing. The lower the zone, the more aerobic you are and the more fat you’re burning as percentage of total energy. The higher the zone, the more anaerobic you are the more glucose and glycogen you’re burning. Lower zones are easier and slower. Higher zones are harder and faster. 

This is a sound way to think about “cardio,” but it doesn’t cover every zone. There’s one big training zone missing from the conversation: Zone Fun.

Subscribe now

In Zone Fun, intensity doesn’t matter. It can be high or low. You can go fast or slow. Intense or easy. Zone Fun oscillates across all the heart rate zones. Because the prime concern and determinant is whether or not you’re having fun, the other stuff melts away. Not in importance—all the aerobic zones still matter, intensity still matters—but in what you have to consciously pay attention to.

You can just keep going as long as you’re having fun and moving your body.

I’d go so far as to say that hitting Zone Fun is the most important metric for your cardiovascular training. If you reach Zone Fun, if you’re moving fast and slow and medium and moving with intensity and then slowing things down, you are doing the right thing. You are improving your health and fitness. Having fun is a good barometer for doing the right thing health wise. Your body intrinsically “knows” when you’re improving skills, increasing VO2max, building an aerobic base and your ability to tolerate anaerobic discomfort, strengthening muscle—fun is the tell. 

We have evidence that Zone Fun training is even more effective than other kinds. When you subject rodents in the lab to forced exercise—sticking them on a rodent wheel, making them tread water, tying weights to their tails and making them drag them and climb ladders—you see moderately beneficial effects, but sometimes negative effects. When you give rodents the opportunity to engage in voluntary exercise like free access to a wheel, and they choose to do it, the benefits are greater. For instance, one study found that mice doing a ten week voluntary running program had more favorable shifts in muscle tissue fiber development than mice doing ten weeks of forced running.1 Another even found that mice with colitis had higher mortality and inflammation when they were forced to exercise and lower inflammation and mortality when they chose to exercise.2

Okay, so what are some ways to practice Zone Fun training?

Ultimate Frisbee

My favorite way to get into Zone Fun is with the greatest sport of all time: Ultimate Frisbee. Sadly, I don’t get to do this as often as I’d like to. But when I lived in Malibu, I had a weekly game set aside with the same group of guys. It was a mix of running, jumping, throwing, jostling for positioning, sprinting, and jogging. Most importantly, it was fun. It was a game. It was a game requiring strategy, planning, and intense concentration. Because when you’re concentrating or when you’re thinking ahead and thinking in the moment, you’re having fun. You’re not thinking about the hard work that you’re doing. You don’t even realize that you’re breathing hard, that you’re starting to get sore. You don’t notice the burning. And therein lies the beauty of Zone Fun.

Fat tire biking

I’ve taken fitness influencers out on the sand that are half my age and they always tell me it’s the hardest workout of their lives and can barely keep up. No joke. But the thing is, you keep going because riding through sand next to the beautiful water, seeing all the lovely sights that one sees, invigorates and makes the work worth it.

Standup paddling

Paddling is my other method of Zone Fun training. Up on the paddleboard, I get into my rhythm and flow, and the hours just melt away. I'm never quite breathing hard. My serratus anterior muscles are getting an incredible workout (there's no better ab exercise). My heart is working, the blood is flowing, and the fat is burning. This is a gentle form of training.

Share

Play sports

The perfect way to access Zone Fun training is to play sports.

Pick a sport that you used to really enjoy when you were younger. The trick to training is that it shouldn’t feel like work. You also shouldn’t feel like you’re learning to play. You should come into it fairly naturally. So, if you’re playing basketball, you should be able to dribble the ball, make a layup, and generally feel comfortable with the flow of the game. If you’re trying to pick up a game of soccer, make sure that you’re comfortable handling the ball, passing, shooting, and generally move with the way the game moves. And that’s true for anything. You don’t want to be learning the fundamentals when you’re trying to engage in Zone Fun. You don’t have to be an all-star, but you shouldn’t be a total beginner.

Make the boring interesting

Another way to use Zone Fun training is to take what might normally be a boring form of movement and make it more interesting and fun. For example, instead of running on a treadmill, go for a trail run. Changing topography, textural shifts, rising and climbing the trail, plus all the plants and animals and beautiful smells around you, will make what’s normally a boring plod around the track much more interesting and fun. Deep into the endurance world, I did most of my training on trails and mountain passes just because it was so much more interesting than just running around the track or on a treadmill. It was the only way I could make the incredibly uncomfortable training bearable.

Another substitution might be mountain biking instead of road biking. Insead of zoning out on a road, you have to constantly stay alert for roots, rocks, jumps, drops, and trees. You have to stay immersed in the moment—you can’t just bear down and suffer like you can on the road. This gives you just as good a workout without feeling like it.

Rock scrambling

Rock scrambling is my absolute favorite way to traverse the wild. I grew up in a small fishing town in Maine, scrambling all over the rocks that lined the coast. I think this was how I got most of my movement and training foundation. There's something so satisfying about scrambling on all fours up extremely steep rock faces. It’s just pure fun and I think it triggers something primordial within us.

Loaded partner carries

Here's what I mean by loaded partner carries:

Get a group of at least two people together. Choose a walking path, ideally with an incline. Up a mountain is best. Then, pick a heavy object, at least one per person. If you have two people, choose 1-2 objects. If you're a group of three, choose 2-3 objects. And then you go on the walk while carrying the objects and switching off with each other.

A kettlebell and a weighted sandbag. A big log and a heavy rock. A pair of dumbbells. A barbell and a rucksack. You get the idea. Just go on the walk with a friend or friends, taking turns carrying the objects you brought. This is very hard work, but because you're working together, it's fun. You have each other to lean on and nobody wants to let the other people down. Turns out that camaraderie is extremely motivating.
