The future of protein supplementation?

(Image: ChatGPT)

PlaybookHQ, a leading YouTube channel covering sports science and technology, recently explored a potentially exciting development in protein supplementation: Space Milk, a novel yeast-derived protein supplement (see here).

The product is made from yeast protein extracted through fermentation. Space Milk describes it as the “perfect protein”, claiming that it provides a complete amino acid profile, is easy to digest and offers an alternative to conventional protein supplements.

The video highlights potential limitations of existing protein sources, including incomplete amino acid profiles in certain plant-based proteins, concerns about heavy metal contamination in some supplements and digestive issues associated with dairy-based whey protein. Space Milk claims to address these limitations and emphasises transparency through independent third-party testing and publicly available results.

Although the concept is intriguing, further independent research is needed to substantiate claims about its digestibility, protein quality and effects on muscle protein synthesis, as well as any potential advantages over established protein sources such as whey.

It will be fascinating to see whether yeast-derived protein supplements prove to be a genuine innovation in sports nutrition or simply another short-lived industry trend.

Why are FC Bayern Munich players wearing tape on their ears?

(Image: Facebook)

Recent images of FC Bayern Munich players wearing adhesive tape on their earlobes have attracted considerable attention on social media (see here), with many people wondering why the tape is there. However, the practice is not new: FC Bayern Munich players were also spotted wearing similar tape last season.

The explanation lies in the club’s sports science and performance-monitoring practices. FC Bayern Munich’s medical and sports science staff collect blood lactate samples from players’ earlobes instead of using the more traditional fingertip method. These samples allow staff to measure blood lactate concentrations and assess players’ physiological responses to training. The resulting data can help staff tailor training intensity, monitor responses to physical workloads and adjust where necessary.

So, there is a scientific explanation for the adhesive tape seen on FC Bayern Munich players’ ears. It is not a new fashion trend nor evidence that all the players suddenly decided to have their ears pierced at the same time!

Young athletes don’t develop every physical quality at the same rate

(Image: Premier League)

A recently published longitudinal meta-analysis examined patterns of physical development in male adolescent athletes. Drawing on 28 studies across nine sports, the researchers focused on three key physical qualities: lower-body power, acceleration and endurance. Lower-body power was assessed primarily through the countermovement jump, acceleration through the 10-metre sprint, and endurance through tests including the Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test, the 20-metre shuttle run, and interval shuttle runs.

Notably, improvements in lower-body power and endurance were not linear. The fastest rates of improvement occurred at approximately 13.2 years for lower-body power and 13.8 years for endurance, broadly coinciding with the typical timing of peak height velocity in male athletes. Yet acceleration followed a different pattern. Although it is tempting to assume that every physical quality improves rapidly as young athletes enter puberty, 10-metre sprint performance improved relatively steadily between the ages of 12 and 17, with no clearly identifiable surge. Acceleration, therefore, did not appear to benefit from the same obvious maturational boost as lower-body power and endurance.

The key message from this research is that young athletes do not develop every physical quality at the same rate. Around peak height velocity, qualities such as lower-body power and endurance may improve rapidly, while others, including acceleration, may develop more steadily throughout adolescence.

Sasanelli, dolci tradizionali a Ognissanti: come farli morbidi al cuore

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ho conosciuto i sasanelli (che mi dicono chiamarsi anche sosomelli o sassanelli) a Gravina in Puglia. Fuori dalla data cui erano associati un tempo, cioè ad Ognissanti e della commemorazione dei defunti. Dolcetto invernale che molte fonti dicono nati appunto a Gravina e che si è poi allargato geograficamente e anche nel calendario.

Il “marchio” geografico è rassicurante anche se non c’è di mezzo una Dop o una Igp. Un po’ come dire pistacchio di Bronte o pasta di Gragnano. Quindi Sasanello gravinese è quel dolcetto che fa un po’ impazzire chi vuole farlo a casa perché deve essere morbido al cuore, all’interno. E in questo mi ricorda la damnatio delle Tette delle monache di Altamura che hanno occupato spesso le pagine web alla ricerca della ricetta migliore. E un po’ mi ricordano per consistenza i mustacciuoli delle feste natalizie napoletane e campane. Morbidi e golosi. Un imperativo.

Che a Gravina ha conosciuto diciamo un’evoluzione quando al vincotto del mosto d’uva ha fatto seguito il vincotto di fichi. Precisazione che va fatta perché a leggere vincotto — nome che è rimasto — non dovrete andare a caccia di mosto d’uva appena vendemmiato e messo a decantare. La storia ci dice che la sostituzione è arrivata perché le note dei fichi meglio si intonano alla ricetta del Sasanelli. E noi devotamente ci crediamo perché solo in questa versione li abbiamo assaggiati. E li abbiamo trovati buonissimi.

Gravina o Giovinazzo per i Sasanelli

Dicevamo Gravina ma qui la ricetta è una variazione proposta a Giovinazzo. Stiamo parlando di una settantina di chilometri di distanza. Ma che sono importanti perché come ogni ricetta tipica che si rispetti, anche per i Sasanielli vale la regola che un condominio ha un segreto migliore del dirimpettaio. Figuriamoci dopo aver attraversato più di mezza Puglia.

Ma c’è anche la questione che questa ricetta è proposta da Nicola Giotti dell’omonima pasticceria. Ovvero colui che è tra i custodi di questo dolce della tradizione. Nicola è la terza generazione della Pasticceria Giotti fondata dal nonno — stesso nome — e da Gerarda De Marco nel 1947. Dalla sua (e anche dalla ricetta) c’è la sua appartenenza all’Apei, l’associazione Ambasciatori Pasticceri d’Eccellenza fondata da Iginio Massari.

E quindi la particolarità rispetto alla ricetta originale dei sasanelli: la farcitura con mele cotogne. Ecco cosa ci dice Giotti.

“Accanto alla versione a base di vincotto di fichi, mandorle tostate e gocce di cioccolato fondente, la farcitura con confettura di mele cotogne aggiunge una nota fruttata e delicatamente aromatica, evocando le conserve casalinghe e il valore delle materie prime stagionali. L’incontro tra la morbidezza dell’impasto e il gusto caratteristico della confettura restituisce un equilibrio di sapori che parla di memoria, territorio e tradizioni familiari”.

Quindi, non scappiamo di fronte al problema numero 1. Cioè la morbidezza dell’impasto che tale deve restare nel cuore di questo goloso biscotto.

Come farli morbidi

Diciamo subito — così lo tenete a mente — che il segreto (mica tanto segreto) per ottenere sasanelli morbidi all’interno e leggermente rustici fuori è fare attenzione alla cottura. Quindi se vi siete già cimentati nella ricetta e avete ottenuto delle simpatiche pietre, ricordatevi delle avvertenze.

  1. Riposo dell’impasto. Fai riposare l’impasto coperto per circa 30-60 minuti a temperatura ambiente.
  2. Formatura. Preporziona delle palline o dei mucchietti irregolari (questi sì simili a piccole pietre) e schiacciali appena.
  3. Cottura breve. Cuoci in forno preriscaldato (intorno ai 170°C–180°C) per soli 10-15 minuti. Non cuocerli troppo: devono risultare asciutti sotto ma ancora morbidi al cuore.

E se non volete incorrere in errori o ripeterli, potete sempre andare da Nicola Giotti a questo indirizzo o farveli spedire. Ricordando che i sasanelli a Natale valgono sempre insieme alle cartellate.

Railway Archives 1950: How the East Coast Main Line Recovered from the Devastating Floods of 1948

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The October 1950 issue of The Railway Magazine detailed the reconstruction work carried out following severe flooding on the East Coast main line between Berwick and Dunbar in August 1948. The report describes the replacement of seven bridges, repairs to a damaged culvert near Ayton and reconstruction work near Burnmouth.

Free Kirk Bridge over the Eye Water temporarily rebuilt as a viaduct consisting of military-type steel trestles and girders

The floods of August 1948

ON August 12, 1948, exceptionally severe floods caused serious damage on the East Coast main line between Berwick and Dunbar. The railway was closed completely for nearly 11 weeks, while temporary repairs were executed, and was reopened for goods traffic on October 25, and for passengers on November 1. Although the Great North Road runs at no great distance from the railway, difficulty was experienced in bringing materials and plant to some of the sites, and the preliminary work included the construction of special approach roads. The work of permanent reconstruction was put in hand in the autumn of 1948, and was finally completed on May 21 of this year with the erection of the 71 ft. 6 in. steel girders and concrete decking of bridge No. 130, near Reston, enabling the down track to be laid over it.

Replacing the damaged bridges

On the five-mile section between Grantshouse and Reston, seven bridges carrying the railway over the Eye Water were totally destroyed, and, as a temporary measure, steel bridges of a military type were built to replace them and to restore traffic as quickly as possible. They were erected in such a way that after the reopening of the line to traffic it would be possible to build concrete abutments and wing-walls as substructures for the permanent bridges without causing interference with traffic. This work progressed steadily according to schedule, and on February 19 this year work began on the placing of the new superstructures in position.

Six of these permanent superstructures are of the deck type, with one pair of steel girders under each line of railway, and the seventh is of the through type with a small depth of construction to give sufficient clearance between the river bed and the underside of the superstructure.

Temporary structure of the Free Kirk Bridge still in place after the concrete abutments and wings of the permanent bridge had been built

Working Sunday by Sunday

On each of the Sundays between February 19 and May 21 inclusive, with the exception of March 5 and April 9—or on twelve working Sundays in all—the half of each of the six deck-type superstructures, carrying either the up or down line, was replaced. The same procedure in the erection was followed each Sunday.

First, two locomotive cranes removed the girders and the top portions of the steel trestling forming the half of the temporary bridge carrying the up line. The two new steel girders to carry this track—which are of all-welded construction, and with diaphragms fixed in position weighed in all 26 tons—were next brought forward and placed in position. Special dished precast reinforced concrete units in 3-ft. widths were then set on top of the new girders to form a well-deck floor on which were laid the stone ballast and permanent way to carry the up line. On the following Sunday the other half of the temporary bridge was removed and the permanent superstructure erected to carry the down line, the transfer from temporary to permanent superstructures occupying two consecutive Sundays in the case of each bridge.

The seventh bridge

Meanwhile, the seventh bridge, already referred to as of the through type, had been replaced. The work entailing occupation of the line was carried out on the other two Sundays of this period. On March 5, in the first phase, the three main girders, also of welded construction, were placed by railway cranes on a prepared staging to one side of the temporary bridge and the steel joists to form the floor were set in place. On weekdays thereafter the steel joists were riveted to the main girders, and then encased in concrete to form the floor, which was waterproofed by covering with bituminous sheeting.

On Sunday, April 9, in the second phase, the line was completely cut—both up and down tracks being removed by a steam crane—and the new span, complete with deck or floor, and weighing about 350 tons, was slewed with the aid of hand winches into position over temporary runways of steel balls running between old rails. After being rolled into position, the span had to be lowered about 4 in. on to its bearings. This was accomplished by the use of sand-jacks—a simple arrangement of piston and cylinder—by which movements to very fine limits are obtained by the controlled release of sand from an opening in the base of the cylinder.

The Free Kirk Bridge reconstructed as a permanent concrete and steel deck structure carrying normal traffic

Reconstructing the culvert near Ayton

In addition to these bridges, the rehabilitation of the railway included the reconstruction of the culvert carrying the Horn Burn under the railway, near Ayton, where the flood waters had been impounded to a depth of some 26 ft. against the railway embankment. At this place, the clay in the 52-ft. embankment had been under water pressure for some weeks before the water level could be lowered and the burn diverted through a temporary culvert. As it was necessary to reconstruct the culvert close to its original position, the hazards of tunnelling through clay in an unstable condition, due to the absorption of water, had to be reduced by the application of electro-osmosis to dry out the material. In this work the Department of Scientific & Industrial Research gave much valuable assistance. The new culvert, 200 ft. long, was constructed with precast reinforced concrete segments, similar to those used in building the London tube railway tunnels, and is being lined internally with reinforced concrete placed in situ to give a finished internal diameter of 9 ft.

Repairs near Burnmouth

Another item in the 1948 flood damage was a slip which occurred in the embankment at milepost 53½, near Burnmouth. At this point the main line runs close to the sea, but about 200 ft. above the foreshore. As a temporary measure, the line was at first moved some 8 ft. to the landward side to permit the passage of trains at reduced speed. Boring operations here proved the presence of rock at reasonable depth below the railway formation, and mass concrete retaining walls and a bridge have now been constructed as a permanent repair. 400 yd. of new retaining wall built on the landward side has allowed the tracks to be re-aligned here for normal high-speed running.

Speed restrictions lifted

The speed restrictions which have operated since October, 1948, over the seven bridges, and between Burnmouth and Berwick-on-Tweed, were lifted when the summer timetable came into operation, and, later, when full speed is also permissible over the embankment at Ayton culvert, a chapter of events unprecedented in the history of railways in Britain will be brought to a close.

Plants are incredibly diverse, and so are botanists! In its mission to spread fascinating stories about the plant world, Botany One also introduces you to the scientists behind these great stories.

Today, we have Dr Sean Hoban. Hoban thinks of himself as a conservation biologist first: he wants to understand how plants and their populations work, but what he ultimately seeks is to see that knowledge used in the real world. His team integrates ideas and tools from botany, evolution, and ecology to help conserve plants both in situ and ex situ.

Hoban is a Senior Scientist and Tree Conservation Biologist at The Morton Arboretum near Chicago. The Arboretum has been researching and conserving trees for more than a hundred years, while also working with communities and educators to foster a love of trees and to share knowledge about tending to them, so it’s a good home for me. His research includes understanding small and declining populations, identifying threats to genetic diversity, prioritising plant species for conservation, and figuring out how well seed banks and botanic gardens conserve diversity. Hoban is also interested in seed dispersal, biogeography, and how forests respond to increasing drought and heat.

One of the most central areas of Hoban's work focuses on translating conservation science into practical tools, including conservation gap analyses for botanic gardens and indicators for monitoring genetic diversity at national and global scales, such as under the Convention on Biological Diversity. You can follow his work at his lab website, LinkedIn and Bluesky.

What made you become interested in plants?

I think it started with two things happening when I was young. One was becoming aware of the deforestation crisis. Amazon deforestation was frequently in the news when I was growing up, and I remember my shock at the scale of what was being lost. It was probably my first realisation that something as enormous and vital for life as a forest could be threatened by humans.

At the same time, I did a lot of hiking and camping in my teenage years. I fell in love with being under a tree canopy and started paying attention to the trees themselves, not just forests as scenery, but individual species and how different they could be. I became interested in looking closely at trees and thinking about how they survive: how they reproduce, tolerate stressful conditions, and persist for such long periods.

Those two interests, fascination with forests and concern about their loss, came together in my interest in plant conservation biology.

Hoban with a Quercus welshii individual in deep sand in Utah in 2017. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What motivated you to pursue your current area of research?

I’ve always been drawn toward applied science. I love understanding how the natural world works, but I also want science to be in the service of society. I want to help us become better caretakers of the world around us. That motivation has shaped the questions I choose and the scale at which I work.

It is also why a significant part of my research has moved toward conservation policy and developing tools that other people can actually use. For example, I’ve worked on conservation gap analyses that help identify which species and populations are not yet sufficiently protected by botanical gardens. I also helped create, test, improve, and train people on how to work with indicators of genetic diversity that countries can use to monitor and then take action to restore nature, under national and international commitments.

I love being a part of the process of moving from scientific knowledge to conservation action. And I love bringing more academic scientists out into the policy and management space. We can learn an enormous amount about genetic diversity, ecology, and threats to species, but ultimately we need to turn that knowledge into decisions: What should we conserve? How? And how can we tell whether our efforts are working?

Hoban in his role as a global conservation policy expert, attending the Convention on Biodiversity meeting in Cali, Colombia, in 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What is your favourite part of your work related to plants?

I love seeds. I am fascinated by seeing a plant produce seeds, watching how those seeds disperse, and then seeing seedlings establish and grow. A seed is such a small piece of life, but inside it is the species’ genetic inheritance and the possibility of a whole new plant.

Seeds also connect many parts of my research. Dispersal determines how populations are connected across landscapes, and across time, from years to millennia. Seed collection is one of our important tools for ex situ conservation and something I’ve been privileged to take part in, actually helping add to the living collections at The Morton Arboretum. And regeneration in wild populations, whether seedlings can establish and survive, is fundamental to whether a population or forest will persist into the future.

There is also something wonderful about seeing and holding (and planting) seeds. A lot of my work involves genetic data, computer models, large datasets, and conservation policy, which can become abstract. Finding seeds, or seeing seedlings emerge, brings everything back to the plant itself. It is a reminder of what the analyses and conservation plans are intended to protect.

Sean holding two seeds of the threatened butternut tree in 2024, a finding that sparked subsequent intensive research into the ecology of seed production and regeneration of this tree. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Are any specific plants or species that have intrigued or inspired your research? If so, what are they and why?

I’ve worked intensively with the butternut tree (Juglans cinerea) for 20 years, and it is a tree that continues to inspire me. Butternut is a North American tree with considerable ecological and historical importance, but its populations have declined severely because of an introduced fungal disease, butternut canker.

What fascinates me is that butternut is a survivor. The disease and decline situation is serious, but unlike the American chestnut, butternut has not been devastated to quite the same degree. Mature trees still survive, reproduce, and, under the right conditions, can thrive. That means there is real potential for conservation and restoration.

A lot of our team’s and our collaborators’ work now asks what those " right conditions " are. How much light does butternut need? What forest management practices help it regenerate? What soils and habitats are best? And how much of survival and disease response is related to genetics?

I like working on a species where there are difficult conservation problems but also genuine reasons for optimism. With enough knowledge and effort, we have an opportunity to help this tree persist and recover. If you are reading this and interested in participating, you can be part of it!

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Could you share an experience or anecdote from your work that has marked your career and reaffirmed your fascination with plants?

One of the things I’ve enjoyed most about working on butternut is not a single field experience, but the community of people around the tree. I’ve had the opportunity to work with an extraordinary variety of people: researchers, students, retired foresters, land managers, naturalists, and people who love the land for agriculture or for bird watching, as well as Martin Kratt, one of the Kratt Brothers, who has become involved in butternut conservation.

Two colleagues of mine now lead a monthly conference call that brings together about 30 butternut researchers and enthusiasts across the continent. Some people have spent decades working with this species and bring an incredible depth of knowledge; others are students or people encountering these questions for the first time. It is a very supportive and affirming group, united by wanting to understand and conserve this tree.

That experience has reminded me that conservation is collaborative. No single researcher or institution has all the knowledge needed to save a species. Plants can bring together people with very different backgrounds and expertise, sometimes over generations. The community and our shared sense that we might help butternut recover is continually rewarding and reaffirming.

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois, 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What advice would you give young scientists considering a career in plant biology?

Be interdisciplinary. Some of the most interesting and important questions in plant biology are between traditional fields, and many conservation problems cannot be solved from a single perspective.

My own work has taken me through genetics, ecology, botany, computer modeling, biogeography, fieldwork, seed conservation, and eventually policy. I didn’t begin my career knowing that I would work across all of those areas. Often I learned something because it was the tool I needed to answer the next question.

I would encourage young scientists to develop strong expertise in something, but not let that expertise be a boundary. Learn quantitative and computational skills, but also learn natural history. Spend time in the field. Talk to people in other disciplines. Learn how conservation practitioners and policymakers think about problems by talking to them in person. And learn to communicate your science to people who don’t share your speciality.

Be widely curious. Being willing to cross disciplinary boundaries can lead you to questions and new areas of work that you would never have anticipated.

Hoban with Quercus havardii in Texas. This species is adapted to sand dunes and typically grows to about half a meter tall. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What do people usually get wrong about plants?

I think people often underestimate how much diversity exists within a plant species. We tend to think of a species as a single thing: a white oak is a white oak, and conserving the species means making sure enough individuals survive. But individuals and populations can be genetically very different from one another. A population at the dry edge of a species’ range may contain different adaptations from one growing in a cooler or wetter environment. Losing populations can therefore mean losing unique genetic diversity even when the species itself has not gone extinct.

I experienced this recently when seeing beech trees from across Europe in a common garden. Their leaf colour, growth form, and response to drought varied dramatically by population! That matters so much in a rapidly changing world. Conservation isn’t only about keeping species on the planet; it is also about maintaining enough variation within species for them to remain resilient and continue evolving.

I also think plants are often perceived as passive. But when you study their genetics, reproduction, behaviour, responses, and movement across landscapes, you see dynamic stories: migration, adaptation, hybridisation, population expansion and contraction, and exchanges of genes over large distances. A forest may look still, but biologically and evolutionarily it is living and changing.

Even for Desperate Restaurants, Fake Reviews Just Aren&#8217;t Worth It

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Yelp was first launched in 2004, it flopped. The company’s initial model of an email-based referral service failed to take off until its creators realized users were taking the platform into their own hands; in lieu of sending one another recommendations via email chain, they began using a “hidden” link at the bottom of the startup’s website to write unsolicited reviews of everything from doctors to restaurants. Yelp 2.0 launched in 2005, and gave those reviews a more visible place to live online — one that emboldened users to feel more authentic stewardship, by way of direct authorship, over hyping up the businesses they enjoyed (or didn’t). The startup bought a whopping 20-million-business database, and amplified a world of reviews outside of the restaurant- critic echo chamber. It was a fresh democratization of information, which was itself one of the more optimistic legacies of the Y2K internet. 

It’s been over 20 years since Yelp was founded. At its best, the platform still feels like a place where the voices of hungry, curious diners can share their personal experiences and offer recommendations and tips; at its worst, it feels like a bloated repository for reviews filled with misdirected anger, unreasonable expectations, or else completely fake customer reviews. Initially, unsubstantiated and unreliable reviews were more often written by a person — either someone from the business itself, or paid by the business; now, a fake review can be penned by a paid influencer, a detailed generative AI prompt, or purchased from third-party agencies that will produce fake bot reviews in bulk.

Fake reviews were perhaps inevitable. Bot-driven comments became ubiquitous during an overripe moment in the untamed internet’s lifespan. But how does a restaurant shamed for gamifying its reviews — even if it was, presumably, out of desperation in a tough economic landscape — regain customers’ broken trust? 

… The offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp.

In 2026’s AI-addled digital ecosystem, researchers are starting to understand even more about how paid-for, planted reviews impact the restaurants behind them, and whether trying to gamify platforms to achieve high ratings is worth it at all for businesses. A recently published working paper on the consequences of restaurants flagged by Yelp for fake (planted, incentivized) reviews found that since 2023, more than 4,900 businesses have been flagged with Yelp’s “Consumer Alert” banner, which exists on the penalized business’ page for a 90-day period and lets Yelp users know that the business has been caught peddling fake reviews. In 2020, the fast-casual chain Burgerim was found guilty of planting fake reviews, and received over 200 digital warning banners across its various locations’ Yelp pages.

The impacts of that digital pillory extend far beyond its 90-day duration. Yi Cao is an assistant professor of accounting at George Mason University, and the co-author of the paper; almost immediately, Cao noticed the offending restaurant’s foot traffic went down by 8 percent, and dipped even lower around the holidays, a time when would-be diners rely even more on Yelp. Cao’s team cross-analyzed the business’ performance on Yelp with real-life foot traffic reports (from global location data firm SafeGraph) and credit card transaction data (courtesy of Consumer Edge), and discovered that most of the penalized restaurants struggled to recover to pre-alert traffic even after a year and a half. The stink of chicanery lingered. 

While Cao says his team doesn’t know every signal that Yelp uses to identify fake reviews, they do know that it relies “substantially on reports from consumers and business owners,” and that “they also have an algorithm running in the background to screen reviews for AI-generated language — which is a sign of possible review inflation.” At this point, the cost of planting a good review is only getting steeper as the technology capable of detecting it improves. “This is a classical theory of a trade-off between the benefits of manipulating reputation and the risks of getting caught,” Cao explains, “When the risk is relatively low, the business owners decide the incremental benefits are worth the risk.”

Yelp’s real restaurant reviews don’t necessarily need to be etched in stone, but as AI-penned reviews become less detectable and more sophisticated, what diners want now, more than ever, is a human touch and a sense of authenticity.

Una mattinata a Gallarate, questa volta fermandosi

Per una storia un po’ lunga da spiegare, ci siamo ritrovati a Gallarate con un paio di classi del corso turistico del nostro Istituto “Erminio Maggia” di Stresa. Un’uscita didattica per conoscere il territorio intorno a noi, anche quei luoghi che attraversiamo spesso senza fermarci.

Da Gallarate sono passato parecchie volte, in autostrada o in treno, ma non mi ero praticamente mai fermato. Salvo qualche incursione al Galaxy Grill, per andare a mangiare da Gianni, ma questa è un’altra storia.

Questa volta, invece, abbiamo visitato il centro storico. La prima impressione è stata quella di una cittadina pulita e tranquilla, con una zona pedonale in cui si passeggia volentieri. Niente traffico, persone che vanno al lavoro, uffici, bar e diversi locali dove fermarsi per uno spuntino o un pranzo. Anche se, essendo martedì, parecchi ristoranti sembravano chiusi.

Si intuisce che la vita del centro ruota molto intorno agli uffici e ai servizi. I palazzi ottocenteschi, dall’aria signorile, si mescolano alle costruzioni del Novecento, con accostamenti talvolta un po’ dissonanti. Poi si incontrano una piccola chiesa romanica, davvero graziosa, e una grande chiesa dalle forme classiche. E soprattutto c’è il MA*GA, il museo d’arte contemporanea: molto bello, una delle ragioni per fermarsi a Gallarate e dedicarle un po’ di tempo.

Naturalmente, non poteva mancare qualche assaggio. Abbiamo provato gli amaretti di Gallarate: piuttosto grossi, buoni, decisamente impegnativi. Dolci che non passano inosservati. E poi i Galletti di Gallarate, una particolare pasta ripiena, bianca e rossa, che richiama il simbolo della città.

Camminando, si percepisce anche una città che si è trasformata. Il passato industriale affiora tra gli edifici e gli spazi vicini al centro, dove alle fabbriche sono subentrate altre funzioni: abitazioni, uffici, attività e laboratori artigianali. È l’impressione di una mattinata, naturalmente, ma il cambiamento si lascia leggere nel paesaggio urbano.

Non mancano le contraddizioni del presente. Nei pressi di un centro Caritas c’erano alcune persone in attesa, forse di un pasto o di un aiuto. Anche qui, nella tranquillità di una città di provincia, si incontrano le questioni che attraversano il mondo: migrazioni, lavoro, povertà, integrazione.

Nel complesso, però, Gallarate mi ha lasciato una sensazione piacevole. Mi è sembrata una cittadina in cui tornare un sabato, passeggiare per il centro, visitare il museo e fermarsi a mangiare in un ristorante.

Senza programmi complicati. Questa volta, almeno, invece di passarci soltanto, mi sono fermato. E ne è valsa la pena.

Quando cammini in montagna a cercare porcini, trovi spesso un sacco di funghi rotti: presi a bastonate, schiacciati con un piede, fatti a pezzi e lasciati lì. Sono quelli che chiamiamo, un po’ sbrigativamente, “funghi matti”: quelli che non raccogliamo perché non commestibili, tossici o semplicemente sconosciuti. Ma che ragione c’è di romperli? Non si possono lasciare interi, dove sono?

Non tutto quello che cresce nel bosco deve per forza finire nel nostro cestino. E il fatto che un fungo non serva a noi non significa che non serva a niente. Il bosco non è un supermercato e noi non siamo gli incaricati di eliminare la merce che non ci piace. Questi funghi distrutti mi ricordano le firme lasciate con la bomboletta sui muri delle case. Il gesto, in fondo, mi sembra simile: una cosa non è tua, ma ti prendi ugualmente il diritto di rovinarla. Come se fosse necessario lasciare una traccia del proprio passaggio, anche soltanto facendo un danno. Desiderio di immortalità? Ben poca cosa direi…

Vandalismo, insomma. Anche senza un muro di mezzo.

Mi domando se dietro ci sia qualcosa di radicato nella specie umana: quella smania di intervenire, di imporsi, di guastare ciò che si potrebbe tranquillamente lasciare com’è. Forse esiste una spiegazione psicologica o antropologica che non conosco.

Intanto, però, una domanda molto semplice rimane: se quel fungo non ti interessa, perché non passi oltre?
