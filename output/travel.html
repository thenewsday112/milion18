Sam O’Brien

On a fall afternoon in Halachó, a small town in the Mexican state of Yucatán, Wilson Alonzo stood before a smoldering mound of dirt and began digging. Slowly clearing away branches, leaves, and soil, the chef unearthed a large disc enclosed in banana leaves. He peeled the wrapping back and examined the golden edges of the round tamale inside. He nodded. It was ready for the altar.

Every October and November, Yucatecans like Alonzo gather at the edges of underground ovens and cook mukbil pollo (“buried chicken”), a sacred tamale made only for the Yucatán Peninsula’s Day of the Dead. Known as Janal Pixán (“Food for the Souls” in Yucatec Maya) or Los Finados, the holiday welcomes returning spirits: children on October 31, adults on November 1, and all spirits on November 2, followed by a farewell feast eight—or in some cases, 30—days later. Above anything else that might grace an ofrenda, which could include anything from hot chocolate to relleno negro to beer, mukbil pollo is the star that occupies every altar’s center.

“It is the absolute highlight of the offering,” Alonzo said. One bite confirms why: The crispy, caramelized crust of lard-enriched masa gives way to a smoky, savory mouthful of chicken bathed in a brothy crimson sauce of achiote, tomato, sweet and spicy peppers, epazote, onions, and garlic.

Sam O’Brien

But Alonzo—whose restaurant, Ya’axche, specializes in traditional Yucatecan dishes—said it’s not mukbil pollo’s flavor that nourishes spirits; instead of eating it, they consume the tamale through its aroma. And a crucial part of that aroma, he added, comes from the píib. Dating back to the ancient Maya, this underground cooking technique uses wood, stones, and leaves to impart a slightly herbaceous, smoky succulence to dishes like mukbil pollo—also known as pibipollo or pib—and cochinita pibil.

“Cooking it in a gas oven simply isn’t the same,” Alonzo said. “You lose the unique aromas and flavors imparted by the earth itself.”

Not everyone sees the píib as essential. In cities, where outdoor space is limited, most tamales are baked indoors. When Steffan Igor Ayora-Díaz, a retired anthropologist, was growing up in the city of Valladolid, his mother made mukbil pollo at home, then brought it to a local bakery to cook in an industrial oven. “Nobody said, ‘Oh, it has to be in a píib,’ ” he explained.

Ayora-Díaz now lives in the Yucatán state capital of Mérida, where he said beliefs surrounding Janal Pixán lean more secular. Not only do most eschew the píib, but he and many of his friends no longer build ofrendas, instead celebrating the holiday by gathering with family over a meal. Mukbil pollo is still important, but it plays a role more analogous to turkey on Thanksgiving. “We’re still going to eat it because that’s the food that you eat on these days, but you’re not really thinking of dead people when you’re eating it,” he said.

Sam O’Brien

While purists decry these nontraditional approaches to the holiday, Ayora-Díaz points out that mukbil pollo is a blend of ancient and modern. “It’s a ‘traditional’ dish, but I’m putting ‘traditional’ in quotes because it’s relatively new,” he said. The Maya have been cooking in píibs and eating tamales for millennia, but ingredients like garlic, onions, and lard arrived with the Spanish conquistadors in the 1500s. And, according to Ayora-Díaz, mukbil pollo only started appearing in written records in the 1900s.

Janal Pixán, too, is a hybrid of sorts, blending Christian rituals for All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day with Maya beliefs surrounding death and ancestor veneration. In Xocén, a small village of largely Yucatec Maya residents about 20 minutes outside Valladolid, honoring the dead can mean not only praying for their souls at Catholic mass but welcoming them into one’s home as guests and offering them the same hospitality as the living.

Sam O’Brien

“We are dealing with spirits who require respect and specific observances, and who actually come to visit us,” Miguel Tun, a Yucatec Maya tour guide, said. “Even the yards look clean during this time. You walk past someone’s house and see that everything is spotless because visitors are coming.”

Tun lives in a modern home next to his parents’ palapa in Xocén. The Tuns, like many Yucatec Maya, make their tamales only for the biix, or eighth day of Janal Pixán, when souls are believed to return to purgatory. In Xocén, the local version of mukbil pollo is perfectly suited for this journey. Called chachak waaj (“red tamale” in Yucatec Maya), it’s like a small hand pie.

“The idea is that the souls can easily carry the food with them,” Tun said. “It’s about seeing them off. It’s like when people travel—say, from abroad—to visit, and we give them a gift.” 

This gift isn’t just the tamale itself but the time spent creating it. I visited the Tuns to learn how to make chachak waaj from scratch. We began in the morning. After shredding chicken, slow-cooking sauce, mixing manteca (rendered pork lard) into masa, assembling each tamale, and wrapping them in banana leaves, we needed headlamps to navigate the píib in the darkness. 

Sam O’Brien

“If you are going to make an offering as part of a spiritual practice, it has to be something into which you have poured your effort, you have sacrificed your time,” he said. “That holds significant meaning within the Maya worldview.”

The hours spent making chachak waaj also provide an opportunity to reminisce about the dead. “It’s a moment where the whole family is together, remembering things while cooking, making preparations, or kneading the dough,” Tun said. “Sometimes the conversation is about what’s happening in the community, but there are moments where someone makes a comment about something the deceased person used to do or say.”

Sam O’Brien

At 9:30 p.m., Tun and his father returned from the backyard píib carrying several trays of tamales. It was time for the final step. The Tuns, several cousins, and an aunt gathered on plastic chairs and tree stumps and started unwrapping. They inspected each crust, making sure it was sufficiently crispy (the ones that weren’t would head back to the píib). As they worked, scattering charred leaves at their feet, they talked about an upcoming dance recital for one of their cousins.

Later Tun told me this is, for him, the most important part of Janal Pixán. “Even though a loved one is gone, you demonstrate strength, you show that the family remains united,” he said. “You want that person, when they arrive, to see everyone together, to see that bond. That’s what matters most. I believe that’s the true essence of the Day of the Dead.”

From the Archives: 1982 – The Day the Queen Mother Named Class 47 No. 47 541 at Aberdeen

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

On Wednesday, October 20, 1982, the Queen Mother named diesel-electric locomotive No. 47 541 The Queen Mother at Aberdeen Station. In this article from the January 1983 issue of The Railway Magazine, Patrick Kingston recounts the ceremony, the preparations and an unexpected visit to the locomotive’s cab.

A historic occasion at Aberdeen

A COLOURFUL event in railway history took place at a ceremony at Aberdeen Station during the late afternoon of Wednesday, October 20, 1982, when a class “47/4” diesel-electric locomotive became the first British railway engine to be named by a member of the Royal Family. The ceremony was performed by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, following postponement, because of a national railway strike, when the naming was to have taken place at Glasgow Central on June 30.

Above: HM Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother stands beside No. 47 541, “The Queen Mother”, at Aberdeen Station on October 20, just after she had named the locomotive and unveiled nameplate and heraldic plaque.

Preparing the station

The setting for the ceremony was the north end of Platform 6 at what was called Aberdeen Joint Station which, in pre-grouping days before 1923, was shared by the Caledonian and Great North of Scotland companies. To help brighten up what is normally a drab part of the station, the immediate area of the ceremony had been cleaned with the decorative columns and canopy being freshly painted. An area of the platform was covered with red carpet and to complete the picture a floral arrangement was provided by Aberdeen City Council. Aberdeen is now an “open” station without ticket barriers so passengers and public alike were able to witness the occasion from behind some specially arranged crowd barriers.

The arrival of No. 47 541

Meanwhile the locomotive that was to be the focus of attention arrived from nearby Ferryhill shed—it was No. 47 541, the number being duly scribbled down in the notebooks of many press reporters! For a locomotive built in 1963, the 19 years of many miles of travel were forgotten, 47 541 looking as though it had only just been completed—such was the immaculate appearance of an engine that has already borne two numbers, first D1755 and then 47 161. An air of festivity was created by the arrival of the orchestra of the Aberdeen Strathspey & Reel Society who played selections of Scottish music to entertain the guests and those who had gathered to watch. On the adjacent Platform 7 there was some concern when the 15.50 to Inverness was delayed about ten minutes to maintain a connection for passengers arriving on a late-running train from the south. Sadly, for passengers on the Inverness-bound train, the connecting train arrived as the final preparations were made and with a wave of the guard’s green flag their train disappeared from view beneath the modern concrete development that now spans the station’s northern approaches.

The Queen Mother arrives

Moments later the Royal limousine arrived through an archway onto an area of the station that was once a bay platform, now filled-in as a result of the rationalisation of the ex-Great North of Scotland lines northwards from Aberdeen. Smiling and wearing a coat in a delicate shade of her favourite colour—blue—the Queen Mother stepped from her car to meet local dignitaries and railway officials including Harold Taylor, the Works Manager at Swindon where the nameplate that was to be unveiled had been made. On a dais, in front of the locomotive, a speech of welcome was given by Ken Peters, a member of British Rail Scotland’s Regional Board.

Naming The Queen Mother

In reply the Queen Mother said it was a great honour to know that a locomotive would bear her name and she was also happy that the ceremony was taking place at Aberdeen—a station so frequently used by members of her family. Concluding her speech she said: “May God bless all those who work in her and travel behind this great locomotive. I now have great pleasure in naming 47 541 The Queen Mother.” To much applause the Royal grandmother stood proudly beside “her” locomotive. Then followed a presentation of an “O”-gauge working model of the locomotive she had just named which she said that she was sure the younger members of the Royal Family “will love”.

An unexpected cab visit

The Queen Mother then met Ferryhill driver James Stephen and his wife. This was followed by an impromptu visit to the cab, into which the Queen Mother climbed from the platform by using a wooden box as a makeshift step. Sitting in his seat, Driver Stephen explained the controls, then the Queen Mother asked to see the engine room. Driver Stephen opened the engine-room door, but tactfully explained that it would be impractical for her to enter.

The ceremony concludes

After leaving the cab the Queen Mother stood to admire, once more, 47 541, “her” locomotive, and then thanked all who had contributed to such a happy occasion before being driven back to resume her holiday at Birkhall on Deeside. In conclusion my sincere thanks to R. Gemmell, BR Director of Public Affairs (Scotland) and R. R. Marshall and his staff of the Scottish Information Office for the special facilities granted to me.

Moelleux of Summer Fruits

📅 Sep 12, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

This may or may not happen to you, but sometimes when I’m a guest in someone’s home, where everyone pitches in to make dinner, all eyes (or just the host’s eyes) fall on me to make dessert. Gulp. I happy to pitch in with the cooking or bringing along a few baked goodies for the weekend, but I’m not always prepared to bake on the spot.

If you’re lucky, there may be a scale, a cake pan, a sharp knife, and hopefully sugar, butter, eggs, and flour. If you’re really lucky, there might be a whisk and a spatula, too. But if you’ve ever tried to peel a dozen apples with a steak knife because it’s the only one that’s even close to sharp, or eyeball 1 cup/140g) of flour, you know what I’m talking about. I once did a post on Things I Bring When I’m a Guest for the Weekend, which raised the ire of some, but I require a minimum of a knife, a spatula, a bowl, and a cake pan if I’m going to bake something, like a cake.

You’re likely familiar with the Moelleux au chocolat, the soft warm individual melting cakes that everybody loves. (If you have the kind of friends that have six to eight ramekins, well, that’s great.) I recently saw French food writer and journalist François-Régis Gaudy (his book, Let’s Eat France, translated into English, is a lot of fun and pretty amazing) make a Moelleux à la abricot on his Instagram page, which didn’t require a mixer or any special equipment. Not even a whisk!

His Apricot Cake was a very casual affair, baked in a rectangular mold on a sheet of parchment paper, and came out of the oven rather free-form, looking exactly like something that would come out of a French home cook’s kitchen, when baking dessert for friends. I’m fine with that but didn’t know if you were, so I thought I’d tinker with it and make it in a cake pan, which is easier to slice. I also fiddled around with the ingredients, as well as putting the brown granulated sugar on top (rather than underneath), to give the moelleux a nice sheen.

I used a springform cake pan but you can use any similar-sized vessel; an 8-, 9- or 10-square or round cake pan would work, or even a baking dish. The baking time will vary but if your experience with using a variety of ovens in other people’s homes is anything like mine, you’ll find that each one is different, and what one says is 350ºF/180ºC will be different than another whose dial says 350ºF/180ºC. I don’t travel with an oven thermometer but may add that to things I bring when I’m staying with people.

Tip: If you’re staying in someone’s place for the weekend, you can mix the dry ingredients in a container or zip-top bag then put the dessert together when you get there.

I made one cake with apricots first, which was sensational, but knowing fresh apricots are harder to come by elsewhere, I made another one with plums, which came out great as well. It would work with other summer fruits and berries but I really like the tanginess of apricots or plums against the moist cake layer, so stuck with those two. A handful of berries wouldn’t hurt either, but I do recommend keeping it simple.

Print

Moelleux aux fruits d'ete

Inspired by a recipe from French food writer and journalist François-Régis Gaudry The ground almonds are really delicious in the dessert, but if you can't get ground almonds (sometimes called almond meal or almond flour), you can use 1 1/4 cups (175g) of all-purpose flour in place of the mix. The picture in the post of the batter shows 3 eggs being added, and I took that photo when I first tested the recipe, which I did a few more times after that. The final recipe that I came up with, below, uses 2 eggs. Course Dessert Servings 8 servings

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds (680g) apricots or plums (or another summer fruit, see headnote)
  • 3/4 cup, plus 2 tablespoons (100g) almond flour
  • 1/2 cup (65g) all-purpose flour
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking powder preferably aluminum-free
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup plus 2 tablespoons (230g) granulated sugar
  • 5 ounces (140g) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 2 large eggs at room temperature
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract (or 1/2 teaspoon almond extract)
  • 1 1/2 tablespoons coarse granulated brown sugar such as tubinado sugar (or granulated white sugar)

Instructions

  • Halve the fruit and remove the pits. Slice the fruit into 3/4- to 1-inch (2-3cm) wedges. Set aside. Preheat the oven to 350ºF (180ºC.) Grease a 9-inch (23cm) springform pan very well with butter or non-stick spray. (If you don't have a springform pan, you can use another cake pan but it may be harder to get the cake out so you may want to also line the bottom with parchment paper.)
  • In a medium mixing bowl, stir together the almond flour, all-purpose flour, baking powder, salt, and sugar. (My preferred tool to use for this step is a silicone spatula.) Add the soft butter and mix it in with the spatula until it's broken up into little pieces, roughly the size of kernels of corn. Stir in the eggs and vanilla extract, until it's almost smooth. It's fine if there are small pieces of butter visible.
  • Scrape the batter into the prepared pan and smooth the top. Arrange the wedges of fruit on their sides in concentric circles over the batter, snugly placing the wedges against each other, pressing them gently into the batter as you go. Avoid putting them right up against the sides of the pan. It's best to leave some room for the batter to rise between the fruit and the pan, to avoid juices from the fruit adhering to the sides of the pan as the moelleux bakes. (It's unavoidable - and natural - if using juicy fruits, like plums, to have juice run out. But placing the slices not right up against the sides of the pan will help you get the cake out of the pan later.)
  • Sprinkle the coarse sugar over the top and bake until the center just feels set; a toothpick inserted into the center should come out free of cake crumbs stuck to it, about 1 hour. Remove from the oven and set on a cooling rack. If any fruit juices have bubbled up and stuck to the sides of the pan, run a knife around the outside of the cake, which will help it release later.

Notes

Notes: If you want to make this gluten-free, I'd go with the almond flour rendition, and use a gluten-free flour substitute in place of the wheat flour. If you want to reduce the sugar in the recipe, you could cut it by 6 tablespoons/75g and skip the sugar on top, although I wouldn't.

BAKED MAC ‘N’ CHEESE

SERVES: 4

INGREDIENTS
• 1 tbsp avocado oil
• 1 onion, chopped
• 2 garlic cloves, chopped
• Sprinkle of salt and pepper
• 1 tbsp hempseeds
• 1-8 oz. box chickpea pasta (serves 4)
• 3 cups whole milk

• 2 tbsp cassava flour
• 1 tbsp Dijon mustard
• 1⁄2 cup raw Gruyere cheese
• 1⁄2 cup raw sharp cheddar
• 1⁄2 cup raw sheep’s milk
• 1⁄2 cup raw Manchego
• 2 cups cooked broccoli

DIRECTIONS
1. Add olive oil to a pan over medium heat and begin to sauté the onion and garlic until cooked through and slightly browned. Add a pinch of salt and pepper.
2. In a medium saucepan, bring water to boil and cook chickpea pasta according to directions on the box.
3. **Pre-heat oven to 400 F while pasta finishes cooking.
4. Once pasta is finished, strain it and use the saucepan to begin making the cheese mixture.
5. Over medium heat, add mustard and milk, and slowly begin to add in the coconut flour, whisking regularly.
6. Slowly drop in the cheese, adding a 1⁄2 cup at a time while continuing to stir.

7. Once all cheese is melted, add in the onion and garlic and mix well.
8. Stir in the broccoli and pasta until everything is coated evenly.
9. Finally, coat a ceramic or oven-safe dish with non-stick spray, and place the mac ‘n’ cheese in the dish.
10. Top with hemp seeds and place in the oven for about 15 minutes.
11. Remove from the oven when the top is lightly browned, plate it, and enjoy!

SAVORY OVEN ROASTED CARROTS

SERVES: 4-6

INGREDIENTS
Carrots
• 2-3 large bunches carrots
• 2 tbsp olive oil
• 1⁄3 cup raw pumpkin seeds
• 1⁄4 cup pomegranate seeds
• Freshly ground sea salt, to taste
• Freshly ground black pepper,
to taste
• Fresh parsley to garnish

Zesty Yogurt Sauce
• 1 cup plain Greek yogurt
• 2 tsp fresh garlic, minced
• 1⁄4 cup tahini
• 1 tbsp fresh lemon juice
• 1⁄2 tsp ground cumin
• Freshly ground sea salt, to taste

DIRECTIONS
1. Preheat the oven to 375°F.
2. Wash, pat dry, peel, and cut the stems off the carrots.
3. On a large baking sheet, add the carrots and drizzle with olive oil, then sprinkle with salt and pepper, and place in the oven. Roast for about 20 minutes.

4. In a small blender or food processor, add all the ingredients for the yogurt sauce and
blend until smooth. You can slowly add a splash of water to help thin the mixture until you get to a smooth consistency.
5. When you have about 5 minutes left on the carrots, add the pumpkin seeds to a small baking sheet and allow them to roast for a few minutes.
6. When carrots are easy to pierce with a fork and look nice and roasted, remove from the oven. Add them to a dish. When you’re ready to serve, drizzle them with the yogurt sauce and sprinkle the pumpkin and pomegranate seeds all over. Garnish with parsley and add salt and pepper to taste.

APPLE CIDER THYME BEE'S KNEES

SERVES: 1

INGREDIENTS
• 4 fresh thyme sprigs
• 2 tbsp raw honey (you can sub with monk
fruit to make og sugar)
• 1 tbsp apple cider vinegar
• 2-3 lemons, juiced
• 1⁄2 cup water
• 2 oz gin (or vodka)
• Extra thyme and star anise to garnish
(optional)

DIRECTIONS
1. Add thyme, ACV, honey, and water in a pan over medium-high heat. Bring to a boil, then remove it from the heat. Stir regularly.
2. Let it cool, then strain the mixture.
3. Fill a shaker with ice and add the lemon juice, ACV mixture, and alcohol.
4. Shake her up and serve in a cute coupe glass with a sprig of thyme and star anise to garnish.
