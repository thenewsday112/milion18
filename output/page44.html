Switch 2 security takes a hit as hackers unlock homebrew access

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A security researcher has demonstrated a software-only partial jailbreak of the Switch 2, gaining "userland" access that he says "lets us run homebrew, reverse engineer and mod Switch 2 games, back up save files off the Switch, and access most of the Switch 2’s peripherals through MMIO."

The hack relies on the original Switch version of SmileBasic 4, the latest in a long-running series of programming environments and interpreters that run on Nintendo hardware. The so-called "tethered" hack also requires access to a macOS or Linux PC on the same network to run a node.js package that helps enable privileged system access.

Researcher Sammy Hajhamid writes that he used OpenAI's Codex to poke at SmileBasic's language interpreter, using a custom harness that could send USB commands to the system and view results via HDMI (as well as power cycling via a Bluetooth light switch flipping device). After "substantial effort for ~2 weeks," that harness discovered a 32-bit integer overflow that occurs when removing items from an array in SmileBasic. That error gives access to an array pointer, which in turn allows read-and-write access to portions of system memory.

This emulator reimagines Nintendo’s Virtual Boy with a full color display

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Last year, we reported on Red Viper, a Virtual Boy emulator that added a splash of Super Game Boy-style color to Nintendo's famously failed red-on-black "portable" 3D headset from the '90s. Now, a new emulation experiment, VBoy Color, has taken the concept to a higher level, using a tile-based palette system that allows for many more distinct colors in each individual Virtual Boy frame.

Rather than simply replacing the Virtual Boy's four shades of red with four different colors, VBoy Color's readme files explain how the emulator's game-specific Color Packs can assign a distinct four-color palette to each 8x8 tile in a Virtual Boy scene. That means the emulator can differentiate between green pipes, grey platforms, and red brick wall in Mario Clash, for instance, even though all those in-game objects share the same overlapping red hues in the original game.

The Game Boy Color-esque results feel like being transported into an alternate history where Nintendo invested in a full-color display rather than cheaper red LEDs for the Virtual Boy hardware. The increased contrast between differently colored elements is particularly welcome in games like Jack Bros. or Vertical Force, where the original red-on-red display often makes it hard to differentiate the player-character from busy red background elements.

Forget the pre-party panic and embrace the spooky; in this article on Halloween charcuterie board ideas, we’ll show you how to curate a haunted grazing table that’s visually striking, effortlessly assembled, and undeniably crave-worthy.

Think Zones, Not a Snack Formula

Skip the strict charcuterie rules. Build it like a haunted house guests wander through: a cauldron dip, a pumpkin patch, a crunchy graveyard, monster bites, a green swamp, a sweet corner, and maybe a witchy drink.

The goal is simple: give guests different flavors, textures, temperatures, and serving styles. Some people will want a hot bite. Some will want something creamy. Some will want fruit and crunch. Some will head straight for the cookie dough dip. That is the point.

Pick three or four zones for a smaller party, or go all in.

Start With One Big Visual Moment

Anchor your board with a single ‘showstopper’—a visual centerpiece that sets the eerie mood immediately. Instead of scattering themes randomly, let this one element dictate your color palette and vibe. 

For example, place a serving of Pumpkin Soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs as your pumpkin patch centerpiece, or set out a mini cauldron filled with Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso. Alternatively, build a sweet crypt anchored by Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough, or feature a dramatic pitcher of Ginger Citrus Mocktail as your witches’ brew. 

Once your anchor is set, it serves as the gravitational pull for the rest of your snacks, making it easy to arrange extras like tombstone bites or eyeball eggs around it.

Choose Two or Three Recipes, Max

You do not need seven different homemade dishes to impress; two or three are plenty to anchor a balanced spread. To build a cohesive board with zero overwhelm, pick one item from each of these three categories:

  • The Savory Anchor: Start with a hearty, dippable base like the Protein Pickle Dip or Cottage Cheese Queso.
  • The Substantial Bite: Choose one warm, protein-packed main, such as Air-Fryer Boneless Buffalo Wings or Chickpea Salad Sandwich bites for a plant-forward option.
  • The Sweet Finish: Balance the salt with something creamy like Cottage Cheese Cookie Dough or a refreshing Ginger Citrus Mocktail.

“A grazing table should still feel like enough food, not just a collection of bites. Adding a few protein-rich options, like deviled eggs, wings, yogurt-based dips, cheese, or chickpea bites, can help the spread feel more satisfying,” Emily Sullivan, RD, MyFitnessPal Registered Dietitian, says. (3) 

By sticking to this ‘one savory, one hearty, one sweet’ formula, you ensure a variety of textures and flavors while keeping your prep time manageable. Need more inspiration? You can browse endless options via MyFitnessPal Recipe Discovery.

Make It Feel Grown-Up

Halloween food gets cartoonish fast. Ground it with marinated olives, sharp cheese, spicy pickles, herbed nuts, whole-grain crackers, roasted red peppers, a fresh-herb dip, or a mocktail in a coupe glass.

Then add just one or two playful touches: olive spiders, candy eyes, or skeleton hands by the tongs.

Keep the food recognizable. Just give it a costume.

How To Arrange It

Start with anything in cups or bowls. That means the pumpkin soup cups, dips, olives, pickles, nuts, and sweets. These pieces give the board height and shape.

If you are serving soup in pumpkin-shaped mugs, set them on a tray or platter first so they are easier to move and refill.

Then place the warm or prepped foods, each in its own spot. Fill the gaps with crackers, apple slices, grapes, carrots, cucumbers, celery, pepper strips, berries, and mini chocolate.

Add the Halloween details last because cracker tombstones can go soggy fast. Keep candy in one corner so it looks intentional.

Keep It Safe

Refresh the board instead of letting it sit untouched for hours.

You do not need every item on the board to be themed. One strong visual moment, like pumpkin soup cups or a cauldron dip, can make the whole table feel festive.

“Cold foods should stay at 40°F or colder, hot foods should stay at 140°F or warmer, and perishable foods should be tossed if they have been out for more than 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold,” Sullivan says (2).

That covers dips, cheese, wings, and anything egg- or dairy-based.

Easy fix: put out smaller portions, keep backups in the fridge, and refresh the board halfway through the party.

Logging a Party Plate

Snap a photo of your plate before you eat. MyFitnessPal Meal Scan can identify foods so you are not guessing later.

You may still need to tweak portions manually. But it beats trying to remember a plate of wings, dip, crackers, fruit, and candy after the fact.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

  • What are good Halloween appetizers for adults?

Savory dips, mini sandwiches, wings, skewers, stuffed peppers, cheese, olives, spiced nuts, vegetables, crackers, and a sweet or two all work well. Aim for foods that are easy to eat one-handed while mingling. For more variety, include a mix of protein foods, dairy, vegetables, fruit, healthy fats such as nuts, seeds and olives,, and whole grains. (1)

  • How do I make a board look spooky?

Use dark trays, black bowls, cracker tombstones, olive spiders, candy eyes, skeleton hands, or a spooky label. You do not need every food to be themed. A few details can carry the whole board.

  • How far ahead can I prep?

Wash produce, make dips, mix the mocktail base, and cut bread or crackers ahead. Add sparkling water last, and wait to assemble anything crunchy until closer to serving.

  • How long can it sit out?

Perishable foods can sit out for up to 2 hours unless they are kept hot or cold. If the temperature is above 90°F, that window drops to 1 hour. (2)

Bottom Line

Skip the standard snack-board formula.

Build in zones: a pumpkin patch, cauldron dip, swamp crunch, monster bites, tombstone sandwiches, a sweet crypt, and a witches’ brew. Anchor it with a few MyFitnessPal recipes, fill the rest with easy staples, and add just enough spooky styling to make it feel festive.

The best board is the one people actually gather around.

Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This excerpt was originally published in Pre Shift, our newsletter for the hospitality industry. Subscribe for more first-person accounts, advice, and interviews.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel. 

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point. 

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it. 

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them. 

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.” 

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”
