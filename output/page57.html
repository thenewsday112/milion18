Eccentric training: strength and flexibility combined?

(Image: Steel Supplements)

A very interesting and cleverly designed new study has shown that eccentric training may improve flexibility just as effectively as static stretching. Participants completed a 10-week intervention, training three times per week. The researchers used a within-subject design, whereby one leg performed eccentric-only hamstring training while the other performed conventional static hamstring stretching. This meant that each participant effectively acted as their own control.

The results showed similar improvements in joint range of motion following both interventions. However, the eccentric-trained leg also experienced significantly greater increases in hamstring strength and muscle size, highlighting an important additional benefit of eccentric training.

Therefore, while both methods appear effective for improving hamstring flexibility, eccentric training may offer coaches and athletes a more time-efficient approach by simultaneously targeting flexibility, strength and muscle size.

The fitness tests used by Premiership rugby clubs

(Image: Men’s Health)

With the new Premiership Rugby season approaching, clubs have been putting their players through a range of gruelling pre-season fitness tests. A recent article published in The Times has shed some light on the various tests being used across the league.

Unsurprisingly, the Bronco Test remains a popular choice among Premiership clubs. The article reports that Gloucester and England centre Seb Atkinson recorded an impressive time of 4 minutes 8 seconds, believed to be one of the fastest Bronco performances ever recorded in professional rugby. Saracens are another club that utilise the Bronco Test, but they also complete an additional beep-style test immediately afterwards, known as the “Fight Test”. It appears that the Fight Test is designed to assess players’ work rate while already fatigued from the preceding Bronco Test, although details surrounding the exact protocol remain limited.

Gloucester, meanwhile, have introduced their own unique fitness test known as the “Shed Challenge”. The name comes from the club’s famous Shed stand at Kingsholm, which is approximately 105 metres long. Working in pairs, players begin by pushing a 105 kg sled along the 105-metre length of the Shed. They then flip and roll a tractor tyre over the distance before finishing the challenge by carrying sandbags up and down every set of stairs in the Shed.

While scientific research validating the Shed Challenge as a fitness test is understandably going to be difficult to find, there can be little doubt that it serves a purpose beyond simply testing physical fitness. By incorporating one of the most recognisable features of Kingsholm into the challenge, Gloucester have created a demanding physical test that also helps reinforce a strong and unique club identity.

So, while Premiership rugby clubs are utilising a mixture of traditional and more unconventional fitness tests during pre-season, the Bronco Test appears to remain one of the most popular choices. Maro Itoje’s secret to career longevity

Maro Itoje (Image: Yahoo Sports)

While we are on the topic of rugby, Maro Itoje has recently made headlines with his interesting take on career longevity (see here). Itoje skipped England’s July Test matches, giving him what he described as his first proper summer break in 12 years.

Having earned over 100 England caps and toured three times with the British & Irish Lions, Itoje has accumulated an enormous playing and travel load throughout his career. He believes the planned summer break has left him feeling physically and mentally refreshed and suggests that extended periods away from the sport could be key to prolonging international careers.

Interestingly, this story echoes a previous bulletin I wrote on cycling legend Tadej Pogačar (see here), whose philosophy was that sometimes “the best recovery can be to stay on the sofa and do nothing.” With the competitive and travel demands of elite sport continuing to increase, perhaps the secret to career longevity isn’t always doing more, but ensuring athletes have genuine periods of extended rest where they can simply switch off.

It will be interesting to see how Itoje performs this season following his first proper summer break in over a decade.

This is the Thai version of mapo tofu! Soft tofu in a sauce made with ground pork or chicken, shrimp, and any kind of vegetables you like. A great fridge cleanout recipe that's also kid friendly! Danubio Salato is a traditional Neapolitan recipe. Soft, light and buttery brioche buns are stuffed with a mix of cheese and ham. Our recipe uses mozzarella, smoked scamorza, and prosciutto cotto. A delicious pull-apart bread made for sharing.

Recipe overview

  • The recipe: a traditional Neapolitan pull-apart bread.
  • Flavour and texture: soft, light and buttery rolls with a savoury ham and cheese filling.
  • Prep and bake time: 1 hour 15 minutes.
  • Proofing time: around 6 hours.
  • Makes: 16 stuffed buns.
  • Perfect for: sharing with a crowd, lunch, parties, picnics, aperitivo.
Jump to recipe

Danubio Salato is such a fun recipe to make. The recipe comes from Naples, although the exact origin is unclear.

When I was researching the history of this recipe, I found so many different stories and versions of its origin. It’s very similar to Austrian Buchteln, which are filled with a sweet plum jam, but how it ended up in Naples is another story. It’s thought to have been created by Neapolitan pastry chef Mario (or Giovanni) Scaturchio, who created his own savoury version after visiting Austria in the 1920s. Some say it was in honour of his aunt; others say his wife? We’ll never know for sure.

Nowadays, it’s popular in Naples around Easter, Christmas and for celebrations. Danubio is perfect for sharing, and you can have so much fun with the fillings (see more options and ideas on that below).

Ingredient notes

  • Flour: Use strong bread flour if possible. I used Canadian bread flour, which is similar to Italian Manitoba flour. You can also use Italian 00 flour.
  • Butter: Make sure it’s unsalted and at room temperature so it incorporates into the dough well.
  • Eggs: You need 2 egg yolks for the dough and 1 whole egg for the egg wash before baking. You can use your 2 leftover egg whites to make Ricciarelli cookies or freeze them.
  • Yeast: I use fast-action yeast, which can be added directly into the flour. Check if your yeast needs to be activated in liquid first (use some of the milk from the recipe to do this).
  • Cheese and ham: we use mozzarella, smoked scamorza and prosciutto cotto (ham) for the filling. See more options below. I prefer using a low-moisture block of mozzarella rather than grated because it melts better, but use what you have available.

Filling variations

When it comes to cheese, especially mozzarella, make sure you use something with low moisture. Fresh mozzarella would be too wet for the filling. I’ve been testing this with pre-grated mozzarella but found that the best option was a low-moisture block of mozzarella like the Galbani version pictured in my ingredient photo.

  • Meat: You can use ham, salami or any cured meats you like. Cooked sausage is also a great option.
  • Cheese: Use a low-moisture cheese (avoid fresh mozzarella). Fontina, gorgonzola, provolone, smoked scamorza, taleggio are all great options. Use something that melts well, too.
  • Veg: Sun-dried tomatoes (pat dry of excess oil), olives (either chopped or tapenade), wilted spinach or greens (make sure they have as little moisture as possible), mushrooms.
  • Pesto: I’ve tested this with a combination of mozzarella and homemade basil pesto. It worked great, but my tip is to use a small amount of pesto, as it can be tricky to seal.
  • Pizza filling: I haven’t tried this filling yet, but some recipes suggest adding passata (tomato puree) and mozzarella for a pizza-inspired filling.

Recipe tips for making Danubio Salato

  • Don’t overstuff: don’t overstuff the buns or they’ll be too difficult to seal and shape. The filling may also pierce the dough and leak out.
  • Switch up the fillings: you can stuff each bun with a mix of different fillings, which is really fun for parties.
  • Prep in advance: if you want to make this in advance, you can assemble the danubio, then refrigerate it overnight. Let it proof at room temperature for at least 2 hours before baking.

Danubio Salato (Neapolitan Stuffed Brioche Buns)

Use the camera icon to toggle the step by step photos on and off. Danubio Salato is a Neapolitan pull-apart brioche bread stuffed with cheese and ham. Super fun to make and perfect for sharing with a crowd. Course Appetizer Cuisine Italian Prep Time 45 minutes minutes Cook Time 30 minutes minutes proofing time 6 hours hours Total Time 7 hours hours 15 minutes minutes Servings 16 servings Calories 221kcal Author Emily Wyper

Equipment

  • stand mixer with dough hook
  • 10-inch (25cm) cake pan (preferably springform)
  • large mixing bowl
  • Plastic wrap (cling film)

Ingredients

For the brioche dough

  • 500 g strong bread flour (3 ½ cups plus 1 tablespoon)
  • 1 teaspoon (4g) fast-action (instant) yeast (see notes)
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 2 egg yolks at room temperature
  • 250 ml (1 cup plus 1 tablespoon) milk
  • 85 g (6 tablespoons) unsalted butter at room temperature (plus extra for greasing)
  • 1 teaspoon fine sea salt

For the filling

  • 100 g (3.5oz) ham or salami can use a mix
  • 200 g (7oz) cheese (We used a mix of low-moisture mozzarella and smoked scamorza)

Egg wash

  • 1 egg
  • 1 tablespoon whole milk

Instructions

To make the dough

  • Gently warm the milk until it’s lukewarm.
  • Put the flour in the bowl of your stand mixer and add the yeast, sugar and salt (don’t place the yeast directly on top of the salt). Give it a quick mix with a fork.
  • Add the 2 egg yolks and lukewarm milk, then knead using the hook attachment on a medium speed until it forms a rough dough (about 5 minutes).
  • Next, with the mixer on, add the butter a little at a time. Once all the butter is incorporated, knead on a medium speed for 10 minutes. The dough might look quite wet and sticky, but it comes together into a smooth and elastic dough after 10 minutes of kneading.
  • Grease a mixing bowl with butter. Form the dough into a ball and place it in the mixing bowl. Cover with plastic wrap (cling film) and let it proof for 3 hours or until doubled in size.

How to form and stuff the buns

  • Grease your springform cake tin with butter and set aside. Cut the mozzarella, ham and other cheeses you are using into small cubes; set aside.
  • Tip the dough from the bowl onto a clean work surface and cut it in half. Roll each half into logs around 12 inches in length. Cut each log into 8 equal pieces, giving you 16 pieces in total.
  • Working with one piece of dough at a time, flatten it with your hand into a rough circle and fill it with some ham and cheese (don’t overstuff). Bring the edges together and pinch it at the top to seal the filling inside.
  • Turn the dough over, so it’s seam side down on your worktop. Cup your hands around the dough and gently rotate it in a circular motion, keeping the edges tucked under to form a smooth ball, then place it in the cake tin.
  • Repeat this with the rest of the dough until you have 16 filled buns in your cake tin. Cover with plastic wrap (cling film) and leave to proof until doubled in size (around 2-3 hours).

Bake

  • Preheat your oven to 180℃/350℉ (fan or conventional).
  • Whisk the egg and milk together in a bowl, then lightly brush it over the brioche.
  • Bake in the oven for 30 minutes if using a fan oven or 35-40 minutes if using a conventional oven, until golden brown on top (see my notes on this). Let it rest for at least 10 minutes before serving.

Notes

  • Oven temperature: I found that baking the Danubio at 200C/400F in a static/conventional oven caused the Danubio to brown too quickly. Keep the temperature at 180C/350F if using a conventional oven, but bake it for 35-40 minutes until golden brown on top. If using a fan oven, bake at 180C/350F for 30 minutes.
  • Cake pan size: a 10-inch cake pan works best to fit all the buns in. I have tested this recipe with a 9-inch cake pan, and it works well, but you will have around 3 buns leftover. You can proof and bake the extra buns in a separate cake pan/dish.
  • Yeast: I use fast-action yeast that can be added directly into the flour. Check if your yeast needs to be activated first; you can do this in a little milk from the recipe.

Multiple times a year I look around and declare that this is my favorite time of year to cook.  I love it in the winter when citrus season peaks. I love it when fava beans are the brightest green with the silkiest, creamiest texture (before they go too starchy). And I love it when the pluots are sweetest and the heirloom tomatoes most concentrated.  This was the feeling I had earlier in the week when I got home from the farmers’ market with the most inspiring ingredients I’ve had in years. I wrote everything down in a list —lemongrass, banana peppers, fresh cranberry beans, baby romaine, etc.. — and then started daydreaming about what I might make. The first thing was this summer-maxxing lunch salad. I tapped my pantry for mung beans, whisked together a simple vinaigrette, and then assembled everything into a wildly colorful, substantial salad where every bite is a bit different. 

High Salad Lunch Salad: Ingredients

The key here is assembling peak everything. If the tomatoes where you are look a little sad, skip them and bump up one of the other ingredients. Or, swap in another ingredient you love – this salad is very forgiving.

  • Tomatoes: My neighbor Emily tipped me off to just how good the Green Zebra tomatoes were this week, and she was spot on. I love their concentrated flavor and beautiful lime green and yellow striped markings. Keep an eye out for heirloom tomatoes where you are, and go for the ones with the best flavor.
  • Pluots: I love the complexity of a perfect, ripe pluot, and while a lot of people I know wait for peach season, I’m actually team pluot. You can use either here.
  • Crispy Shallots: A lot of people buy these, but I like to make my own. Fry thinly sliced shallots in a good amount of oil until deeply browned, then use a spider to transfer them to a paper towel. It’s one of the assertive elements that also brings crunch and savoriness to this salad.
  • Banana Peppers: Keep an eye out! I love their color and mild bite and spiciness. If you can’t find them and like bell peppers, swap in some yellow or red bell peppers. Quite different, but reasonable!
  • Vinegar: The vinegar here is important and should taste delicious. I’ve been using a red wine vinegar that is agrodolce – a bit sweet, but still plenty acidic (it’s this one). If you don’t have an agrodolce vinegar, consider adding a bit of honey or sugar to your vinaigrette to round it out a bit. Just enough to get it to your liking. 

A few of the other ingredients and flowers that were at the market at the same time. On the ingredient front there were: perfect carrots, yellow beans, fresh cranberry beans, shallots, lemongrass, and some little lettuces hiding in there. Marigolds made an appearance after weeks and weeks of show-stopping dahlias. It’s one of the first things I notice as we round the corner into fall. 

High Summer Lunch Salad: Serving Ideas

I served this salad basically as a meal on its own, with a bit of Zanzibari Sesame Flatbread from The Food of Oman (page 177), published in 2015. Sidenote: this is a wonderful book that Ken at Now Serving turned me on to a few years back. The flatbread has a rich coconut milk dough, and I’ve been making variations of it ever since I brought the book home. It seems like the book might be out of print, but poke around for a used copy – they’re out there. I also added a little plate of sheep’s milk feta drizzled with olive oil, more of a creamy bonus than an essential. 

As far as drinks go, I boosted some sparkling water with lemon, a bit of saffron, and plenty of ice, then called it a day.

Introduction

Can you tell us a bit about yourself? What town and region in Italy are you from?

I’m Milanese, born here in 1970. We opened Bela Tusa in May 2025 on Via Pietro Borsieri, in the Isola neighbourhood, together with Norma Galdamez, who is my business partner and a mixologist who runs the bar.

Isola is the neighbourhood where the Porta Nuova towers have been built. We’re on the ground floor, just a few metres from the development, and we cook food that Milanese families used to make at home.

On the walls of our trattoria are sixty years’ worth of photographs by Virgilio Carnisio documenting working-class Milan. The courtyards of the old tenement buildings, washing hanging out to dry, old-fashioned trattorias, and trades photographed as they were disappearing.

“Si mangia dentro la città che non ce l’ha fatta.”

Note from Emily: I left this last sentence in Italian as the direct translation is “you eat inside the city that didn’t make it”. It means that outside in the Isola neighbourhood, is a new and modern Milan, but inside the trattoria you are eating traditional food and looking back at photos of the old Milan, like stepping back in time.

Regional Cuisine

What do the locals eat in your region? Are there specific dishes your region or town is known for?

The first thing I’d like to say to anyone who arrives here in Milan is that it’s not a city of pasta and tomato sauce. It’s a city of rice and butter. The rice fields start right on the outskirts of the city, and for centuries butter has been the cooking fat here.

That’s where the whole repertoire comes from: yellow risotto with ossobuco; yellow because of saffron. Costoletta (cutlet) with the bone; Mondeghili, which are fried meatballs made with leftover boiled beef. Cassoeula, when the cold weather arrives. And beef cheek, the dish people talk about least but the one closest to our hearts.

It’s worth saying a little more about Mondeghili because the name itself carries the history of the city. Mondeghili comes from the Spanish word ‘albóndiga’, and before that from the Arabic ‘al-bunduqa’ which means small hazelnut. Milan was ruled by Madrid for 170 years, and it was the poorer side of the cuisine that preserved these words and names. A dish invented so that nothing would go to waste, with an Arabic name that arrived here through Spain.

Photo courtesy of Bela Tusa Trattoria

Italy is known for producing beautiful seasonal produce. How does the cuisine of your region change with the seasons?

Milanese cuisine is shaped by the plains and winter, and you can see it.

Autumn and winter are its true seasons. Cassoeula waits for the first cold weather, because the Savoy cabbage needs a frost before it’s good. It’s the dish we look forward to the most.

Spring and summer here lighten things up. Risotto remains but takes on something different. This summer we made risotto with figs, lemon zest and basil. It’s the dish that people wrote to us about the most.

There’s also something that isn’t seasonal but is part of everyday life, and that’s bread. The Milanese michetta is hollow inside, almost all crust, and you have to ask for it before ten o’clock. In the humidity of the Po Valley, a dense crumb becomes rubbery within a few hours, so the city developed a bread designed to be eaten the same day. By evening, it’s no longer the same, which is why it doesn’t appear in any guide.

For someone visiting your region or town for the first time, what dishes or food experiences would you recommend they try?

Three things at the table and one outside it.

At the table: yellow risotto with ossobuco, mondeghili and costoletta. These are the three dishes we take seriously.
This cooking is not disappearing because of taste; it is disappearing because of time. Ossobuco needs three hours, and nobody has three hours. That is why it survives in trattorie and not in houses. The day a trattoria closes, you do not lose a restaurant; you lose the last place where that recipe was still being made all the way through.

Outside: go into a bakery before ten in the morning and ask for a michetta. We get our bread from Tondo, at 12 Via Cola Montano, just a short walk from here. It’s a tiny bakery run by Silvia Cancellieri, closed on Sundays and Mondays, and they describe themselves as a “radical bakery” rather than a bakery in the traditional sense. Organic flours, sourdough starter and the name of the farmer behind every flour.

And one thing that isn’t a dish. Just a few hundred metres from us, at 8 Viale Montegrappa, the Cucine Economiche opened in December 1883. You could eat there for just a few cents, and workers who couldn’t go home at lunchtime would have their lunch there. Anyone who wants to understand where Milanese cuisine comes from needs to know that it was born there, not in restaurants.

Daily Life and Eating Habits

Can you share what you eat on a typical day?

I wake up early. I gave up coffee a few years ago, so in the mornings I just have orange juice. The main meal I have in my day is lunch, and it tends to be substantial. In the evening I tend to eat light because I don’t like to go to bed feeling full.

How often do you eat out?

On days when I’m not working, I eat out. Going out is a treat for me, and I don’t put any limits on myself.

I go out often and like eating alone, especially at lunchtime and sometimes in the evening too. I like to go back to the same places; it’s a bit like being at home: I see people I know, friends, and I feel comfortable. I live alone, and in this respect I’m like the cobbler who has no shoes. In my fridge you’ll find nothing but dark chocolate and fruit, usually apples.

Family and Traditions

What does Pranzo della Domenica (Sunday lunch) look like in your home?
What dishes are typically served and how long does it last?

Sunday lunch is a distant dream for me. When I think about it, I go back to when I was a boy, when there were still lots of us in the family, and my Nonna was around to cook for me.

Since I started working this job, many years ago, Sunday has been a working day for me and a busy one at that.

What dishes or desserts are eaten during Christmas time in your region?

Ravioli in brodo (broth) and capon: these are the classic dishes that can’t be missing

For dessert, we have Panettone; it’s Milanese, and it’s the time of the year when the city exports something of its own instead of importing it. We serve it with ‘la salsa mascherpa’, which is a mascarpone cream known by its Milanese name; mascherpa is what we call mascarpone here.

We’d love to know all about your Christmas Day traditions. What does the meal include, and how is the day celebrated?

I’m Milanese, and for us, the big celebration is lunch on December 25th. In the south of Italy, the big meal is eaten on Christmas Eve. It’s a difference that people coming from outside Italy almost always miss.

That said, every Christmas I can remember has been spent in a trattoria, either here or in places I’ve run in the past. Every now and then I’ve managed to carve out a Christmas for myself with what’s left of my family, and even then we don’t eat at my place; we go to a friend’s trattoria.

A Recipe to Remember

Gianmarco has shared two recipes, both written by chef Luca Di Lorenzo, and this is how he makes them in their trattoria in Milan, Bela Tusa.

A note from Gianmarco on mondeghili: they are a recovery dish. Nothing was bought to make them. Whatever boiled or braised meat was left from the day before went in, which in our kitchen right now means the trimmings from the ossobuchi and the cheeks. If you make them with meat bought for the purpose, you will get a good meatball, but not this one.

MONDEGHILI (Deep Fried Meatballs)

Serves 5 to 6, about 28 meatballs

Ingredients

  • 400 g stale bread, plus cold water to soak it
  • 200 g braised veal and beef, in our case the parts of the ossobuchi and the cheeks that do not go out on plates
  • 400 g lean minced beef
  • 150 g Grana Padano aged 12 months, grated
  • 4 eggs
  • 75 g mortadella, minced
  • 30 g salt
  • 3 g garlic powder
  • gremolada to taste: chopped parsley and grated lemon zest, in a ratio of 10 to 1
  • black pepper, ground, to taste
  • breadcrumbs for coating
  • seed oil for frying

Method

1. Soak the stale bread in cold water in a bowl.

2. Put the braised meat through the mincer while it is still warm, together with the mortadella and the softened bread.

3. Add the minced beef, the eggs, the salt, the pepper, the garlic powder, the gremolada and the grated Grana.

4. Work the mixture hard, until it is even and compact.

5. Shape them like ordinary meatballs, pressing the air out, then flatten them slightly.

6. Coat in breadcrumbs and fry in seed oil at 180 degrees.

Each one weighs 52 to 53 g raw. A portion is five.

Tips

Why each step is done that way, which is the part no recipe online gives you

The meat goes through the mincer warm, not cold, because warm it breaks down properly.

You work it hard because the mixture has to come out compact, or the meatballs fall apart in the pan.

You press the air out because any air left inside makes them crack while they cook.

You flatten the balls because that is how the old recipe wants them, otherwise, you have made a plain meatball. The traditional version is cooked in butter in a pan, and flattening them helps them cook through. We fry them, but the shape stayed. It is the fingerprint of a way of cooking nobody uses any more.

RUSUMADA (Red Wine Zabaglione)

Serves 2

This is the one Milanese people over sixty remember, and almost nobody makes any more. It was what a child was given when they were thin, or after an illness: egg yolk beaten with sugar, let down with red wine. Half medicine, half treat. We add cream, which the old version did not have.

  • 4 egg yolks
  • 38 g sugar
  • 275 ml red wine
  • 75 ml single cream

Beat the yolks with the sugar until pale and thick. Add the cream, then the wine, and keep beating until it is smooth. Serve cold, in a glass.

Tips

Since the yolks are not cooked: use pasteurised eggs, or the freshest you can find.

We make it in one batch of about six litres, and the proportions are what carry over: red wine 59%, yolk 16%, cream 16%, sugar 8%.

Is HYROX more about running than strength?

(Image: Velites)

Dr Stephen Patterson, Professor in Applied Exercise Physiology and Performance, recently published a highly informative post on HYROX on LinkedIn. In the post, Patterson outlines the key findings from recent research examining performance in HYROX.

The research analysed HYROX performance across seven competitive seasons and nearly 40,000 individual PRO and ELITE race results. The findings show that performance has improved considerably as HYROX has developed. Among the top 100 performers, race times improved by approximately 13 minutes in the men’s field and 17 minutes in the women’s field from Season 1 to Season 7. Interestingly, much of this improvement came from faster running, with running times improving by approximately eight minutes in men and 10 minutes in women.

However, Patterson also highlights that poor performance in the strength-based stations can disproportionately increase overall finishing time, particularly among slower athletes. Therefore, while running ability appears to be a major driver of HYROX performance, athletes still need sufficient strength to avoid losing significant time during the loaded stations. The challenge appears to be finding the right balance: developing enough strength to perform these stations efficiently, without adding unnecessary muscle mass that could potentially compromise running performance.

AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve analysis

(Image: AiKYNETIX)

With the US Open currently ongoing, the timing of our next bulletin seems very apt. AiKYNETIX, which has featured in SFS Weekly in the past (see here), has made a breakthrough in the tennis world with its new tennis serve analysis.

The tennis serve is a highly complex movement that requires the coordinated transfer of energy through the entire kinetic chain, from the lower body through the pelvis, trunk and shoulder before eventually reaching the racket. Traditionally, gaining a detailed understanding of this sequence has often required sophisticated biomechanics equipment and expertise.

However, AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve feature breaks the serve down into different biomechanical phases based on established frameworks. Rather than analysing the serve as one complete movement, the system can examine what is happening at different stages of the serve. This phase-based approach may help coaches better understand how an athlete transfers movement through the kinetic chain. For example, the analysis can examine the transition from lower-body contribution into pelvic and trunk movement, before progressing through the shoulder and upper limb and eventually into racket action and ball contact.

Another interesting development is the ability to generate detailed analysis reports directly from the platform. This could help bridge the gap between collecting biomechanical information and actually using it within the coaching environment.

So, if you are working in the tennis industry, then it is definitely worth checking out AiKYNETIX’s new tennis serve feature. Its real potential may lie in making detailed biomechanical analysis more accessible and practical within everyday coaching environments. And impressively, this can all be done by simply uploading a smartphone video of a tennis serve!

Does Neutonic enhance productivity?

(Image: Chloe Hamard)

Neutonic, the productivity drink created by influencers Chris Williamson and James Smith, has experienced a remarkable rise in popularity and is quickly becoming a staple on shelves in shops and markets worldwide. But does it truly enhance productivity?

Shortly after its launch, nutritionist and performance coach Adam McDonald shared a well-rounded video review of the drink. Although this video dates back over two years, it remains a hidden gem, offering an expert perspective that many would benefit from revisiting.

In his review, McDonald points out a key advantage of Neutonic: the ingredients list clearly states the exact doses of each component, unlike many products that hide this information in proprietary blends. This transparency allows consumers to understand what they’re consuming. He further dives into each ingredient and the science supporting them. For example, Cognizin may improve memory and attention, but the evidence is limited, particularly among healthy adults aged 20 to 40, the drink’s target market. Rhodiola Rosea shows promising evidence for reducing mental fatigue and boosting cognitive performance under stress. The combination of caffeine and L-Theanine enhances alertness and reaction times while mitigating the jitteriness and anxiety often associated with caffeine alone. Although Panax Ginseng might improve reaction time, the evidence for this is highly inconsistent. Lastly, B Vitamins play a vital role in brain function, but they are unlikely to provide a direct productivity boost unless someone is deficient.

While McDonald acknowledges that Neutonic has some scientific backing for its ingredient selection and is better formulated than many energy drinks, he emphasises that prioritising sleep, improving time management, and managing distractions will likely yield far greater benefits for productivity than consuming Neutonic. Considering that Neutonic comes with a relatively high price tag compared to other energy drinks, and any boosts in focus and attention are likely to be subtle, it may be wiser to explore other strategies for enhancing productivity rather than relying on a can of Neutonic.
