A Salmonella outbreak in the United Kingdom linked to eggs from Ukraine has sickened almost 80 people across two years.

The strain is associated with 40 illnesses in England in 2026 and 36 in 2025. 

As part of a large Salmonella Enteritidis outbreak with 700 patients connected to imported eggs, microbiological testing of nearly 3,000 eggs was undertaken by the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA). This sampling detected Salmonella Enteritidis in a batch of eggs from Ukraine and a separate outbreak.

Affected eggs came from Yasensvit and are stamped with the code 3UA1012066. The Food Standards Agency (FSA) and Food Standards Scotland (FSS) asked businesses to stop using them.

Yasensvit, part of the Ovostar Union Group, is one of the largest producers of eggs and egg products in Europe and operates in international markets. The company previously urged people to avoid drawing what it called “premature conclusions” until all the facts were established.

Detection of Salmonella
In October, Salmonella Enteritidis was detected in three samples of six pooled eggs each from the Ukrainian producer. Nine isolates were sent to the reference laboratory and it was confirmed via whole genome sequencing (WGS) that they were the same strain of Salmonella Enteritidis and associated with human infections.

The date of sampling was Sept. 23, 2026. Eggs were reportedly laid and packaged on Aug. 23, with an expiry date of Sept. 19. This means samples were taken four days after the best before date.

There are 78 confirmed cases with sample dates between Dec. 17, 2024, and Sept. 26, 2026. Wales has two cases while the rest live in England. In total, 32 are from London and 24 are from the East of England. Cases range from younger than 1 to 99 years old with a median age of 30. Across all age groups, 39 are female.

There was one outbreak in June 2026 with six confirmed patients linked to a restaurant in Yorkshire and Humber where non-UK eggs were used for certain dishes.

The State Service of Ukraine on Food Safety and Consumer Protection said it would conduct an inspection at the company in Kyiv including taking product samples for lab testing to verify information received from UK authorities.

Cyclospora update
UKHSA, Public Health Scotland and Public Health Wales are also investigating travel-related Cyclospora infections.

Reported cases decreased in August and September, after a peak in June and July 2026. There were 49 illnesses in June, 47 in July, 10 in August, and seven in September. 

Between April and September, 135 cases of cyclosporiasis were reported with 81 from England, 43 from Scotland and 11 from Wales. The median age of patients was 47, and more than 60 percent were female. It was previously reported that between April and mid-July, 67 people had fallen sick.

Of the 95 cases with travel information available, 88 had been to Mexico, with Riviera Maya remaining the primary region. Patients stayed in different hotels and consumed a variety of food and drinks as part of all-inclusive holiday packages.

There were 87 cyclosporiasis cases in England in 2025 and 124 in 2024. Cyclospora cayetanensis is a parasite that does not naturally occur in the UK. Travelers to Mexico and other areas where the infection is more common can reduce their risk by following good food and water hygiene measures, including drinking bottled water and eating thoroughly cooked food.

Plants are incredibly diverse, and so are botanists! In its mission to spread fascinating stories about the plant world, Botany One also introduces you to the scientists behind these great stories.

Today, we have Dr Sean Hoban. Hoban thinks of himself as a conservation biologist first: he wants to understand how plants and their populations work, but what he ultimately seeks is to see that knowledge used in the real world. His team integrates ideas and tools from botany, evolution, and ecology to help conserve plants both in situ and ex situ.

Hoban is a Senior Scientist and Tree Conservation Biologist at The Morton Arboretum near Chicago. The Arboretum has been researching and conserving trees for more than a hundred years, while also working with communities and educators to foster a love of trees and to share knowledge about tending to them, so it’s a good home for me. His research includes understanding small and declining populations, identifying threats to genetic diversity, prioritising plant species for conservation, and figuring out how well seed banks and botanic gardens conserve diversity. Hoban is also interested in seed dispersal, biogeography, and how forests respond to increasing drought and heat.

One of the most central areas of Hoban's work focuses on translating conservation science into practical tools, including conservation gap analyses for botanic gardens and indicators for monitoring genetic diversity at national and global scales, such as under the Convention on Biological Diversity. You can follow his work at his lab website, LinkedIn and Bluesky.

What made you become interested in plants?

I think it started with two things happening when I was young. One was becoming aware of the deforestation crisis. Amazon deforestation was frequently in the news when I was growing up, and I remember my shock at the scale of what was being lost. It was probably my first realisation that something as enormous and vital for life as a forest could be threatened by humans.

At the same time, I did a lot of hiking and camping in my teenage years. I fell in love with being under a tree canopy and started paying attention to the trees themselves, not just forests as scenery, but individual species and how different they could be. I became interested in looking closely at trees and thinking about how they survive: how they reproduce, tolerate stressful conditions, and persist for such long periods.

Those two interests, fascination with forests and concern about their loss, came together in my interest in plant conservation biology.

Hoban with a Quercus welshii individual in deep sand in Utah in 2017. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What motivated you to pursue your current area of research?

I’ve always been drawn toward applied science. I love understanding how the natural world works, but I also want science to be in the service of society. I want to help us become better caretakers of the world around us. That motivation has shaped the questions I choose and the scale at which I work.

It is also why a significant part of my research has moved toward conservation policy and developing tools that other people can actually use. For example, I’ve worked on conservation gap analyses that help identify which species and populations are not yet sufficiently protected by botanical gardens. I also helped create, test, improve, and train people on how to work with indicators of genetic diversity that countries can use to monitor and then take action to restore nature, under national and international commitments.

I love being a part of the process of moving from scientific knowledge to conservation action. And I love bringing more academic scientists out into the policy and management space. We can learn an enormous amount about genetic diversity, ecology, and threats to species, but ultimately we need to turn that knowledge into decisions: What should we conserve? How? And how can we tell whether our efforts are working?

Hoban in his role as a global conservation policy expert, attending the Convention on Biodiversity meeting in Cali, Colombia, in 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What is your favourite part of your work related to plants?

I love seeds. I am fascinated by seeing a plant produce seeds, watching how those seeds disperse, and then seeing seedlings establish and grow. A seed is such a small piece of life, but inside it is the species’ genetic inheritance and the possibility of a whole new plant.

Seeds also connect many parts of my research. Dispersal determines how populations are connected across landscapes, and across time, from years to millennia. Seed collection is one of our important tools for ex situ conservation and something I’ve been privileged to take part in, actually helping add to the living collections at The Morton Arboretum. And regeneration in wild populations, whether seedlings can establish and survive, is fundamental to whether a population or forest will persist into the future.

There is also something wonderful about seeing and holding (and planting) seeds. A lot of my work involves genetic data, computer models, large datasets, and conservation policy, which can become abstract. Finding seeds, or seeing seedlings emerge, brings everything back to the plant itself. It is a reminder of what the analyses and conservation plans are intended to protect.

Sean holding two seeds of the threatened butternut tree in 2024, a finding that sparked subsequent intensive research into the ecology of seed production and regeneration of this tree. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Are any specific plants or species that have intrigued or inspired your research? If so, what are they and why?

I’ve worked intensively with the butternut tree (Juglans cinerea) for 20 years, and it is a tree that continues to inspire me. Butternut is a North American tree with considerable ecological and historical importance, but its populations have declined severely because of an introduced fungal disease, butternut canker.

What fascinates me is that butternut is a survivor. The disease and decline situation is serious, but unlike the American chestnut, butternut has not been devastated to quite the same degree. Mature trees still survive, reproduce, and, under the right conditions, can thrive. That means there is real potential for conservation and restoration.

A lot of our team’s and our collaborators’ work now asks what those " right conditions " are. How much light does butternut need? What forest management practices help it regenerate? What soils and habitats are best? And how much of survival and disease response is related to genetics?

I like working on a species where there are difficult conservation problems but also genuine reasons for optimism. With enough knowledge and effort, we have an opportunity to help this tree persist and recover. If you are reading this and interested in participating, you can be part of it!

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois. Photo by Sean Hoban.

Could you share an experience or anecdote from your work that has marked your career and reaffirmed your fascination with plants?

One of the things I’ve enjoyed most about working on butternut is not a single field experience, but the community of people around the tree. I’ve had the opportunity to work with an extraordinary variety of people: researchers, students, retired foresters, land managers, naturalists, and people who love the land for agriculture or for bird watching, as well as Martin Kratt, one of the Kratt Brothers, who has become involved in butternut conservation.

Two colleagues of mine now lead a monthly conference call that brings together about 30 butternut researchers and enthusiasts across the continent. Some people have spent decades working with this species and bring an incredible depth of knowledge; others are students or people encountering these questions for the first time. It is a very supportive and affirming group, united by wanting to understand and conserve this tree.

That experience has reminded me that conservation is collaborative. No single researcher or institution has all the knowledge needed to save a species. Plants can bring together people with very different backgrounds and expertise, sometimes over generations. The community and our shared sense that we might help butternut recover is continually rewarding and reaffirming.

Hoban assessing the health of a Juglans cinerea seedling in Illinois, 2025. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What advice would you give young scientists considering a career in plant biology?

Be interdisciplinary. Some of the most interesting and important questions in plant biology are between traditional fields, and many conservation problems cannot be solved from a single perspective.

My own work has taken me through genetics, ecology, botany, computer modeling, biogeography, fieldwork, seed conservation, and eventually policy. I didn’t begin my career knowing that I would work across all of those areas. Often I learned something because it was the tool I needed to answer the next question.

I would encourage young scientists to develop strong expertise in something, but not let that expertise be a boundary. Learn quantitative and computational skills, but also learn natural history. Spend time in the field. Talk to people in other disciplines. Learn how conservation practitioners and policymakers think about problems by talking to them in person. And learn to communicate your science to people who don’t share your speciality.

Be widely curious. Being willing to cross disciplinary boundaries can lead you to questions and new areas of work that you would never have anticipated.

Hoban with Quercus havardii in Texas. This species is adapted to sand dunes and typically grows to about half a meter tall. Photo by Sean Hoban.

What do people usually get wrong about plants?

I think people often underestimate how much diversity exists within a plant species. We tend to think of a species as a single thing: a white oak is a white oak, and conserving the species means making sure enough individuals survive. But individuals and populations can be genetically very different from one another. A population at the dry edge of a species’ range may contain different adaptations from one growing in a cooler or wetter environment. Losing populations can therefore mean losing unique genetic diversity even when the species itself has not gone extinct.

I experienced this recently when seeing beech trees from across Europe in a common garden. Their leaf colour, growth form, and response to drought varied dramatically by population! That matters so much in a rapidly changing world. Which is why I want to share my astonishment at discovering that Aviv Regev was right – not only is Alzheimer’s closely associated with our sense of smell, but there is a growing body of research suggesting that our noses might have something to do with why the disease develops in the first place.

The first bit you might already know: people with Alzheimer’s tend to lose their sense of smell, sometimes years before any symptoms of memory loss start to show up. But it turns out that our ability to smell is a surprisingly good indicator of our overall health (looking at you, covid-19). It’s not that losing your sense of smell itself will kill you. More that the olfactory nerves are deeply intertwined with some of the most important memory structures in the brain, so when they start to deteriorate, it can be an early warning that something much more serious is going on.

And this connection seems to work in both directions. One intriguing study (admittedly with quite a small sample size) put a diffuser with essential oils by older people’s beds, thus exposing them to all kinds of wonderful scents while they slept. After six months, the people who had the olfactory enrichment performed better on memory tests than those in a control group.

All very interesting. But not what Regev had been suggesting. Her words seemed to imply that noses are actually responsible for the trouble in the first place.

It turns out that the reason scientists are even entertaining this possibility is because of how fiercely our brains are protected from the outside world. Our skulls are like an actual fortress: even our blood isn’t allowed in without passing a particularly fussy bouncer called the blood-brain barrier.

And that means that, despite all the elaborate defences protecting your brain, there is a potential route in, sitting right in the middle of your face.

So, here’s the idea. Maybe the beta-amyloid plaques, the sticky little protein fragments that appear to play such a key role in the cascade of Alzheimer’s in the brain, are what’s left over after the brain has fought an infection. And maybe the nose is how the infection arrives.

The remarkable thing, though, was that within 72 hours, the bacteria had travelled up the nerves in the nasal cavity and got through to the brain. But there is some other, rather unsettling evidence that infections aren’t the only unwelcome visitors that manage to get in that way.

A group in Mexico studied stray dogs that spent their lives breathing in polluted air. You don’t see the same thing in dogs that lived in cleaner, more rural environments.

Now these studies have been extended to look at the brains of very small children and young adults who died from gun violence or car accidents – and they find something similar. Tiny, metal–rich particles, thought to originate from exhausts and brake dust, turned up in their brain tissue, alongside inflammation and accumulations of beta-amyloid. The very sorts of changes associated with Alzheimer’s, appearing in brains that were sometimes only a few years old. Put together, it implies that the disease might have an environmental cause, one that begins much much earlier than we thought.

If tagatose acts as a prebiotic, does it produce the same digestive effects associated with other prebiotic ingredients? The answer is yes, but not exactly. And understanding why helps formulators create products consumers love instead of products consumers remember for the wrong reasons.

What Makes Something a Prebiotic?

The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) defines a prebiotic as: A substrate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms, conferring a health benefit. That’s an elegant scientific definition. Translated into plain English, a prebiotic is food for your beneficial gut bacteria. Those bacteria ferment the ingredient and produce compounds known as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) including:

  • Butyrate
  • Acetate
  • Propionate

These metabolites help support:

  • Colon health
  • Gut barrier integrity (mucosa)
  • Immune modulation
  • Mineral absorption
  • Healthy glucose metabolism
  • Satiety signaling

The take home word here is fermentation. And fermentation creates gases.

Why Prebiotics Can Cause Digestive Symptoms

Many formulators have worked with ingredients like:

  • Inulin
  • Chicory Root Fiber
  • Fructooligosaccharides
  • Galactooligosaccharides
  • Gum Acacia
  • Resistant starch
  • Partially Hydrolyzed guar gum

They’re fantastic ingredients. They’re also famous for producing customer reviews that include words like: “I felt bloated.” “My stomach sounded like a drum solo.” “Great product…don’t eat it before a meeting.” That’s because most traditional prebiotics arrive almost completely intact into the large intestine. Gut microbes suddenly receive a massive buffet. The bacteria celebrate. Your customers sometimes do not. Rapid fermentation creates:

  • Hydrogen
  • Carbon dioxide
  • Methane (in some individuals)

Along with:

  • Bloating
  • Gas kinetics
  • Abdominal discomfort
  • Increased bowel movements
  • Disaster pants

These are not safety concerns. They’re simply normal consequences of fermentation.

Tagatose Is Different

Tagatose doesn’t behave exactly like a dietary fiber. Instead, it follows a more interesting path. A significant portion of ingested tagatose is absorbed in the small intestine. The remainder travels into the colon where beneficial bacteria ferment it. That means the microbial benefits remain. But the fermentation load is generally smaller than with many traditional fibers. Think of it as feeding your microbiome, without throwing it an all-you-can-eat buffet.

What the Research Shows

Multiple human studies have demonstrated that tagatose can:

  • Increase beneficial Bifidobacteria
  • Increase Lactobacillus populations
  • Produce beneficial short-chain fatty acids
  • Support a healthier gut microbiome

At the same time, gastrointestinal tolerance has generally been shown to be favorable at typical food-use levels. Like every fermentable carbohydrate, tolerance is highly individual.

Dose Matters

Here’s where formulation becomes important. Small amounts incorporated throughout the day are generally well tolerated by most consumers. Very large single doses, >100 grams, are another story. As intake increases, more tagatose escapes digestion and becomes available for microbial fermentation.

That can increase:

  • Gas
  • Bloating
  • Loose stools
  • Gastrointestinal discomfort

This isn’t unique to tagatose. It’s true of virtually every prebiotic ingredient. Even apples can do this. It’s simply the biology of fermentation.

Think of It Like Exercise

Nobody walks into a gym after years on the couch and deadlifts 500 pounds. Your gut microbiome isn’t much different. When consumers gradually increase prebiotic intake, the microbial ecosystem often adapts. Tolerance frequently improves over time. This is one reason many nutrition professionals recommend introducing prebiotics gradually.

Soluble Fibers Under Fire – The Science of Acid Stability, Thermal Processing, Molecular Chain Length, and Functional Performance in Food and Beverage Formulation

📅 Oct 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

by Thom King – Founder, Chief Innovation Officer, Food Scientist, Icon Foods

Soluble dietary fibers have become some of the most versatile ingredients in modern food and beverage formulation. They provide much more than a means of increasing dietary fiber content. Depending on their molecular architecture, soluble fibers can contribute bulk, viscosity, mouthfeel, water binding, solids management, sugar replacement, emulsion stabilization, and, in certain cases, prebiotic functionality.

However, there is a fundamental problem with the way soluble fibers are sometimes selected and used: solubility is frequently mistaken for stability.

A fiber that dissolves beautifully in water at room temperature may behave very differently after exposure to pH 3.0, high-temperature short-time (HTST) processing, ultra-high-temperature (UHT) treatment, retorting, or six months of storage in an acidic beverage.

The molecular structure of the fiber, its degree of polymerization (DP), glycosidic linkages, branching, molecular-weight distribution, and susceptibility to hydrolysis all influence its performance.

Understanding these relationships is essential for formulators developing reduced-sugar beverages, protein drinks, nutritional supplements, dairy alternatives, confectionery, baked goods, sauces, and functional foods.

This paper examines the chemistry of soluble fiber stability, compares major commercial fiber categories, explores the relationship between chain length and functionality, and provides practical formulation guidance, starting usage levels, processing considerations, and analytical recommendations.

The central argument is straightforward, a soluble fiber should be selected for the conditions it must survive, not simply for the properties it possesses when it enters the mixing tank.

  1. Introduction: The Fiber That Went into the Tank May Not Be the Fiber That Comes Out

There is something wonderfully deceptive about soluble fiber. At first glance, many commercial soluble fibers look remarkably similar. They arrive as white or slightly off-white powders, dissolve in water, and contribute varying degrees of body, sweetness, or viscosity. A specification sheet might describe them as soluble, low viscosity, heat stable, clean tasting, or suitable for beverage applications.

Unfortunately, none of those descriptions tells us everything we need to know about how that fiber will behave in an actual food matrix. Consider a reduced-sugar fruit beverage formulated at pH 3.2. The product contains 5% added soluble fiber and undergoes thermal processing before being packaged for ambient distribution. On paper, the formulation appears straightforward.

But what happens when the fiber is exposed to heat in an acidic environment? Will the polymer chains remain intact? Will glycosidic bonds hydrolyze? Will the molecular-weight distribution shift? Could additional mono- and disaccharides form? Will the finished beverage retain its intended viscosity, sweetness profile, and declared dietary fiber content throughout shelf life?

Some soluble fibers are relatively resistant to these conditions. Others, particularly certain fructan-based fibers, are more susceptible to acid-catalyzed hydrolysis.

Research on inulin and fructooligosaccharides demonstrates that degradation depends on the combination of pH, temperature, heating time, molecular chain length, and food matrix. These variables cannot be evaluated independently. 

Thermal stability does not necessarily mean acid stability. And acid stability at room temperature does not guarantee acid stability during thermal processing. For food and beverage formulators, that distinction can mean the difference between a successful commercial launch and a shelf-life study that turns into a forensic investigation.

  • What Is a Soluble Fiber, Really?

Soluble fibers are a diverse group of carbohydrate-based materials capable of dissolving or dispersing in water, depending on their chemistry and physical structure. They are not one interchangeable ingredient category. Commercially important examples include:

  • Resistant dextrin and resistant maltodextrin
  • Inulin and fructooligosaccharides (FOS)
  • Galactooligosaccharides (GOS)
  • Polydextrose
  • Partially hydrolyzed guar gum (PHGG)
  • Acacia fiber, also known as gum Arabic
  • Pectin
  • Beta-glucans
  • Certain xylooligosaccharides and arabinoxylan-derived fibers

Each material has a different molecular architecture, and those differences influence its processing behavior. The term soluble fiber describes a physical characteristic, not a universal chemical structure or guaranteed physiological effect.

Furthermore, soluble fiber is not automatically synonymous with FDA-recognized dietary fiber. Under the US regulatory definition, isolated or synthetic nondigestible carbohydrates generally require recognition of a beneficial physiological effect before they can be declared as dietary fiber.

The FDA recognizes certain fibers directly under its regulation and permits additional specified fibers to be counted through its stated enforcement-discretion policy. These distinctions are especially important when using novel or proprietary soluble fiber ingredients. 

The four molecular characteristics that matter most

The functional behavior of a soluble fiber depends heavily on four interrelated structural properties.

Degree of polymerization (DP) describes the number of monosaccharide units incorporated into a carbohydrate chain.

Molecular weight describes the mass of the molecule. Although molecular weight generally increases with DP within a given polymer family, different monosaccharides, substituents, and branching structures complicate comparisons between fiber types.

Glycosidic linkage chemistry determines how individual sugar units are connected and influences their susceptibility to chemical or enzymatic hydrolysis.

Branching and molecular conformation influence solubility, hydration, viscosity, accessibility of glycosidic bonds, and interaction with other ingredients.

The mistake is assuming that DP alone determines everything. It does not. A short-chain fructooligosaccharide and a short-chain resistant dextrin may have similar molecular weights while displaying very different responses to acidic processing.

Their chemical linkages are different, and those linkages are where much of the action takes place.

  • Degree of Polymerization: Why Chain Length Matters

Degree of polymerization is one of the most useful concepts in soluble fiber formulation. In simple terms, DP describes how many individual sugar units are linked together to form an oligosaccharide or polymer.

Conceptual illustration of linear carbohydrate chains. Actual commercial fibers contain distributions of molecular sizes and may be branched rather than linear. A DP of 3 represents three linked monosaccharide units. A DP of 20 represents twenty. Commercial soluble fibers rarely consist of molecules with one precise chain length. Most are distributions of molecules with different degrees of polymerization. This distribution is often more important than the average DP printed on a technical data sheet.

3.1 Short-chain fibers

Short-chain soluble carbohydrates, including certain FOS, GOS, and other oligosaccharides, generally have relatively low molecular weights. Within a comparable chemical family, shorter chains often offer higher solubility, lower viscosity, and less tendency to form polymer networks than their longer-chain counterparts.

These characteristics can be particularly attractive in clear beverages, concentrated syrups, and products requiring substantial fiber fortification without excessive thickness. However, shorter chain length does not universally mean greater resistance to hydrolysis. In research involving short-chain FOS at pH 3.5, pentasaccharides were more resistant to thermal treatment than trisaccharides. The surrounding food matrix also significantly influenced stability. 

3.2 Intermediate-chain fibers

Intermediate-chain fibers often provide a useful compromise between solubility, processing characteristics, and textural functionality. Depending on their chemistry, these materials may provide greater solids contribution or mouthfeel than very short oligosaccharides while maintaining manageable viscosity. This can be advantageous in reduced-sugar beverages, nutrition bars, dairy alternatives, and certain confectionery applications. The exact performance must be evaluated by fiber family rather than by DP alone.

3.3 Long-chain fibers

Longer carbohydrate chains can exhibit substantially different physical behavior. Within a given polymer family, increasing molecular weight often increases the potential for chain entanglement, hydration-related viscosity, and structural contribution.

Long-chain inulin, for example, can contribute creamy texture and fat-mimetic properties under appropriate processing conditions. However, increasing chain length can also reduce cold-water solubility, increase hydration time, or encourage aggregation.

Research examining inulin-type fructans identifies DP as a major determinant of solubility, with longer-chain fractions generally less soluble than oligofructose and short-chain FOS. 

3.4 Why molecular-weight distribution matters

Imagine two soluble fibers with the same average molecular weight. One contains a relatively narrow distribution of molecules. The other contains a mixture of very short chains and substantially longer polymers.

Despite similar average values, these materials may behave differently during hydration, thermal processing, storage, and fermentation. The longer fraction may contribute disproportionately to viscosity, while the shorter fraction may influence osmolality, sweetness, and fermentation behavior.

Consequently, an average DP or molecular-weight value is insufficient to predict all functional characteristics. Formulation principle: Ask suppliers for molecular-weight distribution data whenever chain length materially affects the product’s performance. A single average is useful, but it is not the whole story.

  • Acid Hydrolysis: The Chemistry Behind Fiber Degradation

Hydrolysis is the cleavage of chemical bonds through reaction with water. In carbohydrate polymers, hydrolysis can break glycosidic linkages, converting longer molecules into shorter oligosaccharides and, potentially, monosaccharides. Under acidic conditions, certain glycosidic bonds become more susceptible to cleavage. Hydrogen ions facilitate bond cleavage, while increasing temperature generally accelerates the reaction. With additional hydrolysis, some fragments may ultimately be converted into individual sugars. However, not all carbohydrate linkages respond identically to acidity.

4.1 The importance of linkage chemistry

Inulin and many FOS preparations contain β-(2→1) fructosyl linkages. These bonds are susceptible to acid-catalyzed hydrolysis. Consequently, inulin-type fructans can undergo molecular degradation when exposed to sufficiently acidic environments, particularly at elevated temperatures.

In a study by Glibowski and Bukowska, inulin degradation increased with temperature and heating time at pH 4 or below. At pH 5 and above, the tested inulin remained chemically stable under the reported experimental conditions.  That does not establish pH 5 as a universal safety boundary for every commercial inulin ingredient or every processing schedule. It does establish that acidity is a critical variable.

4.2 Hydrolysis is not always complete destruction

An important distinction is that hydrolysis does not necessarily convert all dietary fiber into simple sugars. Initial cleavage may reduce a long-chain polymer to shorter fragments that remain nondigestible and potentially retain dietary fiber functionality. Further hydrolysis can generate digestible sugars.

The extent of this conversion depends on the original polymer structure, reaction conditions, and degree of degradation. For example, the breakdown of inulin can produce shorter fructans before ultimately releasing fructose and other sugars.

This means that a decrease in average molecular weight is not automatically equivalent to an equal decrease in dietary fiber content. The molecular profile must be measured.

4.3 What about the finished product’s sugar content?

This is particularly important in reduced-sugar and no-added-sugar formulations. If an acid-sensitive fiber generates measurable free sugars during processing or storage, the finished product may have a different sugar composition than the original formulation.

That does not automatically determine whether a product violates a particular labeling claim, but it can affect analytical values and regulatory assessments. The correct approach is to evaluate the finished product after processing and throughout its intended shelf life. A calculated formulation is not a substitute for finished-product analytical verification.

  • Temperature, Time, and pH: The Three-Way Interaction

In commercial processing, temperature is rarely the only variable responsible for degradation. The real issue is cumulative exposure. A fiber may survive a short pasteurization step yet experience measurable degradation during extended hot holding or prolonged ambient storage. Likewise, a fiber that remains stable at pH 4.5 may behave differently at pH 2.8.

5.1 Temperature accelerates chemical reactions

For many chemical degradation reactions, increasing temperature increases the reaction rate. This relationship can often be described using the Arrhenius equation:

The important implication is that degradation rates can change substantially with temperature. However, it would be incorrect to assign one universal temperature threshold to all soluble fibers. An inulin preparation and a resistant dextrin preparation can have entirely different kinetic behavior.

5.2 pH changes the chemical environment

Because pH is logarithmic, a decrease of one pH unit represents a tenfold increase in hydrogen-ion activity, approximately, under comparable conditions. That does not mean hydrolysis necessarily becomes ten times faster.

The reaction rate also depends on linkage chemistry, temperature, matrix composition, and the mechanism governing the reaction. Nevertheless, relatively small changes in pH can substantially alter the degradation risk of acid-sensitive carbohydrates.

This principle is consistent with research modeling FOS degradation during continuous thermal processing, where high retention was predicted under particular short-time conditions.  The appropriate conclusion is not that short-time processing makes every fiber safe. It is that the entire time-temperature history matters more than the maximum temperature alone.

5.4 The process does not end when the heater switches off

A commercial product may spend time in a balance tank, heat exchanger, holding tube, filler, cooling tunnel, and finished-goods warehouse. Each stage contributes to the chemical history of the fiber. In addition, acid-catalyzed hydrolysis may continue during storage, although generally at a much slower rate at lower temperatures.

For acid-sensitive fibers, accelerated shelf-life testing can reveal degradation that is not apparent immediately after processing. This is especially important for shelf-stable beverages with intended shelf lives of nine to eighteen months.

6.1 Resistant dextrin and resistant maltodextrin

Resistant dextrins are generally produced through controlled modification and processing of starch-derived carbohydrates. Their nondigestibility results from molecular structures and linkages that resist normal small-intestinal digestion.

For formulators, the attraction is straightforward: certain resistant dextrin preparations can deliver substantial dietary fiber with relatively low viscosity, limited sweetness, and good solubility.

This makes them attractive in ready-to-drink beverages, powdered drink mixes, reduced-sugar syrups, and nutritional products. However, resistant dextrin is not one standardized molecular structure. The source starch, manufacturing process, purification, residual sugars, degree of branching, and molecular-weight distribution can vary. Two products described as resistant dextrin may not behave identically.

When formulating acidic beverages, I would evaluate resistant dextrin as an early candidate, but I would not assume that its resistance to human digestive enzymes automatically establishes resistance to every industrial acid-and-heat process.

6.2 Soluble corn fiber and soluble tapioca fiber

These ingredients deserve particular attention because the terms are sometimes treated as though they identify a precise chemical composition.

A commercial soluble corn or tapioca fiber may contain a mixture of nondigestible oligosaccharides, higher-molecular-weight carbohydrates, residual digestible carbohydrates, and other constituents. Depending on processing, the ingredient may have a broad DP distribution.

Some grades are engineered primarily for low viscosity and beverage use. Others are designed to replace sugar solids in bars, confectionery, or baked goods. The first questions I would ask are: What is the fiber percentage on an as-is basis? What analytical method was used? What is the residual sugar profile? What is the DP distribution? How does the ingredient perform at the finished product’s pH after thermal processing?

A supplier’s description of soluble tapioca fiber tells me the starting material. It does not tell me enough about the final carbohydrate architecture. For reduced-sugar products, this distinction is particularly important because different commercial grades can contribute different amounts of digestible carbohydrate.

6.3 Inulin: outstanding functionality, but watch the acid

Inulin is a fructan, typically derived commercially from chicory root and other botanical sources. Its principal structure contains fructose units connected through β-(2→1) linkages. Longer-chain inulin can contribute creamy texture, fat-mimetic properties, and structural functionality. Shorter-chain preparations generally offer better solubility and reduced viscosity.

Unfortunately, inulin’s fructosyl linkages are susceptible to acid-catalyzed hydrolysis. Glibowski and Bukowska demonstrated increasing degradation with time and temperature at acidic pH. Other reviews confirm the importance of DP, pH, and temperature in determining inulin stability. 

For an acidic beverage undergoing thermal processing, I would not select inulin solely because it provides excellent fiber content or clean-label appeal. I would first establish whether sufficient intact fructan remains after processing and storage. In neutral-pH dairy systems, certain nutritional products, and applications where inulin’s textural contribution is especially valuable, it can be an excellent ingredient. The point is not to avoid inulin. The point is to use it where its chemistry makes sense.

6.4 Fructooligosaccharides: chain length makes a measurable difference

FOS typically consists of relatively short fructan chains. These ingredients can contribute prebiotic functionality and some sweetness, depending on the preparation. However, their stability under acidic thermal conditions requires careful evaluation.

In a study by Vega and Zúñiga-Hansen, short-chain FOS were examined at pH 3.5 in citrate buffer, orange juice, and tomato juice. The researchers found that pentasaccharides were more stable than trisaccharides under the tested conditions. They also found that the surrounding matrix influenced degradation.

Their simulations predicted greater than 95% FOS retention under certain continuous thermal-processing conditions.  It demonstrates that FOS should not simply be labeled heat unstable without considering the actual processing schedule. A short exposure can behave very differently from an extended hold. However, at high temperatures and sufficiently acidic pH, substantial hydrolysis remains possible. Research on FOS hydrolysis at temperatures from 80°C to 120°C also demonstrated that degradation accelerated under acidic conditions.  For commercial formulation, the best approach is to quantify individual FOS fractions before and after processing.

6.5 Galactooligosaccharides

GOS consists of galactose-containing oligosaccharides produced through enzymatic conversion, commonly from lactose. Commercial GOS preparations vary in linkage distribution, DP, and residual sugar composition. Their behavior cannot be reliably predicted from DP alone.

GOS can be attractive in functional beverages, dairy products, and nutritional formulations, but  processing stability should be established for the specific commercial preparation. The presence of residual lactose, glucose, and galactose also needs consideration, particularly when calculating sugars and evaluating shelf-life changes.

For acidic, heat-processed products, I recommend measuring the individual carbohydrate fractions rather than relying exclusively on total soluble solids or a single dietary fiber value.

6.6 Polydextrose

Polydextrose is a highly branched glucose-based polymer manufactured under controlled conditions. It has a long history of use in reduced-sugar food applications, particularly where bulk and solids are required without the full sweetness of sucrose. It can contribute body, solids, and textural modification while generally maintaining manageable viscosity. Its branched molecular architecture differentiates it from linear fructans. 

Polydextrose is worth evaluating in thermally processed foods, including confectionery, fillings, and certain beverages. However, its processing stability should still be confirmed under the specific pH, temperature, water activity, and holding conditions involved.

In confectionery, I would also pay attention to hygroscopicity, moisture migration, and glass-transition behavior. A fiber can survive thermal processing chemically and still create problems with stickiness or shelf-life texture.

6.7 Partially hydrolyzed guar gum

PHGG is an especially interesting ingredient because its molecular architecture illustrates the practical value of controlled depolymerization. Native guar gum is a high-molecular-weight galactomannan that can generate substantial viscosity at low concentrations.

Through controlled enzymatic hydrolysis, the average molecular size is reduced. The result can be a soluble fiber with dramatically lower viscosity than native guar.

A published review reports that a 5% PHGG solution exhibited viscosity around 10 mPa·s, compared with approximately 2,000–3,000 mPa·s for a 1% native guar solution. The review also describes good tolerance of acidic and thermal conditions for the preparation studied. 

Reducing chain length can preserve useful nutritional characteristics while dramatically changing processing behavior. For formulators developing fiber-enriched beverages, PHGG can be particularly attractive where high fiber delivery and relatively low viscosity are required.

However, the degree of hydrolysis matters. More recently, research involving PHGG preparations of different molecular weights demonstrated substantial differences in rheological and emulsion-stabilizing behavior.  This reinforces a recurring theme: not every PHGG grade is functionally interchangeable.

6.8 Acacia fiber

Acacia fiber, commonly known as gum arabic, is a complex, highly branched polysaccharide system containing arabinogalactan structures and protein-associated fractions. Its structure makes it particularly interesting in beverages.

Despite its relatively high molecular mass, gum arabic can produce comparatively low-viscosity solutions. Its protein-associated components contribute to its ability to stabilize oil-in-water emulsions. Acacia has a well-established history in beverage flavor emulsions, including citrus oil systems. 

However, emulsion stability is not simply a function of whether the gum remains chemically intact. The species, protein-associated fraction, ionic environment, pH, and processing history can all affect performance.

In one study, gum acacia beverage emulsions were less stable at pH 2.5 than at pH 4.5 or 5.5, and the two acacia species evaluated differed in their response.  For acidic beverages, acacia can be an excellent candidate, particularly where both fiber and emulsion functionality are desired. But it is important to distinguish polymer retention from emulsion stability.

6.9 Pectin

Pectin is another category where molecular structure dictates function.

Commercial pectins vary in molecular weight, degree of esterification, amidation, and distribution of charged groups. High-methoxyl pectin and low-methoxyl pectin behave differently in response to pH, soluble solids, and calcium.

Acidic conditions can support particular pectin gelation mechanisms while also promoting depolymerization during excessive thermal exposure. This is why pectin can function beautifully in a fruit preparation and still lose viscosity when subjected to an inappropriate processing schedule.

For pectin, formulators must consider not only hydrolysis but also calcium availability, esterification chemistry, ionic strength, and interactions with proteins. The best pectin is not necessarily the one with the highest molecular weight. It is the one with the correct structural properties for the intended application.

6.10 Beta-glucans

Beta-glucans, particularly those derived from oats and barley, are important because their molecular weight is closely connected to their viscosity-related functionality. Their polymer chains can undergo molecular-weight reduction during processing, depending on temperature, pH, mechanical conditions, and residual enzyme activity.

For products using beta-glucan to deliver viscosity or support a physiological claim, maintaining appropriate molecular characteristics can be especially important. A finished product may retain measurable beta-glucan mass while exhibiting a different viscosity profile because its molecular-weight distribution has shifted.

That is why measuring only the amount of beta-glucan may not adequately describe functional retention.

6.11 Xylooligosaccharides and Arabinoxylan Oligosaccharides

Xylooligosaccharides (XOS) and arabinoxylan oligosaccharides (AXOS) represent another important group of soluble carbohydrates. XOS generally contains xylose units connected through β-(1→4) linkages. AXOS may also contain arabinose substitutions along the xylan backbone.

These substitutions influence solubility, fermentation characteristics, and interactions with other food components. Chain length and branching influence their behavior, but neither variable independently determines stability. For low-pH applications, acid hydrolysis must be evaluated experimentally.

XOS can offer attractive prebiotic functionality at relatively low inclusion levels, but the effective dose depends on the specific preparation and the physiological endpoint being considered. In practice, I would evaluate XOS and AXOS as specialty functional ingredients rather than automatically selecting them as primary bulk-replacement fibers. Their cost and intended physiological functionality may make them more appropriate for targeted fortification than for replacing ten or fifteen grams of sucrose.

7.1 HTST processing

High-temperature short-time processing can be advantageous for certain acid-sensitive soluble fibers because it minimizes thermal residence time. However, the relevant variable is not simply the temperature in the holding tube.

A complete evaluation should include preheating, residence time, holding-tube exposure, regeneration, and cooling. A product that experiences 95°C for twenty seconds may undergo less hydrolysis than one held at 80°C for thirty minutes. Whether that occurs depends on the reaction kinetics of the specific fiber.

7.2 UHT processing

UHT processing presents a different challenge. Although residence times are extremely short, temperatures can exceed 135°C. Some soluble fibers tolerate this exposure well, particularly in neutral-pH systems. Others may undergo measurable molecular changes.

For protein-containing beverages, formulators must also consider protein aggregation, mineral interactions, fouling, and viscosity changes. The fiber may not be the only ingredient undergoing structural modification.

7.3 Retorting

Retorting can be particularly challenging because the total thermal exposure may be substantial. In acidic systems, the combination of extended residence time and elevated temperature can increase the risk of hydrolysis for susceptible carbohydrate structures.

For acid-sensitive fructans, I would be particularly cautious. Where permitted by the process and formulation, adding the fiber after the most severe heating step may be advantageous, but any such approach must preserve validated microbial safety and aseptic integrity.

7.4 Hot-fill beverages

Hot filling introduces a frequently overlooked variable: cooling time. A beverage may reach its target filling temperature, but the fiber remains exposed to elevated temperatures until the product cools.

Large containers, insulating packaging, and slow cooling can increase cumulative thermal exposure. For acid-sensitive fibers, the entire thermal profile should be measured. Not just the filler temperature.

The significance of water activity

Water activity is another variable that deserves attention. Hydrolysis requires water, but the relationship between water activity and degradation is not necessarily linear. In a dilute beverage, abundant water provides a favorable environment for hydrolytic reactions when the linkage chemistry and pH permit them.

In a concentrated syrup or confectionery matrix, lower water availability can alter reaction pathways. However, low moisture does not guarantee chemical stability. At elevated temperatures, other reactions, including dehydration, caramelization, and Maillard-related chemistry where appropriate reactants are present, can become important.

For a formulator, the lesson is simple: a fiber’s stability in an aqueous laboratory solution does not automatically predict its behavior in a high-solids confectionery system.

In these systems, soluble fiber should be evaluated as part of the total carbohydrate and moisture-management system.

8.4 Confectionery

In confectionery, soluble fibers can contribute bulk and solids while reducing reliance on conventional sugars. However, no single soluble fiber reproduces every functional characteristic of sucrose.

Sucrose contributes sweetness, crystallization behavior, solids, glass formation, water activity management, and texture. Replacing it with soluble fiber changes several variables simultaneously.

For hard candy, the formulator must evaluate glass-transition temperature, residual moisture, hygroscopicity, and the potential for stickiness.

For gummies, fiber selection must be integrated with the gelling system, acidification sequence, and final solids.

In both applications, low-pH exposure at elevated temperature can be particularly relevant.

9. Chain Length, Viscosity, and Mouthfeel

One of the most useful relationships in polymer science is the connection between molecular weight and viscosity.

In general, higher-molecular-weight polymers can produce greater viscosity than lower-molecular-weight polymers at comparable concentrations.

However, the magnitude of that effect depends on polymer structure, solvent conditions, concentration, and molecular conformation.

9.1 Why long chains can thicken more effectively

Longer polymer chains can occupy greater hydrodynamic volume and, at sufficient concentrations, become entangled. These interactions increase resistance to flow. Shorter chains generally have less opportunity for entanglement. This explains why enzymatically hydrolyzing native guar gum can dramatically reduce viscosity. But it does not mean that every long-chain fiber produces high viscosity.

Highly branched polymers can exhibit relatively compact molecular conformations. Acacia fiber provides an excellent example. Despite containing high-molecular-weight fractions, it can produce relatively low-viscosity solutions compared with some linear hydrocolloids.

9.2 Chain length also influences osmolality

At a fixed mass concentration, shorter carbohydrate molecules generally produce a greater number of dissolved particles than longer polymers, assuming comparable molecular composition and dissolution. That can increase osmolality.

This is particularly important in high-fiber beverages and products intended for gastrointestinal comfort. Two formulations delivering the same number of grams of dietary fiber may have different osmolality because their molecular-weight distributions differ.

For concentrated liquid products, this is another reason to evaluate more than viscosity alone.

9.3 Molecular weight and fermentation

Chain length can also influence the rate and location of microbial fermentation. Certain shorter oligosaccharides are rapidly fermented by intestinal microorganisms. Other fibers may ferment more gradually.

However, fermentation behavior also depends on glycosidic linkages, branching, accessibility, microbiome composition, and dose. A shorter DP does not automatically guarantee faster fermentation across unrelated fiber families.

For developers making prebiotic claims, ingredient-specific human evidence is considerably more useful than assumptions based on chain length.

10. Best Practices for Low-pH and Thermally Processed Products

If I were developing a new fiber-enriched beverage tomorrow, this is the process I would follow.

Step 1: Establish the finished-product conditions

Before selecting the fiber, define the actual processing and storage environment. At minimum, document the finished-product pH, thermal process, maximum product temperature, total hot residence time, target shelf life, packaging, and expected distribution temperature.

Also establish whether the product requires clarity, viscosity, suspension, or emulsion stabilization.

These specifications determine which fiber families deserve consideration.

Step 2: Select candidates by chemistry

For a clear beverage at pH 3.0 undergoing thermal processing, I would prioritize ingredients with documented stability under comparable conditions.

Resistant dextrin, certain soluble tapioca or corn fibers, PHGG, and acacia fiber would be reasonable initial candidates.

I would treat inulin and FOS more cautiously because of their known susceptibility to acid-catalyzed hydrolysis. That does not eliminate them. It simply means they need stronger process-specific validation.

Step 3: Obtain the right supplier information

A useful supplier technical package should contain more than a solubility statement.

I would request the dietary fiber content and analytical method, molecular-weight distribution, DP profile where available, residual sugar composition, moisture content, viscosity characteristics, pH stability data, and heat-processing studies.

If a supplier claims that an ingredient is acid and heat stable, I would ask for the test conditions. At what pH? At what temperature? For how long? In what matrix? And what analytical endpoint was used? Without those details, the claim has limited value.

Step 4: Hydrate before acidification when appropriate

For many soluble fibers, it is advantageous to disperse and hydrate the ingredient before reducing the system to its final acidic pH. This can minimize localized exposure to high acid concentrations and improve dissolution. However, the ideal addition sequence depends on the fiber.

Some ingredients require high shear or elevated temperature for complete hydration. Others dissolve readily under ambient conditions. The sequence should be optimized for the specific ingredient and finished product.

Step 5: Minimize unnecessary hot holding

Once the fiber is incorporated, avoid extended thermal exposure that provides no processing benefit. For acid-sensitive ingredients, unnecessary hot holding can increase degradation risk.

Shorter thermal residence time may improve retention, provided that the validated food-safety process is maintained.

Step 6: Evaluate the actual commercial process

Benchtop stability studies are useful screening tools. They are not substitutes for pilot-scale and commercial validation.

A commercial heat exchanger introduces temperature gradients, residence-time distributions, and mechanical conditions that may not be represented by a laboratory water bath.

Where fiber integrity is critical, collect samples before processing, immediately after processing, and throughout storage.

Step 7: Measure the right endpoints

The appropriate analytical program depends on the fiber family. For fructans, chromatographic analysis of individual oligosaccharides and free sugars may be useful.

For resistant dextrin and other heterogeneous fibers, validated dietary fiber methods and molecular-weight profiling can provide important information. For hydrocolloids, rheology and molecular-weight distribution may be essential.

The objective is to determine whether the ingredient retains its intended nutritional and functional properties.

Step 8: Conduct a real shelf-life study

An ingredient can survive processing and still change during storage. For acidic beverages, I would consider an initial stability program at refrigerated, ambient, and elevated temperatures, as appropriate to the intended distribution environment.

The testing should evaluate fiber retention, free sugar formation, viscosity, clarity, sedimentation, sensory changes, and microbiological safety. Accelerated testing can help identify risks, but it should not be treated as a perfect substitute for real-time shelf-life verification.

11. A Practical Stability Study for Food and Beverage Formulators

A structured experimental design can provide much more useful information than testing one fiber at one pH. For an initial screening study, I recommend the following approach.

Example fiber stability screening matrix

3.0 pH · 90°C · 30 sec

Measure initial and final dietary fiber, molecular-weight distribution, free sugars, pH, viscosity, and visual stability. Run a matched unheated control and replicate samples.

11.1 Experimental design: isolate the variables

I recommend beginning with a small factorial screening rather than one heroic batch. Test representative grades of resistant dextrin, soluble tapioca fiber, PHGG, acacia, inulin, and FOS at identical delivered-fiber concentrations. Hold ingredient concentration constant on an as-supplied dietary-fiber basis, not merely equal powder weights. For each material, document lot number, moisture, analytical method, and initial sugar profile.

An illustrative screen uses pH 2.8, 3.2, and 4.0; an unheated control; 90°C for 30 seconds; and 90°C for 5 minutes. These are chemistry-screening conditions only, not validated food-safety processes. Randomize run order, prepare at least duplicate independent batches, and use triplicate analytical measurements where practical. If a candidate fails, determine whether pH, thermal residence time, or their interaction drives the change.

11.2 Sample collection and acceptance criteria

Sample immediately after hydration, after acidification, after heat treatment and cooling, and at planned shelf-life intervals. Suggested checkpoints are day 0, 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and the intended end of shelf life. Storage temperatures should reflect the actual distribution system; an elevated-temperature arm is a stress test, not an automatic substitute for real-time data.

Predefine acceptance criteria around the product rather than inventing a universal “stable” percentage. Useful endpoints include eligible dietary fiber per serving, free glucose/fructose/other relevant sugars, molecular-weight distribution, viscosity at a specified shear rate and temperature, turbidity, sediment, flavor, and sensory acceptance. Report analytical uncertainty and mass balance. A loss of viscosity is not necessarily a proportional loss of dietary fiber.

12. Calculate Delivered Fiber, Not Ingredient Weight

The label is not interested in how many grams of powder I tipped into the tank. It is interested in how much qualifying dietary fiber survives in the finished serving. Begin with fiber as supplied, then account for moisture, solids, and verified process retention.

For a powder containing 90% eligible dietary fiber as supplied, delivering 5.0 g requires 5.0 / 0.90 = 5.56 g ingredient per serving. For a 355 mL serving, that corresponds to approximately 15.65 g ingredient per liter. If 90% fiber is instead specified on a dry basis and moisture is 5%, fiber as supplied is 0.90 × 0.95 = 85.5%, requiring 5.85 g ingredient per serving.

For a syrup containing 70% solids with 90% eligible fiber in those solids, as-supplied fiber is 0.70 × 0.90 = 63%, so a 5.0 g fiber target requires 7.94 g syrup. These are theoretical inputs, not finished-product guarantees. If validated processing retention is 95%, divide the target by the additional factor 0.95. Do not assume that a measured molecular-weight shift means a matching percentage loss of qualifying fiber.

Formula: ingredient required (g/serving) = target qualifying fiber (g/serving) ÷ [as-supplied qualifying fiber fraction × validated retention fraction]. Use retention only when supported by a suitable analytical method and the actual process.

13. Analytical Methods and the Regulatory Reality

A generic “total fiber” certificate is not enough for every carbohydrate. AOAC 2009.01 and 2011.25 are integrated methods that capture relevant low-molecular-weight nondigestible carbohydrates more comprehensively than certain older methods; AOAC 2017.16 is another integrated approach. The appropriate method still depends on the ingredient, matrix, and regulatory purpose. For fructans, dedicated fructan methods and chromatographic profiling may be useful alongside total dietary fiber analysis. Verify the laboratory method and its limitations before comparing suppliers or declaring label values. [5–7]

Under 21 CFR 101.9(c)(6)(i), dietary fiber includes specified intrinsic and intact nondigestible carbohydrates and isolated or synthetic nondigestible carbohydrates determined by FDA to have beneficial physiological effects. FDA also identifies additional isolated or synthetic fibers for which it intends to exercise enforcement discretion. A supplier calling an ingredient “soluble,” “prebiotic,” or “resistant” does not by itself establish that every gram is eligible for Nutrition Facts declaration. Review the exact identity, FDA status, analytical method, and documentation. [8, 9]

For mixtures, maintain the supporting records required by 21 CFR 101.9(g)(10) when conventional analytical methods cannot distinguish qualifying from nonqualifying carbohydrate fractions. The finished food, not the marketing brochure, is the compliance endpoint. Nutrient-content and prebiotic claims warrant their own substantiation review. [8]

14. Prebiotic Activity Is Not the Same as Processing Survival

Prebiotic describes a substrate selectively utilized by host microorganisms that confers a health benefit. That is a physiological proposition requiring suitable evidence, not a synonym for fermentable fiber or a promise that a molecule survived the kettle. The original ingredient, its hydrolysis products, the administered dose, and the human evidence all matter. [10]

If a fiber is partially depolymerized, the resulting material may remain fermentable, but its physiological effects cannot simply be assumed identical. When prebiotic positioning is commercially important, connect chemical characterization and process-retention results to evidence for the actual ingredient and dose. Avoid using an in-vitro fermentation result as though it were a finished-product human clinical outcome.

15. A Formulator’s Decision Framework

For a clear, acidic, thermally processed beverage, my first screening group would usually be grade-specific resistant dextrin, selected soluble tapioca/corn fibers, PHGG, and acacia. I would put inulin and FOS through a more demanding hydrolysis study before relying on them for an end-of-shelf-life fiber target. That is a prioritization, not a declaration that one family always wins.

For a bar or confection, the decision changes: solids, glass transition, water activity, stickiness, and texture may outweigh optical clarity. For emulsified beverages, acacia’s interfacial performance may matter more than its viscosity. For viscosity-led systems, pectin or beta-glucan may be useful even though their structural integrity requires close monitoring. Match the molecule to the job.

My go/no-go decision rests on four questions: Does the finished product deliver the intended qualifying fiber? Does its sugar profile remain acceptable? Does the food still function and taste right? Can the process consistently reproduce those outcomes at scale? If any answer is unknown, the formula is still in development.

16.  Conclusion: Formulate for the Finish Line

Soluble fiber is not a single functional ingredient, and solubility is not a stability guarantee. Chain length, linkage chemistry, branching, pH, temperature, residence time, matrix composition, and storage history all influence what remains in the finished product. The smartest formulation is not the one with the prettiest supplier specification. It is the one that delivers reproducible nutritional, sensory, and processing performance after the actual commercial journey.

Start with the finished-product conditions. Choose candidates by chemistry. Measure what matters before and after processing. Calculate delivered, eligible fiber rather than powder weight. Then verify shelf life. The fiber that enters the tank needs to earn its place on the label when the consumer opens the package.

Colostrum vs. Collagen: Which One Should You Take?

A surprisingly common query I get is colostrum vs. collagen—which is better? Maybe it’s the fact that they both start with the same three letters. Maybe it’s that they’re both alternative health darlings currently enjoying a huge uptick in interest.

Whatever the reason for their connection in people’s minds, I thought it would be a good idea to do a quick write-up on how colostrum and collagen might interact in the body, what the relative benefits are, and whether you can take them both together.

Let’s dive right in.

Where Do Colostrum and Collagen Benefits Overlap?

Contrary to what the influencers might tell you, there isn’t a ton of research showing that bovine colostrum has a wide range of benefits for adults. It’s definitely helpful for newborns, since their guts are incredibly permeable, and colostrum is literally the first food they’re supposed to eat. Even cow colostrum has been shown to improve outcomes for newborns and premature infants. No arguments there.

But I’d caution against expecting too much from colostrum supplements. The best case for adding it to your diet as an adult is improving gut health, reducing intestinal permeability, and improving immune function—particularly for athletes.

Colostrum has been heavily researched for its ability to reduce gut permeability in various populations, including athletes, medical patients, and everyday folks.1-4 People who take bovine colostrum supplements often report better gut health and a more intact gut lining. Zonulin, a gut compound that increases permeability, frequently drops with colostrum intake.

Researchers aren’t entirely sure how bovine colostrum benefits the gut. Some people claim it regulates specific growth factors in the gut lining that help repair and support its structure. That’s colostrum’s primary role in infancy: improving gut viability so the immune system can develop, enabling digestion of food instead of relying on the womb for sustenance. Thanks in large part to colostrum benefits, this new member of the human race is able to handle what the world throws at it and develop a healthy immune response and gut lining

The challenge is that the adult gut isn’t as permeable as a newborn’s by design. For instance, the IGF-1 in bovine colostrum isn’t absorbed or utilized by adults.5

Colostrum can also regulate inflammation and help maintain iron balance in athletes, largely due to its lactoferrin content.6 Lactoferrin is an immune protein that improves iron absorption while reducing free iron—an inflammatory agent—circulating in the body. Athletes taking colostrum for instance see higher spikes in inflammation during workouts but lower resting inflammation, showing it promotes “smart” inflammation.

Meanwhile, collagen benefits for gut health are much more direct. Collagen provides the actual building blocks—amino acids like glycine—for repairing and growing the gut lining. Whether it turns out that colostrum does indeed trigger growth factors that help the gut repair itself (by no means confirmed), or if it just lowers inflammation via lactoferrin which allows the repair to proceed, you’ll need extra collagen to make those repairs.

Colostrum assists with gut repair while collagen provides a substrate required for the repair to occur.

And so the two compounds are not competing with each other, but potentially complementary. Both colostrum and collagen improve gut health, enhance skin health and appearance, and boost immune function by different but complementary routes.

Can You Take Them Together?

Yes, you can absolutely take collagen and colostrum together. In fact, I’d argue that if colostrum works, they’re almost made for each other. You don’t need to mix them in the same shake or take them simultaneously; just include both in your routine for maximum benefit.

Adding both colostrum and collagen supplements can create a synergistic effect, particularly for gut and immune health. Collagen serves as the substrate, while colostrum provides potential activation via growth factors.

When Is One More Useful Than the Other?

It depends on your dietary needs. If you only take one, take collagen. Collagen is like the fourth macronutrient, providing glycine—an amino acid we no longer get enough of in modern diets. This makes collagen essential for maintaining skin, bones, blood vessels, and more. It’s also widely beneficial, whether you’re looking for joint health, anti-aging, or gut support.

If you regularly consume foods like bone broth, gelatinous meats, or stews, you may already be getting plenty of collagen. In that case, colostrum supplements could add more value, especially for individuals with immune or gut issues that dietary changes haven’t resolved.

Colostrum, derived from the mature milk of cows after giving birth, is essentially absent from the diet unless you specifically seek it out. It’s best suited for people dealing with gut inflammation or immune dysregulation. Athletes might benefit from colostrum’s ability to regulate iron and inflammation, while others may see improved resistance to respiratory infections.

Here’s How I Break It Down:

Who should take collagen?

Everyone.

Who should take colostrum?

  • Athletes with iron balance issues.

  • Adults prone to upper respiratory tract infections.

  • Individuals with leaky gut or gut inflammation.

If you’re on a budget, you can likely replicate most of the colostrum supplement benefits with a high-quality whey isolate (which contains some lactoferrin, but not as much as colostrum) and a dedicated lactoferrin supplement. My guess is that lactoferrin is doing much of the heavy lifting for colostrum’s benefits.

Final Thoughts on Colostrum vs. Collagen

Both colostrum and collagen offer unique benefits, but collagen stands out as the more universally essential supplement. Its role in collagen production, gut lining repair, and overall health makes it indispensable for most people. Bovine colostrum can complement collagen, especially in specific scenarios, but it isn’t as necessary or widely applicable.

For most, collagen is the clear winner. Still, if you have unique needs or simply want to experiment with bovine colostrum benefits, adding both to your regimen can offer the best of both worlds.

Now, the study had some interesting things, right? There was some nuance there. They readily admitted that protein restriction acts differently in sedentary versus active individuals. Those who are actively training have a higher supposedly “safe” window of protein consumption. Maybe they have a higher tolerance for protein. But overall, the thrust of the paper, and what most people will take away from it, and what your vegan friends are no doubt bragging to you about, is that less protein is better for longevity.

I don’t think it’s as simple as that. Let me explain.

Does Eating Meat Reduce Longevity?

Much of the research rests on the assumption that specific amino acids in excess promote aging. The one they say most is methionine. It’s found in dairy, muscle meat, eggs, and other animal products, and absolutely, excessive amounts of it can promote aging in animal models, and likely humans. No argument there.

But what these studies never explore is the reality that methionine is balanced by another amino acid: glycine. Glycine is almost entirely absent from the classic animal protein products like meat or eggs or dairy, and it’s found almost exclusively in connective tissue, bones, and skin.

There is a classic study where they put rodents on a high-methionine diet, and sure enough, those rats died pretty young. But they took another group and gave them glycine, same amount of methionine, just with extra glycine, and those guys lived long, healthy lives, just as long as the rodents who lived on a restricted-methionine diet.1-2

We see echoes of this in the human literature. Tons of research finds that low glycine levels in the serum predict type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and many of the other diseases classically linked to meat consumption.

Now let’s look at Hong Kong, which is one of the longest-living populations on Earth, who also happen to eat the most meat of anyone on Earth. But the meat they’re eating is not just steaks and burgers and roasts. They’re also eating skin, snouts, feet, and other animal foods that are high in glycine.

This is why collagen is so essential for the modern meat eater who doesn’t want to eat the obscure stuff like skin and bones. Thirty to 40 grams of collagen a day provides enough glycine to balance out all the muscle meat and other animal protein you eat. And although we don’t have direct human trials of balancing out methionine with glycine and looking at lifespan, the circumstantial evidence is pretty strong that it’s going to have good effects.

Except that in the real world, when you see people who actually practice this, these are people who live this lifestyle, they’ve read the studies, they’ve convinced themselves that protein restriction is key to living long. They are generally pretty unwell. They’re frail, always cold, have low energy, and generally aren’t the picture of health we are imagining for ourselves.

Now, they may be better off and look better than the general population in some ways. They tend to be thinner, at least, and, you know, even their biomarkers look pretty good. But I refuse to believe that this is the path to optimal health. You may limp by a bit longer. You may slowly walk into old age, but can you still run hills? Can you play? Are you strong?

And that’s where the study loses me.

My Beef with the Findings

Nothing against the authors themselves. It’s pretty impossible to cover every angle, and as I said before, they did a pretty fair job of noting that active exercisers have a higher tolerance of protein. But the true path to health is on the teeter-totter, the seesaw, the waxing and the waning.

It’s a big steak after a workout and then maybe skipping breakfast the next day and just having some fish with salad for lunch. It’s periods of abundance and periods of restriction. It is high-protein days and lower-protein days. We see this everywhere. We see it in wild animals, predators who eat and gorge themselves for a day or two and then go for several days without eating anything. You see this in the fasting habits of monks and other religious men. Christianity even has protein fasting built into it.

No, I’m not saying that you should fast for three days after you eat a big steak like a tiger or wolf, or eliminate protein for a 14-day period. I’m just saying that the world is cyclical. That seems embedded in our environment and also our biology.

This is why, over the years, I have settled on not daily protein targets, but four- to five-day protein targets, even weekly protein targets. I want to eat an average of 120 grams of protein a day over the course of a week. Some days it’ll be 180, other days it’ll be 70. It doesn’t matter so much day-to-day as it does across the week.

Do studies like this give you pause, or do you remain confident—as I do—in the healthfulness of an “high” (read, “sufficient”) protein omnivorous diet? Let me know in the comments.

What Is the Primal Blueprint?

My basic premise is this:

The Primal Blueprint is a set of simple instructions that allows you to control how your genes express themselves in order to build the strongest, healthiest body possible, taking clues from evolutionary biology.

Sometimes we get so lost in the science of human biology that we just can’t see the forest for the trees. We overlook the simplicity and ease with which we could all be achieving exceptional health and fitness.

You can have a tremendous impact on how your genes express themselves simply by providing your cells with the right environments.

You may already have a fair understanding of how the human genome evolved to where it is today based on the environmental and behavioral factors that shaped our ancestors’ lives.

This list is essentially the original Primal Blueprint—the behaviors that enabled them to become healthy, robust, and resilient human beings, and to pass those traits down to us.

The 10 Primal Blueprint Laws

The goal of the modern Primal Blueprint is not to revert to a caveman lifestyle.

Instead, it asks:

How can we learn from our past to shape a healthier future?

These 10 laws form the foundation of optimal health and longevity.

1. Eat lots of animals, insects, and plants

Focus on:

  • Quality protein (meat, poultry, fish)

  • Colorful vegetables

  • Some fruits (mostly berries)

  • Healthy fats (avocado, olive oil, nuts, full-fat dairy if tolerated)

Observe portion control over time rather than meal by meal.

2. Avoid poisonous things

Limit exposure to:

  • Chemical toxins (pesticides, herbicides, fungicides)

  • Processed foods

  • Sugar and grains

  • Trans and hydrogenated fats

  • Mercury in certain fish

3. Move around a lot (mostly at a slow pace)

Walk as much as possible every day.

Aim for 2 to 5 hours per week of low-level aerobic activity:

  • Walking

  • Hiking

  • Easy cycling

  • Swimming

4. Lift heavy things

Spend 30–45 minutes, 2–3 times per week lifting weights or resistance.

Focus on full-body movements:

  • Pushing

  • Pulling

  • Carrying

  • Climbing

5. Run really fast every once in a while

Do occasional high-intensity sprint bursts, such as:

  • Short hill sprints

  • Beach sprints

  • Intense cycling intervals

6. Get lots of sleep

Sleep is critical for:

  • Energy

  • Immune function

  • Overall health

Prioritize it.

7. Play

Engage in active play regularly.

Play helps:

  • Reduce stress

  • Improve creativity

  • Apply fitness in real life

8. Get some sunlight every day

Moderate sun exposure helps:

  • Produce vitamin D

  • Improve mood

Avoid burning, but don’t avoid the sun completely.

9. Avoid stupid mistakes

Eliminate obvious risks:

  • Wear seat belts

  • Avoid drugs and smoking

  • Stay aware of your surroundings

10. Use your mind

Keep your brain active:

  • Read

  • Create

  • Socialize
