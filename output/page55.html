Why Mikel Arteta is deliberately disrupting Arsenal’s preparation

(Image: The Independent)

Arsenal F.C. manager Mikel Arteta has sparked significant online discussion after revealing an unconventional method he used during pre-season to deliberately disrupt his players’ usual preparation (see here). Arteta said he intentionally had the team arrive late to the stadium, delayed meals, made the dressing room uncomfortably hot and even reduced the number of massage beds available. These planned disruptions interfered with the players’ normal pre-match routines, with Arteta believing that exposing players and staff to these situations could help them become more adaptable when unexpected challenges arise during the season.

While this approach may seem unusual, it closely resembles a psychological technique known as stress exposure training. This involves exposing individuals to controlled and realistic stressors, so they become more accustomed to handling pressure and disruption when similar situations occur for real.

Typically, coaches strive to create the best possible environment for their athletes to prepare and perform. However, Arteta’s approach presents an interesting alternative. Occasionally introducing controlled stress and disruption into the training environment may help athletes become more adaptable and better prepared to cope when things do not go according to plan.

How the Dallas Cowboys are using sports science to beat the heat

(Image: Studio Outside)

Earlier this month, a compelling story emerged about how the Dallas Cowboys, an American football team, are navigating the challenges posed by extreme weather (see here). The Dallas area had gone nearly two months without any measurable rainfall and was facing sweltering heat, prompting the team’s sports science and medical staff to raise concerns about players’ hydration during training sessions.

To address these issues, the Cowboys have gone beyond just monitoring temperature and weather conditions. They have implemented sweat testing and installed specialised urinals that evaluate players’ hydration levels when they urinate. Armed with this data, the sports science and medical staff can better assess whether players are fit to train outdoors in the extreme heat and humidity or if it’s wiser to hold the session indoors.

This situation underscores the importance of looking beyond just the weather when deciding on outdoor training in extreme heat. It also highlights the significance of understanding how each athlete is responding to these conditions, enabling coaches and medical staff to ensure that players are adequately hydrated and ready to handle the rigours of training in such intense heat.

Coaching tips you don’t get on social media!

James de Lacey (Image: Canvas Rebel)

Our very own James de Lacey recently shared some excellent practical coaching tips on LinkedIn for delivering team training sessions more effectively.

Firstly, when addressing players, ensure that all of them are positioned in front of you rather than behind you. This means avoiding the common practice of having players form a circle around the coach. By positioning everyone in front, it becomes much easier to communicate clearly with the entire group.

Secondly, when communicating with players outdoors, take into account the position of the sun. Players should not have to look directly into the sun while receiving instructions. Instead, the coach should face the sun, even if it feels slightly uncomfortable, rather than expecting the entire team to do so. With the sun behind them, players will be in a better position to focus, listen, and absorb instructions.

Lastly, there shouldn’t be a noticeable divide between where the warm-up ends and training begins. Excessive stretching and dynamic movements should be completed by players in their own time before training, allowing the session to flow naturally into the main training activities.

Schiacciata di Patate is a crispy Italian flatbread made with potatoes, rosemary and lots of pecorino cheese. It’s beautifully golden and crispy on the outside with a soft and creamy middle. This is such a delicious snack, antipasto or bite to serve with drinks.

Recipe overview

  • The recipe: a thin and crispy potato flatbread made without yeast.
  • Flavour and texture: Cheesy potato with fragrant rosemary, crispy and crunchy on the outside with a soft and creamy middle.
  • Prep and bake time: 1 hour 10 minutes
  • Serves: 8 large slices or 16 small
  • Perfect for: serving to guests as an aperitivo or antipasto with drinks.
Jump to recipe

There are lots of different versions of Schiacciata di Patate. Some are made with traditional leavened dough topped with potatoes, while others are thin and crispy savoury flatbreads made without yeast.

This style of schiacciata is something my mother-in-law makes often when we visit. She makes it as a quick snack, and it’s absolutely delicious.

Our version uses lots of grated potato in the base with Pecorino Romano and fresh rosemary, then it’s topped with thin slices of potato and more cheese before baking until golden and crispy.

It’s the perfect snack to serve with drinks for an aperitivo or serve it with cured meats, cheese and olives as part of an antipasto spread.

Ingredient notes

  • Potatoes: I like to use a floury potato for a soft and creamy middle. I use Maris Piper, but if you’re in the US, Russet is a great option.
  • Pecorino Romano: I love using salty Pecorino Romano for this, but Parmigiano Reggiano also works really well. You can even use a combination of both.
  • Flour: I use Italian 00 flour, but all-purpose flour will work just as well.
  • Rosemary: fresh rosemary and potato is such a delicious combination, but fresh thyme will also work really well.

Recipe tips and FAQs

Squeeze excess water from the potato: it’s important to squeeze excess water from the potato after grating it so the mixture isn’t too wet. I put the potatoes in a colander and squeeze them with my hands.

Use baking parchment: instead of using a butter knife or spoon, use a sheet of baking parchment on top of the potato mixture to spread it out into a thin, even layer. It makes the process super easy.

Grating the potatoes: I use the grater attachment on my food processor, which takes seconds to grate all the potatoes. If you don’t have this, you can use a box grater instead. If you are grating them in batches, I recommend keeping the whole peeled potatoes submerged in cold water until you are ready to grate them to stop them oxidising.

Slicing the potatoes: I use a knife to thinly slice the potatoes for the topping and do this after making the base filling. If you have a mandoline, then use that as it’s perfect for getting super thin slices. I have tried the slicer attachment on my food processor, but it cut the potato a little too thick, and the schiacciata needed an extra 10 minutes to cook.

What does Schiacciata mean?

Schiacciata comes from the Italian verb ‘schiacciare’, which means to squash, flatten, or press. It’s commonly associated with a thin and crispy Tuscan focaccia but is also used to describe many other Italian recipes with a thin or flat texture.

Schiacciata di Patate (Crispy Italian Potato Flatbread)

Use the camera icon to toggle the step by step photos on and off. A crispy and delicious Italian potato flatbread made with rosemary and pecorino cheese. Course antipasto Cuisine Italian Prep Time 20 minutes minutes Cook Time 50 minutes minutes Total Time 1 hour hour 10 minutes minutes Servings 8 servings Calories 222kcal Author Emily Wyper

Equipment

  • 11×14 inch (28x35cm) Baking tray
  • Baking parchment

Ingredients

Schiacciata base

  • 570 g (1 lb 4 oz) Potatoes (Maris Piper or Russet)
  • 140 g (1 cup) Italian 00 or all-purpose flour
  • 120 ml (½ cup) water
  • 70 g (1 cup) Pecorino Romano finely grated
  • 1 large sprig rosemary finely chopped
  • Salt and pepper
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil plus extra for greasing

Topping

  • 250 g (9oz) Potatoes very finely sliced
  • 30 g Pecorino Romano (generous ½ cup) finely grated
  • 1-2 tablespoons olive oil
  • Salt and pepper

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 180℃/350℉ fan or 200℃/400℉ conventional.
  • Lightly grease a 11x14inch (28x35cm) baking tray and line with baking parchment (this helps the parchment stick to the tray). Set aside.

To make the schiacciata base

  • Peel and grate the potatoes (you can use the grater attachment on a food processor or large holes on a box grater).
  • Squeeze as much water out of them as possible with your hands, then place them in a large mixing bowl.
  • Add the finely chopped rosemary, grated pecorino, a pinch of black pepper and a generous pinch of salt and mix until combined.
  • Next, add the olive oil and flour and mix well. Finally, add the water and mix it into the potato until everything is fully incorporated.
  • Drizzle around 1 tablespoon of olive oil on the parchment lined tray and tip the mixture on top. Place another sheet of baking parchment on top and use your hands to spread the mixture into a thin even layer all over the tray.
  • Peel off the top sheet of baking parchment and set aside.

To make the topping

  • Peel the remaining potatoes and cut them as thinly as possible. Arrange them in an even layer all over the schiacciata. Sprinkle over a pinch of salt and pepper, then the finely grated Pecorino Romano.
  • Drizzle 1-2 tablespoons of olive oil over the top and bake in the oven for 45-50 minutes until crispy and golden all over.
  • Remove from the oven and transfer the schiacciata to a cooling rack for 5-10 minutes before cutting and serving.

Notes

  • Storage: The schiacciata is best eaten while still warm from the oven, when it tastes its best, but it will keep well for 1-2 days in the fridge.
  • Reheating: Although the schiacciata can be eaten cold, it’s much better reheated. Reheat in the oven at 180C/350F for 10-15 minutes.

This is the butternut squash soup I’ve been making when I want something more substantial than a typical silky puree. Red lentils give it body and a protein boost, while tomatoes and tomato paste offset the sweetness of the squash. Garlic, yellow peppers, and fennel seeds keep things dynamic, and a couple handfuls of cashews blend into beautiful creaminess. It’s an ideal lunch soup, and you’ll find me topping it with whatever is waiting for me in the refrigerator. Lately that has meant these roasted cherry tomatoes, an herby marjoram oil (recipe included below), crumbled goat cheese, toasted pepitas, and sometimes, if I’m going all-in, an egg.

A Note on the Squash

I note this in a few places on this page, but it deserves emphasis. You can make this soup using butternut squash as well as a wide range of other winter squash, or even sweet potato. I love using red kuri squash with its dense sweet flesh and deep color. Kabocha squash is another regular go-to, along with acorn squash and adorable honeynut squash. I’m seeing honeynuts more and more alongside traditional butternut squash these days, they look like pint-sized versions.

For the Silkiest Soup

When I’m feeling lazy, I often blend soups like this using a simple hand blender. It makes for easy cleanup. And for this exact soup, the hand blender does a pretty good job – really smooth, but not hyper-silky. All of which is a long way of saying that this soup goes next-level when you use a high-speed blender, and the result is arguably worth the extra effort. But don’t be deterred if a hand blender is what you use! It can still do a good job, the photos here are of a version I made using an old-school hand blender.

Best Toppings for Butternut Squash Soup

You can see in the photos I topped this lunch with a bright herby-olive oil drizzle. There’s cherry tomatoes, a bit of goat cheese, and some crunchy pepitas. But this soup is a real chameleon and welcomes a wide range of topping directions. Some other ideas: kale chips, crispy chickpeas, magic sauce, chile crisp, homemade labneh, roasted lemon chutney, candied walnuts, oil-cured black olives, toasted sliced almonds, toasted pumpkin seeds (you can make these with the seeds from whatever squash you’re using).

Make Ahead and Freeze

This is a favorite soup to make-ahead and freeze in batches for quick, healthful meals down the line. Reheat and add toppings before serving. If the soup thickens up too much for your liking, add a bit of water or broth and re-season.

There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma. 

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO. 

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course. 

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

Exercise Tech to Really Get You Moving

📅 Sep 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

People ask me all the time about exercise tech. Do I have any tools, gadgets, gizmos, or devices I’m using or would recommend? After all, the world of fitness tech has exploded. There are things you can strap on, attach to your body, loop around your wrist, or wear on your finger that all claim to make getting fit easier and more enjoyable (and more trackable).

I don’t see the point. If I ever need a device to tell me if I had a great workout or if I’m sleepy or not, it’s time to throw in the towel.

Subscribe now

You can spend $1000 on a smartwatch with a $30 a month subscription fee plus Strava, plus Peloton workout subscription, plus the new model every year, plus the smart ring, plus the heart rate variability monitor, plus the meditation app subscription to help you get that HRV back up… or you could spend good money on a high-quality piece of equipment that will last forever and make training enjoyable again.

These are my picks.

Get a good watercraft.

Maybe it’s a kayak. Maybe it’s a standup paddle board. Maybe it’s a surfboard or windsurfing rig. Most people reading this will have some level of water access—and I urge you all to avail yourselves of it. There’s nothing quite so Primal and human as traversing a natural body of water. Spend as much as you’re comfortable, and don’t be afraid to buy used.

My personal recommendation is a standup paddleboard from Starboard. Hard board, if you have the storage for it.

Get a good adventure bike.

I say “adventure bike” because while you can take a mountain or fat tire bike out on the road, you can’t take a road bike on the trails or sand. I know, I know, riding a mountain bike or fat tire bike on the road isn’t “optimal” or “efficient,” but this is all about fun, and having a bike that can go almost everywhere is far more conducive to fun than a bike designed for only one specific type of environment.

I’m partial to Trek for both mountain and fat tire bikes. I have a Trek Farley I use twice a week minimum on Miami Beach, and it’s probably my favorite bike I’ve ever owned. And yes, it works on the street.

Get a good weight vest or rucksack.

For as long as hominids have been bipeds, we’ve been supporting heavy loads and walking for miles underneath them. A million years ago, it was the haunch of an antelope we successfully stole from a larger predator thrown over the shoulder. 2000 years ago, it was 50 pounds of armor, weapons, and personal affects piled on our backs on some campaign in the Mediterranean. Whether you were a farmer, explorer, naturalist, prospector, soldier, or parent, you were carrying heavy things while walking across the environment.

Some call this rucking, some call it loaded carries. The point is that we are designed to carry heavy loads and walk. It’s the entire reason why we have opposable thumbs (for grabbing) and bipedalism (to free up our hands and give us an upright posture that can better support a load).

I recommend GoRuck if you want to ruck and Aduro if you want a weight vest. They go up to 30 pounds, so if you want heavier, I’d go with Rogue.

A gym membership.

For the price of a high end fitness watch, Peloton bike, fancy treadmill, indoor rower, and all the other doodads you want, with monthly subscriptions for all of them, you could find a great gym with a sauna, pool, cold plunge, extensive free and machine weight collection and cardio equipment and, most importantly, a pool of interesting members you can socialize with. I’ve met many great friends and business partners at the gym, and they’re often what kept me coming back day in and day out.

Get good footwear.

Footwear is everything. It’s how we interact with the world and how we generate force—by pushing off to start the battle against gravity. No matter what exercise or sport or activity you’re doing, your kinetic chain starts with the feet. If you’re starting with suboptimal footwear, you’re short-changing yourself.

I recommend Peluva, of course—my own footwear brand that I built from the ground up to be the closest approximation to actually going barefoot. I’d love it if we could go barefoot everywhere, but the modern world we’ve built simply doesn’t allow it. Most of the surfaces we walk, run, and move across aren’t meant for bare feet.

Pie Recipes: Favorite Sweet & Savory Pies

📅 Sep 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Once you learn to bake a good pie, you’ll never look back. It’s one of the most optimistic culinary rituals I know – rolling out buttery dough, preparing the filling, carefully crimping pretty edges. This is a collection of the pie recipes that have become little traditions in my kitchen, the ones that feel the most familiar, delicious, and satisfying to bake and share. I hope you love them as much as I do!

Jump to:

  • Start Here: Pie Crust
  • Fruit & Classic Pies

Start Here: Pie Crust

The cornerstone of many of the pies I bake is an all-butter flaky pie crust. It’s a mash-up of everything I’ve learned about pie dough over the years and delivers a beautifully golden, super flaky, all-butter pie crust. Even better, it’s a crust that is easy to make. I use my electric mixer with the paddle attachment to smear butter into flour quickly, and it makes less of a mess than doing it all on a countertop (which I still love to do on occasion). Seriously, this is the one.

  1. All-Butter Flaky Pie Crust

    Most pages about pie crusts are going to point you to their “best ever” pie crust, but I’m taking a slightly different approach. I’m going to share a few of the inspirations that have impacted my pie baking over the years along with the pie dough recipe I’ve tweaked and evolved until it’s my version of perfection. This is it.

Fruit & Classic Pies

  1. Shaker Apple Pie

    The defining detail of this traditional Shaker apple pie is the use of rose water in the filling. Sliced apples, brown sugar, and a splash of floral results in a special combination. Pair it with an all-butter crust and you have a beautiful apple pie on your hands.

  2. Berry Pie

    When you have an abundance of blueberries, blackberries, raspberries, or huckleberries, this is the berry pie to make. It’s classic, with a juicy, sugar-kissed, mixed berry filling. Pair it with a flaky, all-butter crust and you have the ideal berry pie on your hands.

  3. Pumpkin Pie

    A classic pumpkin pie recipe with a couple wildcards to make it extra special. The creamy pumpkin custard is made with with a fragrant five spice blend and baked in a flaky, walnut-lined homemade flaky pie crust.

  4. Shaker Lemon Pie

    The defining detail of this lemon Shaker pie is the use of whole lemons for the filling. Thin slices of lemon including the peel, pith, and flesh are macerated in sugar and then turned into a vibrant, flavor-packed, custardy filling. Pair this with a flaky all-butter pie crust and you’ve got something special on your hands.

Cream, Custard & Chocolate Pies

  1. Homemade Coconut Cream Pie

    There are a couple details that take this coconut cream pie to the next level. Like many versions, it is made with a homemade, flaky, all-butter pie crust filled with a creamy coconut pudding. It is finished with clouds of whipped cream and plenty of toasted coconut flakes. Using buttermilk and a confident amount of salt to offset some of the sweetness is how you take this pie from good to great.

  2. Buttermilk Pie

    Buttermilk pies are simple, unassuming, and a breeze to throw together if you keep pie dough at the ready. The buttermilk filling here is a standout – simple, creamy, and maple-sweetened with a kiss of lemony tang and sea salt.

  3. Heavenly Chocolate Pie

    A decadent chocolate pie featuring a honey-sweetened graham cracker crust and a wildcard cream cheese chocolate filling. From the book Country Wisdom & Know-How – the recipe calls for equal parts tofu and cream cheese in the filling. This combination creates a decadent, mousse-like texture that’s also a breeze to cut into precision slices.

Savory Pies

  1. Vegetable Pot Pie

    This hearty vegetable pot pie is made with a flaky, all-butter pie crust encasing a creamy, herbed potato, carrot, pea and onion filling. A homemade savory pie like this is such a treat! There are a couple tips you should know to help you take it from good to exceptional.

  2. Deep Dish Quiche

    The deep dish quiche of your dreams. It’s made with a flaky all-butter crust. The filling is silky smooth and creamy, while still being perfectly sliceable. Switch up the add-ins based on the seasons – roasted cherry tomatoes in summer, winter squash later in the year.

  3. Vegan Shepherd’s Pie

    Imagine a Shepherd’s Pie with the flavors of an Indian samosa – that’s what’s going on here. Vegan, make-ahead, one-dish meal with a split pea (or lentil) base, mashed potato crust, and spices.

  4. Spinach-Mushroom Quiche

    This is a spinach mushroom quiche recipe that features no dairy, no eggs, and no butter. Sounds like it would lack something in the taste department, right? Wrong. It was delicious.

Hand Pies

  1. Apple Cinnamon Hand Pies

    These apple hand pies are the seasonal treat you’d line up for – nostalgic, portable, and flat out delicious. The filling is a slow-baked, miso-swirled apple butter, and the crust is crisp with sesame-sugar spiked with a bit of chile flake.

  2. Peach Hand Pies

    These peach hand pies are next-level good! Made with a flaky, fool-proof, all-butter pie dough and filled with ripe peaches, goat cheese, plus a bit of green chile and basil, they ride the delicious line between savory and sweet.

  3. Mushroom Hand Pies

    Savory pie people, these mushroom hand pies are for you. Mushrooms, charred scallions, sesame, and miso come together in these portable pockets of deliciousness. You get creaminess from a bit of cheese, and each bite delivers the magic of a golden, flaky pie crust.

Antecedentes y Objetivos: En México se reportan 102 especies del género Amanita, de las cuales 30 están presentes en la Cuenca de México. De estas últimas, siete pertenecen a la sección Vaginatae, lo que representa 5.8% de la diversidad de este género a nivel nacional. Esta sección presenta una alta homoplasia de sus caracteres morfológicos, lo que genera especies crípticas en el grupo. También faltan holotipos y claves de identificación taxonómica especializadas en la sección, así como un consistente criterio de clasificación. El objetivo de este trabajo fue caracterizar e identificar a nivel específico ejemplares de la sección Vaginatae depositados en el Fungario FCME, usando rasgos macro y micromorfológicos, así como análisis filogenéticos basados en secuencias de nucleótidos.

Métodos: Se hicieron revisiones macro y microscópicas de las estructuras de importancia taxonómica, basados en literatura especializada del género. Para la determinación de los ejemplares se utilizaron claves de identificación para el género. La extracción de DNA se realizó con un protocolo modificado de CTAB al 3% y se amplificaron las regiones ITS (ITS1, 5.8S e ITS2) y el D1-D2 del 28S del nrDNA. Los amplicones exitosos se secuenciaron en ambos sentidos. Todas las secuencias disponibles de la sección y las generadas en este trabajo se analizaron mediante los métodos filogenéticos probabilísticos Máxima Verosimilitud e Inferencia Bayesiana.

Resultados clave: Con base en la identificación morfológica y en los análisis filogenéticos basados en las regiones ITS y 28S, se confirmó la presencia de Amanita brunneofuliginea en los bosques de Pinus spp. - Abies religiosa del sur de la Cuenca de México, por lo que esta especie se registra por primera vez en el país.

Miso walnut chocolate chip cookies

There are two things I need to tell you about these cookies.

One is that they’re crazy-good. And the other is that they’re huge. The recipe makes just five cookies but each one is so packed with flavor, that you’ll be glad you can have one all to yourself. Your other four friends or guests will be delighted as well. (But they’re quite generous, so it’s okay to share…)

What makes these cookies extra special is their slightly savory, umami note, due to walnuts glazed with white miso. Miso is fermented soybean paste that can be purchased in Asian markets, but has become so popular, you can often find it in grocery stores. In these cookies, it adds a little je ne sais quoi, since it adds a subtle, yet complex note of flavors. Umami is often described as a flavor that’s craveable, and it certainly makes a compelling case for that description in these cookies.

If you don’t have white miso, or don’t want to make the candied walnuts, no problem. Just use toasted walnuts. Pecans, or another favorite nut will also work. You can also leave the nuts out, or swap in dried fruit for them. Do-ahead tip: The walnuts can be made a few days in advance and kept in an airtight container. The glaze will get a little sticky, but the walnuts are perfectly fine to use in these cookies.

Another “secret” to these cookies is to incorporate large chunks of chocolate into the dough. While it may be tempting to use chocolate chips, they’re really best if you chop up chocolate from a bar of good-quality chocolate. I leave the pieces quite rough and irregular, which – when baked – create large pockets and bites of dark chocolate.

I’ve adapted the recipe from SIFT: The Elements of Great Baking by Nicola Lamb. Her book explains many of the hows and whys of baking, as well as offering an array of creative recipes. Some are completely original and others are riffs on classics, and it’s a book that I refer to often and these cookies are a big hit whenever I bake them. I hope you enjoy these cookies as much as I do!

Miso Walnut Chocolate Chip Cookies

Adapted from SIFT by Nicola Lamb When you form the five mounds of dough, avoid compacting them too tight. You want to form them into loose balls rather than rolling them in your hands into smooth rounds. They're best when you chop the chocolate from bars of good-quality dark chocolate, and I leave the pieces fairly large – about the size of almonds, but you can use store-bought chocolate chunks if you wish. I baked my batch of cookies for 15 to 16 minutes, although the original recipe says 18 minutes. All ovens are different, but they're done when they're light brown across the top. The cookies shown in the post were baked on a cloudy day, so may look a bit dark. You want to err on the side of underbaking rather than overbaking. Another tip is that you can sprinkle extra flaky salt on top of each cookie when they come out of the oven. These cookies are best enjoyed the day they’re made but are delicious the next day as well. Store them in an airtight container at room temperature. Course Dessert Cuisine American Keyword chocolate, chocolate chip cookies, cookies, miso, walnuts Servings 5

Ingredients

Miso walnuts

  • 3 tablespoons mirin rice wine
  • 3 tablespoons granulated sugar
  • 3 tablespoons (50g) white miso
  • 1 cup (100g) walnut halves toasted

Cookie dough

  • 6 tablespoons (90g) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) light brown sugar
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) granulated sugar
  • 1 large egg at room temperature
  • 1 3/4 cups (220g) all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 1/4 teaspoons flaky sea salt or 1 teaspoon Diamond Crystal kosher salt, plus more for sprinkling (optional)
  • 9 ounces (255g) bittersweet or semisweet chocolate chopped in large chunks

Instructions

  • To make the miso walnuts, in a medium saucepan or skillet over moderate heat, warm the mirin, the granulated sugar, and the miso, stirring to incorporate and smooth out the ingredients. When the mixture starts to boil, add the walnuts and cook, stirring frequently, until the walnuts are completely coated and the miso mixture has almost all been absorbed by the walnuts, which will take a couple of minutes. They're done when there's only about a tablespoon of the miso syrup in the pan. Remove from heat and let cool, giving them a stir a few times as they cool.
  • To make the cookies, preheat the oven to 375ºF (190ºC). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper.
  • In the bowl of a stand mixer fitted with the paddle attachment on low speed, or by hand, cream the butter and sugars until well-incorporated for about 1 minute. Do not beat the mixture until it's too light or overly fluffy, which can cause the cookies to spread too much during baking.
  • Mix in the egg. Sift the flour and baking soda together in a small bowl, then mix that into the dough along with the salt.
  • Break or cut the walnuts up a little, but not too much, and stir them into the dough, then stir in the chunks of chocolate. You may need to get your hands in there to get everything thoroughly combined, but don't overmix. The dough can be somewhat irregular.
  • Shape the dough into 5 equal mounds on the baking sheet, each about 6 ounces (180g), placing them evenly spaced apart. Bake the cookies for 15-18 minutes, sprinkling them with additional flaky sea salt after they come out of the oven, if you wish. The cookies are done when they are light golden brown across the top. Err on the side of underbaking rather than overbaking.

Notes

Mirin is a sweet rice wine, available in Asian markets and well-stocked supermarkets. There are some substitutes for mirin here.
