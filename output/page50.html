From the 1983 Archives: The North Express – C. Hamilton-Ellis Recalls a Forgotten Railway

📅 Oct 14, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In this archive article, C. Hamilton-Ellis recalls the Midland & South Western Junction Railway and its North Express service. Drawing on his own memories of the railway, he describes its history, locomotives and the journey through the Wiltshire countryside, accompanied by his railway painting.

The North Express and its origins

SUCH a title as the North Express might mean to us, nowadays, anything from a train on its way from Inverness to Wick, to another heading for the Baltic. But this North Express was to be seen at the head of Southampton Water, and on Salisbury Plain, and threading the Cotswolds. So to its origins.

There were several thorns in the side of the Great Western Railway, distinct from the London & South Western, which became rather a sort of sciatica. A true thorn was the Somerset & Dorset, which the dastardly Midland Railway and the unspeakable South Western had jointly snatched in the 1870s. Another was the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. In spite of its name, this was sturdily independent. It linked the Midlands with the Deep South, with the benevolent support of Paddington’s two old enemies, adding insult to injury by getting running powers over stretches of the Great Western.

The Junction Railway’s beginnings

Beginnings of the “Junction”, which so improbably extended from Andover to Andoversford, about sixty miles northward, had been modest and penurious. The Swindon, Marlborough & Andover Railway reached Marlborough in 1881, to push on to Grafton and ultimately to Red Posts Junction, a little west of Andover on the South Western main line. The Swindon & Cheltenham Extension Railway reached Cirencester from Swindon (GW station) in 1883. Desperate, with no apparent friends, the two companies amalgamated next year as the Midland & South Western Junction Railway. Through traffic, crossing over the Great Western at Rushey Platt, began in 1891.

A Receiver was in possession, but Midland, and LSWR, now had their eyes on this second potential dig at the GWR. The South Western released its promising Chief Storekeeper, (Sir) Sam Fay, to be the new General Manager of the MSWJR in 1892. Himself Southampton-born, he moved-in-fast and fairly licked the staggering outfit into shape and solvency. He got running powers, willingly granted, over the South Western from Andover Junction to Southampton Terminus. There were General Offices in an agreeable Victorian villa overlooking Swindon Town Station, run by Old Swindon. In 1899, with mission accomplished, Fay went back to Waterloo as Superintendent of the Line, before going to the toiling Great Central as General Manager in 1902.

The North Express service

For the first time, with the northern extremity completed, the “Junction” prospered. Under Fay, in 1893, came the forerunner of the North Express, with through carriages from Southampton to Sheffield, and a corresponding South Express. The company had then one express engine, a modest 4-4-0 by Dübs & Company. By 1914 at latest, starting early in the afternoon, it was quicker to ride from Waterloo to Cheltenham than from Paddington, thanks to a smart connection at Andover Junction. One recalls arriving at the latter, from London, and seeing the red North Express waiting on the left-hand curve from Andover Town, having left Southampton at 1.45 p.m. It went on to call at Swindon Town, then without a stop to Cheltenham, 35¼ miles, over the Cotswolds, in 54 minutes.

Memories of the railway

That is the train I have painted, with fond memories from long ago. My picture (reproduced overleaf) shows it, in 1915, coming down through Burderop Woods, above the head of Coate Water just south of Swindon, in the heart of Richard Jefferies’ beloved country, the scene of his best books. Almost continuously the line traversed country of singular beauty: along the eastern flanks of Salisbury Plain, through the Marlborough Downs and so by Chiseldon down to Swindon Town. Then came Cirencester, a delightful town to this day save that now its nearest railway station has to be Kemble Junction on the old Great Western line north to Gloucester.

At Cirencester, too, the company had its locomotive shops, and the handsome through station called Watermoor. (The Great Western had but a picturesque terminus, haunt of fussy rail-motors, on the other side of the town). Alas, poor Watermoor! Last time I saw it, twenty years ago, the buildings were deserted; vandalised and mucked-up by young yobos, such as occur even in such a nice borough as “Ciren”.

The locomotives

Going over the Cotswolds entailed marked contrasts, from the windswept plateau by Foss Cross to the delightful passage of Chedworth, and north of its double-tracked tunnel, a lovely descent to join the reluctant Great Western line out to Kingham. So, by running powers, the trains reached the welcoming arms of the Midland Railway.

Midland & South Western Junction trains scarcely resembled those of its mighty allies, save in the beautiful Midland crimson for engines and carriages. The engine tenders proudly bore the company’s initials in gold, in an extremely florid sort of italic script, all curved and scrolled, possibly inspired by that on the splashers of London & South Western express engines during the Adams period. The locomotive in my picture was one of a pair built in 1914, the last of James Tyrrell’s standard express engines. These two, Nos. 4 and 31, had each two domes, that on the second ring containing top-feed apparatus and being surmounted by a pair of Ross safety-valves, highly polished. This made the engines very exotic in the eyes of the small boy who once was I. There was virtue in the carriages, too. All too rarely for that time, all had electric lighting, even the horseboxes. The lavatory tanks were partly raised above the camber of the roofs, rather suggesting Holland.

The end of the railway

All gone, and for a long time now! Under the Railways Act 1921 this railway passed into the Great Western net, though not without a rearguard action lasting into the summer of 1923, the Government’s deadline. The Great Western gradually ran it down to rather a shadow of its old self. Yet it still served faithfully through the second world war, as well as the first conflict. But, as in other parts of the West Country, Paddington was to win in the end.

C. Hamilton-Ellis

From the Archives: 1982 – The Day the Queen Mother Named Class 47 No. 47 541 at Aberdeen

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

On Wednesday, October 20, 1982, the Queen Mother named diesel-electric locomotive No. 47 541 The Queen Mother at Aberdeen Station. In this article from the January 1983 issue of The Railway Magazine, Patrick Kingston recounts the ceremony, the preparations and an unexpected visit to the locomotive’s cab.

A historic occasion at Aberdeen

A COLOURFUL event in railway history took place at a ceremony at Aberdeen Station during the late afternoon of Wednesday, October 20, 1982, when a class “47/4” diesel-electric locomotive became the first British railway engine to be named by a member of the Royal Family. The ceremony was performed by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, following postponement, because of a national railway strike, when the naming was to have taken place at Glasgow Central on June 30.

Above: HM Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother stands beside No. 47 541, “The Queen Mother”, at Aberdeen Station on October 20, just after she had named the locomotive and unveiled nameplate and heraldic plaque.

Preparing the station

The setting for the ceremony was the north end of Platform 6 at what was called Aberdeen Joint Station which, in pre-grouping days before 1923, was shared by the Caledonian and Great North of Scotland companies. To help brighten up what is normally a drab part of the station, the immediate area of the ceremony had been cleaned with the decorative columns and canopy being freshly painted. An area of the platform was covered with red carpet and to complete the picture a floral arrangement was provided by Aberdeen City Council. Aberdeen is now an “open” station without ticket barriers so passengers and public alike were able to witness the occasion from behind some specially arranged crowd barriers.

The arrival of No. 47 541

Meanwhile the locomotive that was to be the focus of attention arrived from nearby Ferryhill shed—it was No. 47 541, the number being duly scribbled down in the notebooks of many press reporters! For a locomotive built in 1963, the 19 years of many miles of travel were forgotten, 47 541 looking as though it had only just been completed—such was the immaculate appearance of an engine that has already borne two numbers, first D1755 and then 47 161. An air of festivity was created by the arrival of the orchestra of the Aberdeen Strathspey & Reel Society who played selections of Scottish music to entertain the guests and those who had gathered to watch. On the adjacent Platform 7 there was some concern when the 15.50 to Inverness was delayed about ten minutes to maintain a connection for passengers arriving on a late-running train from the south. Sadly, for passengers on the Inverness-bound train, the connecting train arrived as the final preparations were made and with a wave of the guard’s green flag their train disappeared from view beneath the modern concrete development that now spans the station’s northern approaches.

The Queen Mother arrives

Moments later the Royal limousine arrived through an archway onto an area of the station that was once a bay platform, now filled-in as a result of the rationalisation of the ex-Great North of Scotland lines northwards from Aberdeen. Smiling and wearing a coat in a delicate shade of her favourite colour—blue—the Queen Mother stepped from her car to meet local dignitaries and railway officials including Harold Taylor, the Works Manager at Swindon where the nameplate that was to be unveiled had been made. On a dais, in front of the locomotive, a speech of welcome was given by Ken Peters, a member of British Rail Scotland’s Regional Board.

Naming The Queen Mother

In reply the Queen Mother said it was a great honour to know that a locomotive would bear her name and she was also happy that the ceremony was taking place at Aberdeen—a station so frequently used by members of her family. Concluding her speech she said: “May God bless all those who work in her and travel behind this great locomotive. I now have great pleasure in naming 47 541 The Queen Mother.” To much applause the Royal grandmother stood proudly beside “her” locomotive. Then followed a presentation of an “O”-gauge working model of the locomotive she had just named which she said that she was sure the younger members of the Royal Family “will love”.

An unexpected cab visit

The Queen Mother then met Ferryhill driver James Stephen and his wife. This was followed by an impromptu visit to the cab, into which the Queen Mother climbed from the platform by using a wooden box as a makeshift step. Sitting in his seat, Driver Stephen explained the controls, then the Queen Mother asked to see the engine room. Driver Stephen opened the engine-room door, but tactfully explained that it would be impractical for her to enter.

The ceremony concludes

After leaving the cab the Queen Mother stood to admire, once more, 47 541, “her” locomotive, and then thanked all who had contributed to such a happy occasion before being driven back to resume her holiday at Birkhall on Deeside. In conclusion my sincere thanks to R. Gemmell, BR Director of Public Affairs (Scotland) and R. R. Marshall and his staff of the Scottish Information Office for the special facilities granted to me.

Knowing how to roast vegetables well is a power move. It’s a single technique that can be deployed across an impressive range of vegetables turning them into platters of food that is simple, unfussy, and just plain good.

How To Roast Vegetables – The Basics

You can use this basic technique for a wide range of vegetables – potatoes, beets, broccoli, cauliflower, string beans, cabbage, artichoke hearts, asparagus, etc. Seriously, don’t overthink it. Once you get your roasting technique nailed down, you can fancify things with added spices, under-dressings, and the like. But to start, here’s what to know:

  • High heat is your friend. Somewhere in the range of 400°F – it will give you tender interiors and nice, golden exteriors. Go too hot and you’ll burn the edges before the insides have time to cook. Some recipes might go a bit higher, others a bit lower, but if I’m just doing a basic vegetable roast, I typically set the dial right around 400°F.
  • Don’t crowd the pan. Your vegetables are going to put off a good amount of moisture as they roast, and they like some space to crisp instead of steam.
  • Cut vegetables to a similar size. This way they will cook in the same amount of time.
  • Group vegetables by cooking time. Like with like  on the pan. You want to arrange your ingredients in zones based on estimated cooking time. For example, put asparagus which doesn’t take long to roast, on a small pan (or at the edge of a larger pan). Place ingredients that take longer to cook on another pan or in another zone, for example-potatoes or winter squash, That way you can remove them as they finish cooking.
  • Don’t skimp on the oil. You don’t have to go wild, but make sure everything gets a thin, even coat of olive oil before roasting.

Use this play-by-play: Heat oven to about 400°F. Toss each type of vegetable in a large bowl with a splash of olive oil and a good pinch of salt I often add a couple scoops of nutritional yeast in at this point but that’s entirely optional. Roast, tossing a couple times after things start browning, until vegetables are tender and have a good amount of golden color.

I know a lot of people make vegetable pot pies with store-bought puff pastry, but over the years I’ve come to believe a homemade pie crust is, by far, the superior way to go. It makes the whole pot pie endeavor worth it. These pies are rustic and creamy, often lovingly constructed. A great pie crust lends butter-kissed backbone and structure. A flaky, decadent counterpoint to the classic, creamy, vegetable filling. Let’s make a pie!

Vegetable Pot Pie: Real Talk

My main piece of advice here is this. If you’re are aiming to make a vegetable pot pie from scratch, plan ahead. Going from start to finish in one go can be a bit of an undertaking – pie dough, filling, assembly, bake, etc. But! If you keep pie crusts at the ready in your freezer, and make the filling a day or two in advance, pulling this pie together is an absolute breeze.

Pro Tips:

There are a few things I do as I’m making pot pies to level them up. Little details to help delineate my pie from your average vegetarian pot pie.

  • Homemade pie crust: Per the opening paragraph, I’ll go to the mat encouraging you to make your own pie dough. Make a few rounds of dough and keep it in the freezer for months. The flavor! The flakiness! Once you nail down the pie dough making process it’s hard to go back to a store-bought crust. Can you use a store-bought crust for this recipe? Yes, absolutely. Will it be better with a homemade pie crust? Yes, absolutely.

  • Caramelize your onions: Really go for it. Most pot pie fillings have you sauté onions until tender in the beginning. I have you go well beyond that. By browning the onions and celery you’re developing more depth and flavor as the base of your filling.
  • Lemon zest: Sprinkle your pie plate with lemon zest before lining with the pie crust. Add some to your filling as well. The zest combines with the butter from the crust to perfume the whole situation. The brightness of the zest really brings something to the filling as well.

Vegetable Pot Pies: Common Mistakes

There are a number of common pitfalls to avoid when making a pot pie.

  • Cool your filling completely: When you go to assemble your pie, it’s important that your filling is completely cold. Adding a warm filling is going to melt the butter in your crust prematurely and can contribute to a soggy pie bottom in the end. You want a golden, structured bottom crust. The solution? If you’re in a rush, spread the hot pie filling across a large plate, and place in the refrigerator, stirring now and then until cool.
  • Place pie on baking sheet: This is one mistake I still make too often, unfortunately. Placing your pie on a baking sheet while baking will protect your oven from any spills, overflows, or melted butter. It makes clean up much easier and you won’t have things burning onto the bottom of your oven.
  • Keep an eagle eye on your crust: I love a deeply golden, rustic pie crust. But the edges of a pie can get dark while the center is still underbaked. A problem! Have tinfoil strips or a pie shield ready to protect areas of your pie until the rest of the pie catches up. If you want to up your pie game over time, a basic pie shield is a great, inexpensive purchase!


Pot Pie Leftovers:

Refrigerate leftovers, covered, for up to 5 days. Reheat in a 350°F / 175°C oven, covered in foil, until filling is completely hot.

Can I Make Pot Pies Ahead of Time?

If you want to make a pot pie ahead of time, make the filling in advance and refrigerate or freeze until ready to assemble the pie. Thawing first, of course. Make the pie dough ahead of time as well. You’re going to have more success baking the day you want to serve the pie.

Vegetable Pot Pie Variations

  • Make it spicy: Add a couple teaspoons of your favorite curry powder (or to taste) to the filling. You might also add some to the pie crust as well.
  • Explore different vegetables: Use what you have and love! Or explore what is seasonal. Great vegetable options include: sweet potatoes, broccoli, cauliflower, shredded cabbage, pumpkin, and corn. Roasted cherry tomatoes add an amazing burst of flavor.
  • Coconut milk: Use coconut milk (full fat) in place of the heavy cream for a different flavor profile.
  • Pesto: Skip the other herbs in the recipe and stir a couple tablespoons of pesto into your filling at the end. Or dollop it on top of the filling as you’re assembling your pot pie, before putting the top crust on.
