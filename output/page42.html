This week’s plant was an easy choice after reading two articles by Erika Alejandra Chaves-Díaz. One explores a medical innovation inspired by the way Drosera capensis captures its prey, while the other reveals how some insects manage to escape the sticky traps of Drosera species. After reading both, I couldn’t resist learning a little more about these fascinating carnivorous plants.

Drosera capensis, commonly known as the Cape sundew, is a carnivorous plant native to South Africa’s Cape region, where it grows in moist habitats such as marshes, streamsides and boggy areas. Its scientific history goes back to 1753, when Carl Linnaeus described it in Species Plantarum, the book that established the modern system of naming plants using two-part scientific names. Since then, this species has continued to attract scientific attention. It was the first sundew to have its genome sequenced and is known to produce several compounds with potential medicinal applications.

Drosera capensis in its natural habitat. Photo by Tony Rebelo (iNaturalist, CC BY-SA 4.0).

But what makes these plants so fascinating? Like other sundews, Drosera capensis has long, narrow leaves covered in tiny glandular hairs that produce glistening droplets of a sticky substance. When an unsuspecting insect comes into contact with these droplets, it can become trapped, triggering the leaf to slowly curl around its prey before releasing digestive enzymes to break it down.

Interestingly, this trapping mechanism fascinated Charles Darwin, who described it in his 1875 book Insectivorous Plants. Darwin observed that while contact with solid objects could cause the sticky tentacles of sundews to bend, falling water droplets did not trigger the same response. More than a century later, researchers studying Drosera capensis discovered that mechanical stimulation and chemical signals from prey activate electrical and hormonal responses that help the plant capture and digest insects. Water droplets, however, fail to activate these responses fully, preventing the plant from wasting energy reacting to every passing shower.

Time-lapse of Drosera capensis eating a mosquito. Total time elapsed is ~1 hour.

Interestingly, the same mechanism that makes Drosera capensis such an effective predator has inspired scientists to develop new medical technologies. As Erika explained in her article earlier this week, researchers have developed a patch with tiny structures that curl when exposed to body temperature, mimicking the movement of sundew leaves to help close wounds. Although this technology is still experimental, it is a remarkable example of how studying plants can inspire solutions to problems that seem completely unrelated to botany.

However, despite their sophisticated trapping mechanisms, sundews are not always successful hunters. Some insects have surprising ways of escaping their sticky leaves, challenging our idea of these plants as inescapable predators. Next week, Erika will introduce us to some of these remarkable escape artists and the strategies they use to avoid becoming a sundew's next meal. Hopefully, by then, you'll be just as fascinated by these little plants as I am!

Most fleet managers judge an electric van or pickup on range, charger cost and fuel savings. Insurance usually gets attention when the renewal quote lands, and by then the vehicles are ordered and any surprise in the premium or the policy wording is harder to fix.

An EV does not need a different kind of policy. It goes on the same commercial auto policy as the diesel van it replaces. What shifts is the value of the vehicle, how repairs are handled and a few coverage edges that never came up with gas engines. Carriers also differ in how they underwrite electric vehicles, so before you commit at renewal, have an independent fleet insurance broker quote the same vehicle list with several of them and show you where the terms differ.

What Underwriters Will Ask

Expect questions about where each vehicle is parked and charged overnight, who drives it, how far it travels in a day and what it carries. Those answers shape the policy as much as the drivetrain does. A vehicle charging in an unattended yard with a fire exposure nearby may be rated differently from one parked in a secured, monitored building, so describe the setup accurately and early. Gaps between the application and reality are what turn a claim into an argument.

Have the vehicle list ready with year, make, model, purchase price and VIN plus the driver roster and a short description of the routes. Brokers and underwriters move faster when the file is complete, and it leaves less room for assumptions that surface later.

Higher Values and Costlier Repairs

Physical damage coverage pays for your own vehicles, so a pricier truck puts more money at risk. Start with how the policy values a loss. Actual cash value pays what the vehicle was worth just before the loss, after depreciation, and the deductible comes off that. A stated amount policy names a figure up front, but the payout is still limited to the least of that figure, the actual cash value or the repair cost, so it is not a guaranteed payout. Used EV values have moved quickly in recent years, which makes the difference matter.

Repairs are the other piece. Mitchell International, which supplies estimating software to insurers and body shops, reported that repairable EVs averaged about $5,753 per claim in the second quarter of 2024, against $4,806 for comparable gas vehicles, roughly 20% more. Total-loss rates were close to even. That is one dataset from one period, and work vehicles may not follow the same pattern, but it is a reason to expect repair estimates to run higher.

Downtime deserves its own question. If a van waits weeks on a part, a route goes uncovered. Ask whether the policy includes rental reimbursement or loss of use, and whether the carrier’s repair network includes shops equipped for high-voltage battery work.

The Charger and the Battery

A commercial auto policy covers the vehicle, not the charger on the wall. Consumer guidance from State Farm describes the same split for personal policies: a portable charging cord is generally covered under comprehensive if it is stolen or damaged in a covered loss, while a permanently installed charger is less clear and may fall under a property policy or need an endorsement. Commercial policies vary, so confirm where each piece of charging equipment sits before it is installed, particularly if you lease the building.

Battery damage from a crash or fire is a physical damage claim like any other. Gradual capacity loss and manufacturing defects usually are not. Those fall to the manufacturer’s warranty.

Liability and Vehicles You Do Not Own

Liability exposure follows vehicles, drivers and miles, not fuel type. If the electric vehicles are additions rather than replacements, limits set a few years ago may no longer fit. An umbrella policy can add a layer above the auto limits and is worth pricing at the same time.

Transitions also create gaps. Fleets often rent EVs to test a route, or let employees use their own cars while new vehicles are on order. Hired and non-owned auto coverage addresses your liability in those cases, but damage to the rented or personal vehicle itself generally needs separate physical damage coverage for hired autos. Settle that before the trial starts, not after a dented rental.

Put Your Fleet Data to Work

The telematics that handle routing and charging also record braking, speed and idle time. Some insurers use that kind of data in pricing programs, and documented driver reviews, training and maintenance records give a broker something concrete to take to underwriters. Keep them current and in one place.

None of this is exotic. Value, repair, equipment and rental questions are ordinary commercial auto topics, and the EV just makes them harder to ignore. This article is general information, not legal advice or a coverage recommendation, and coverage varies by carrier, policy and state.

Brown Butter Financiers

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Of all the pastries in France, one of my favorites is the financier. Enriched with nuts, and moistened with butter, every bakery in France offers their own version of them. They come in different sizes, shapes, and even flavors; almond is the most popular, but you’ll sometimes come across financiers made with ground hazelnuts or pistachios. I like them all. There are a few theories how this mini-gâteau got its curious name.

One is that, traditionally, they’re baked in small, rectangular molds. Once baked and unmolded, the little cakes resemble bars of gold, which appeals to people in the financial world. Another is that people in France are known to indulge in an afternoon sweet stop at their local bakery, called a goûter. Because people who work in the financial industry normally wear nice outfits or suits, something that’s neat to eat is appreciated, so they can stay presentable when heading back to the office.

I don’t have to worry about that, nor has anything come out of my oven ever turned to gold. (Quelle dommage!) But when I found myself with some leftover brown butter from infusing it in bourbon for Brown Butter Old Fashioneds, since I treat butter like gold, I repurposed it as a base for a batch of financiers.

Using browned butter keeps the butter flavor even more in focus. Some people get a little anxious when they see dark specks in pastries and desserts; I once had a waiter ask me what the dark flecks in the vanilla ice cream were. When I told him they were vanilla beans, and asked him (incredulously) what he thought they were, he replied, “I thought they were dirt.”

So after you brown the butter, when pouring it out of the pan, you don’t want to scrape up too much of the dark bits at the bottom of the pan, but I don’t mind a few in my cakes (in fact, I prefer them). So no need to fret about having a few in your batter. These cakes are also very forgiving, which is why so many bakeries in France offer them. They’re easy to make, keep well, and are the perfect afternoon snack – or as the French say, un snack.

Browned Butter Financiers

For financiers, I use mini-muffin tins, which are easily available. I’m not a fan of silicone bakeware but know that some people like it. This batter is pretty forgiving so can be baked in madeleine molds or even in larger muffin tins, filling them only about halfway. If you use another size mold, you’ll likely need to adjust the baking time; bake them until browned on top, and the feel just set in the center when you touch them. As mentioned, I made these with leftover brown butter from the Brown Butter Old Fashioned recipe. I started with 4 ounces (8 tablespoons/115g) of butter, which yielded the amount called for in the recipe. If starting from scratch, and making your own brown butter, start with that amount of brown butter, then you can measure it out when it’s browned and cooled. (If you need a bit more butter, you can simply add a bit of melted butter to it, to reach the 2 1/2 oz/75g amount. There are links at the end of the post with detailed instructions on making brown butter. Course Dessert Cuisine French Keyword almonds, financiers, french pastry Servings 24 financiers

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (140g) almond or hazelnut flour
  • 3/4 cup plus 2 tablespoons (180g) sugar
  • 5 tablespoons (45g) flour
  • generous pinch salt
  • 4 large egg whites at room temperature
  • 1/2 teaspoon pure vanilla or almond extract
  • 2 1/2 ounces (75g) brown butter slightly warm (liquified)

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 375ºF/180ºC and butter the insides of 24 mini muffin tins generously with softened, not melted, butter, making sure to butter the upper rims of the indentations.
  • In a medium bowl, mix the almond or hazelnut powder, sugar, flour and salt. Stir in the egg whites and vanilla or almond extract, then the browned butter.
  • Fill each indentation of the mini muffin tins almost to the top. Rap the tins sharply on the counter to level the tops, then bake for 13 minutes, until nicely browned. Let the financiers cool in the tins, then remove them, using a sharp knife to help release them, if necessary.

Notes

Storage: The financiers can be stored in an air-tight container for up to one week. They can be frozen for up to two months.

A quiet electric motor changes the sound inside a car, but it does not remove everything competing with the stereo. The cabin remains a listening space shaped by the journey as well as the audio system. For podcasts, the useful question is how clearly a conversation comes through, rather than how impressive the equipment looks on a specification sheet.

Spoken-word programs develop through questions, answers and explanations. Their meaning depends on the words being understood, including the connections between one point and the next. The vehicle’s background sound and the system’s reproduction of the recording both belong in that experience. Quietness describes one part of the surroundings; it does not describe everything between a recorded conversation and the listener.

As an example, the Kim Hughes podcast brings bomb-disposal expert Hughes into conversation with presenter Selema Masekela about his work and preparation. It offers an account of an unfamiliar profession through the person who practiced it. Following that account means following the exchanges that develop it, rather than simply registering that a voice is playing. Spoken-word listening brings this kind of conversation into a cabin that already has its own sounds. Enjoying this kind of podcast–where the subject matter might be complex–to the full means creating a setup that allows everything to be heard clearly, maximizing the amount of information you can get from listening.

The Cabin Shapes the Sound from the Speakers

In an August 2025 technical article on automotive audio measurements, Steve Temme reports tests in an unnamed EV parked in a quiet underground garage. He explains that reflections and microphone position affect measured sound. His tests examine frequency response: how strongly a system reproduces different pitches, which shapes the balance of its sound.

Quiet surroundings and faithful reproduction describe different properties. A cabin can have little background noise while the audio system gives different emphasis to different parts of a recording. The recording supplies the conversation, but the reproduced sound is the result of playback through the installed system within the cabin. Electric propulsion does not specify that system’s tonal balance.

Temme’s measurements also differed between seats in the tested car. A vehicle’s occupants therefore need not receive identical reproduced sound, even though they share the same recording and speaker system. This is another reason that an equipment list cannot fully describe listening. Speaker count identifies how many speakers are installed; it leaves the sound reaching each position unmeasured.

Sound from the speakers reaches occupants within the car’s interior, where it can reflect from surrounding surfaces. The listening position is part of that arrangement. A speaker system is therefore heard in a particular space, from a particular seat, rather than independently of the car around it. This gives cabin design and audio reproduction a direct relationship, while keeping sound produced by movement separate from the way the recording is reproduced through the installed equipment.

Quiet at Rest Is Different from Quiet on the Road

Car and Driver’s July 2026 test of the 2027 Rivian R2 supplies a documented example of the moving cabin. Its Performance AWD Launch Edition, tested with 21-inch wheels, registered 31 dBA at idle and 67 dBA at 70 mph. The reviewers described wind rush and suspension thumps on the highway.

Battery-electric cars have no combustion engine, but wind and road noise remain part of the journey. Describing quietness at rest leaves a separate question about sound while traveling. For spoken-word entertainment, that distinction concerns the surroundings in which the conversation is reproduced. The stereo and the moving car both contribute to the sound reaching the occupants.

The road-test figures measure overall interior sound under the stated conditions. They do not report how clearly individual words were understood or isolate each noise source’s contribution. Likewise, the parked audio measurements describe the system tested in the garage. These are separate vehicles and separate assessments, each explaining a particular part of the listening experience.

The Audio System Can Add Sounds of its Own

In Temme’s unnamed, parked test vehicle, a door panel rattled audibly at a higher playback level. He explains that speaker-driven vibration can affect panels, wiring and fasteners, adding unwanted sound to the audio being reproduced.

Speakers are fitted to the vehicle, so their performance also involves the components around them. A panel that responds audibly to playback introduces a sound beyond the recorded material. This concerns the car’s physical installation, rather than the podcast’s subject or the absence of a combustion engine. It brings the interior into the explanation in a different way from the wind and suspension sounds that accompany movement.

Loudness and Reproduction Quality

The technical article distinguishes maximum output from useful output, judged alongside distortion. Distortion describes changes introduced during reproduction. Assessing it therefore concerns the sound being produced, alongside the level at which the system plays it. That separates how loud a system can play from how well it reproduces the material at that level. A maximum-volume figure describes output; it does not, by itself, describe the quality of a conversation coming through the speakers.

An interview’s questions and answers carry its account of the subject. The car’s sound system reproduces those recorded exchanges within the cabin’s background sound. Equipment specifications identify what is installed, noise readings describe the surroundings, and a listening assessment addresses the result. Each supplies information that the others leave open.

A quiet EV provides a useful starting point for listening, while clear podcast audio depends on what reaches the occupants during playback. The motor, moving cabin and installed sound system have distinct roles. Understanding those roles gives a fuller account of in-car listening than a quietness claim or a speaker count alone.

Trente ans après, cette loi a permis de sélectionner 1 000 objets et de sauver ainsi de nombreux trésors patrimoniaux.

Dans les années 1960, les automotrices électriques de classe 303 ont remplacé les trains à vapeur à Glasgow. Elles sont rapidement devenues un élément familier du quotidien des usagers.
