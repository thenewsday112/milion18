Hannah Fry: I lost my dad to Alzheimer’s. New research into its origins is amazing

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Fran Pulido

I was sitting in a conference in August, drifting as I so often do between listening to what was on stage and pretending to be doing important work on my laptop (by which I mean sending memes on WhatsApp).

All of a sudden, from the stage, the renowned biologist Aviv Regev came out with a sentence that made every single person in the room sit up and pay attention: “Of course,” she said, “we now know that Alzheimer’s starts in the nose.”

I’m sorry, what? Starts in the nose? I didn’t know Alzheimer’s starts in the nose.

My own lovely dad died from Alzheimer’s last year, and ever since his diagnosis, I’ve done an incredible amount of digging into the disease, trying to understand what was happening to him. I was astonished I’d never come across this idea.

And perhaps you haven’t either. Which is why I want to share my astonishment at discovering that Aviv Regev was right – not only is Alzheimer’s closely associated with our sense of smell, but there is a growing body of research suggesting that our noses might have something to do with why the disease develops in the first place.

The first bit you might already know: people with Alzheimer’s tend to lose their sense of smell, sometimes years before any symptoms of memory loss start to show up. But it turns out that our ability to smell is a surprisingly good indicator of our overall health (looking at you, covid-19). One study from 2014 found that smell loss in older people was a better predictor of whether they would be dead within five years than a cancer diagnosis.

Could pollution find its way to the brain via smell receptors? STEVE GSCHMEISSNER/Science Photo Library/Alamy

It’s not that losing your sense of smell itself will kill you. More that the olfactory nerves are deeply intertwined with some of the most important memory structures in the brain, so when they start to deteriorate, it can be an early warning that something much more serious is going on.

And this connection seems to work in both directions. One intriguing study (admittedly with quite a small sample size) put a diffuser with essential oils by older people’s beds, thus exposing them to all kinds of wonderful scents while they slept. After six months, the people who had the olfactory enrichment performed better on memory tests than those in a control group.

All very interesting. But not what Regev had been suggesting. Her words seemed to imply that noses are actually responsible for the trouble in the first place.

It turns out that the reason scientists are even entertaining this possibility is because of how fiercely our brains are protected from the outside world. Our skulls are like an actual fortress: even our blood isn’t allowed in without passing a particularly fussy bouncer called the blood-brain barrier.

One of the only real exceptions is a little bit of bone right at the top of your nose that is riddled with holes. It’s called the cribriform, and it’s where the olfactory fibres dangle down into your nose to give you your sense of smell – a bit like if you pushed some Play-Doh through a sieve.

And that means that, despite all the elaborate defences protecting your brain, there is a potential route in, sitting right in the middle of your face.

So, here’s the idea. Maybe the beta-amyloid plaques, the sticky little protein fragments that appear to play such a key role in the cascade of Alzheimer’s in the brain, are what’s left over after the brain has fought an infection. And maybe the nose is how the infection arrives.

To see if this held any water, in 2022, a group of scientists squirted some chlamydia up the noses of some mice.

(I should add, for the sake of the mice’s reputation, that it was actually Chlamydia pneumoniae, a cousin of the bacterium that causes the STI. But I hope you’ll forgive me the less scientifically accurate version. In a long career, you get very few chances to write sentences that good, and I wasn’t about to waste mine.)

The remarkable thing, though, was that within 72 hours, the bacteria had travelled up the nerves in the nasal cavity and got through to the brain. What’s more, the infection triggered the accumulation of beta-amyloid. Now that’s not definitive proof of how Alzheimer’s starts in humans. But there is some other, rather unsettling evidence that infections aren’t the only unwelcome visitors that manage to get in that way.

A group in Mexico studied stray dogs that spent their lives breathing in polluted air. Inside the dogs’ brains, they found microscopic particles of metals – that seem to have entered through the nose – wrapped in pockets of inflammation and that same beta-amyloid protein. You don’t see the same thing in dogs that lived in cleaner, more rural environments.

Now these studies have been extended to look at the brains of very small children and young adults who died from gun violence or car accidents – and they find something similar. Tiny, metal–rich particles, thought to originate from exhausts and brake dust, turned up in their brain tissue, alongside inflammation and accumulations of beta-amyloid. The very sorts of changes associated with Alzheimer’s, appearing in brains that were sometimes only a few years old. Put together, it implies that the disease might have an environmental cause, one that begins much much earlier than we thought.

So, where does Alzheimer’s start? The answer is no one nose (pun very much intended). There is still a great deal we don’t understand, but the implications from all this research are rather extraordinary. What if the beta-amyloid plaques we’ve spent so long trying to get rid of are, at least sometimes, the aftermath of the brain trying to protect itself? And what if, rather than focusing entirely on clearing up the damage, we could work out what set it off in the first place?

But more than anything, I can’t help but laugh as I think about all this in the context of my dad, who had many wonderful qualities, but a discreet nose wasn’t one of them. We used to say that his nose would enter a room 15 minutes before he did. He would have found it very funny indeed if it turned out to have been the cause of his downfall.

It’s officially soup season, and I’m SO ready for it! This easy Ham and Bean Soup is loaded with tender canned white beans, colorful veggies, and savory chunks of browned ham, all in a thick, creamy broth (made without any actual cream or dairy). It’s a great way to use up leftover holiday ham, but I certainly don’t wait for a holiday to make it! I use just 8 basic ingredients plus a few seasonings to make this hearty, easy, budget-friendly soup, and it’s ready in less than an hour!

“This is excellent. I added leftover chopped up asparagus and four cups of the chicken broth. If ham is not an option smoked kielbasa is great too. I topped the soup with mozzarella an smoked parmigiana cheese.”

Julie W

How I Build Big Flavor in Less Time

Traditional ham and bean soups often start with dried beans and a ham bone that simmer together for hours. And there’s definitely a time and place for that! But I wanted an easy version that didn’t require soaking beans or spending all afternoon waiting for dinner. So I use canned cannellini beans and fully cooked ham to get a head start. They’re already cooked, which means I can spend my time building flavor instead.

One thing I do differently is brown the ham before adding anything else. This gives the edges a little color and leaves behind tasty browned bits on the bottom of the pot (called fond). When I add the onion, carrots, celery, and garlic, their natural moisture loosens those bits and brings that extra flavor into the soup. Sautéing the vegetables also softens them and brings out a little natural sweetness. For the broth, I borrowed a trick from my white bean soup and purée one can of beans with its liquid. This thickens the soup and adds a lovely creaminess without flour, cornstarch, or heavy cream. I leave the other two cans whole for a chunky texture, and after the initial flavor-building stages, everything comes together with just 15 minutes of simmering!

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Give the ham a chance to brown. I cook the diced ham in oil over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, until the edges are lightly browned and golden-brown bits form on the bottom of the pot. This builds the salty ham flavor you’d normally get from simmering a ham bone for hours, just in much less time! If those bits start turning very dark, lower the heat so they don’t burn.
  2. Blend one can of beans, liquid and all! Purée ONE can of cannellini beans with its canning liquid, then drain the other two. The smooth beans thicken the broth, and the whole beans give the soup its chunky texture. No blender? You can thoroughly mash that first can with a potato masher or fork instead. It won’t be as smooth, but this is a chunky soup anyway!
  3. Let the soup boil and thicken. The 15-minute boil allows some of the broth to evaporate while the puréed beans thicken the soup. I give the pot a good stir every few minutes, making sure to scrape along the bottom, to keep the beans from sticking. If it gets thicker than you’d like, stir in a little more broth or water until you reach your preferred consistency.
  4. Taste before adding salt. Cooked ham is pretty salty, especially when it’s cured, and chicken broth adds even more seasoning. That’s why I wait until the ham goes back into the pot before tasting and adjusting. I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I finished each bowl with a little extra black pepper.

What Kind of Ham Can I Use?

For this quick ham and bean soup, I recommend fully cooked ham (or leftover cooked ham from another meal) rather than a ham hock. We’re using canned beans and other ingredients that cook quickly, so there’s no need to spend hours simmering a ham bone to flavor the broth! Thicker pieces work best because they hold their shape and develop nicely browned edges when sautéed.

Boneless vs. Bone-In Ham Steaks

Both of these ham steaks are fully cooked and work well in this recipe:

  • Boneless ham steak: My easiest choice! There’s no bone to work around, and the thick slices are easy to cut into bite-sized pieces. You can buy ham steaks in smaller packs (shown above) or use a package of fully cooked, boneless, sliced ham (as I did in the recipe below).
  • Bone-in ham steak: This works just as well once you’ve removed the bone and any tough rind. Just remember that you need 1 lb. of usable ham, so you’ll need to buy a little extra to account for the weight of the bone.

What About Other Types of Ham?

I saw a LOT of ham options at the store when shopping for this recipe, and you probably will too, especially around the holidays. The fully cooked, boneless spiral-cut ham and smoked shank portion ham shown above can both work, BUT I’d save those bigger hams for a holiday centerpiece (like our baked ham) and use the leftovers for this soup.

I wouldn’t splurge on a big ham for this recipe unless you plan to use it for several meals. Ham slices are cheaper, easier, and all you need for this quickie soup.

Ham hocks are a different story! They’re better suited to traditional bean soups that simmer for hours. The long simmer gives the connective tissue time to soften and the smoky flavor time to develop. The pork hocks pictured above are only partially cooked, so they aren’t suitable for this shortcut recipe as written. If you’d prefer to make soup using a ham hock and dried beans, check out our navy bean soup recipe instead. It uses a smoked ham hock and takes over two hours of cooking, plus soaking time for the beans.

Ham and Bean Soup

This incredibly easy and deliciously chunky Ham and Bean Soup features a medley of colorful vegetables, browned ham, and plenty of hearty white beans. Instructions

  • Gather and prepare all ingredients. Dice the onion, peel and slice the carrots, slice the celery, and mince the garlic.
  • Dice the ham into bite-sized chunks. Add the ham and cooking oil to a large soup pot. Sauté the ham for 3-5 minutes over medium heat, or until it achieves a decent amount of browning. Remove the browned ham to a clean bowl.
  • Add the onion, carrots, celery, and garlic to the pot in place of the ham. Sauté the vegetables for about 5 minutes over medium heat, or until the onions have softened. Allow the moisture released from the vegetables to help dissolve the browned bits of ham from the bottom of the soup pot as you stir.
  • While the vegetables are sautéing, add one of the three cans of beans to a blender, with the liquid from the can, and purée until smooth. Drain the remaining two cans of beans.
  • Add all three cans of beans (one puréed and two drained) to the soup pot with the vegetables. Also add ¼ tsp dried thyme, ¼ tsp pepper, and stir to combine.
  • Next, stir in 2 cups chicken broth. Then turn the heat up to medium-high and allow the soup to come to a boil.
  • Once it reaches a boil, turn the heat down to medium and allow the soup to continue to boil for 15 minutes, stirring occasionally. As the soup boils, it will reduce and thicken. If the soup becomes too thick, add more chicken broth or water to achieve your desired soup consistency.
  • After the soup has boiled for 15 minutes and has thickened, stir the cooked ham back into the soup. Give the soup a taste and adjust the salt or pepper to your liking. I did not add any additional salt, but I did top each bowl with a little fresh pepper. Serve hot!

How to Make Ham and Bean Soup Step-by-Step Photos

Gather all of your ingredients: This is the type of ham I used (honey cured boneless ham). You can use any cooked ham, but a thicker slice works better than thin sandwich slices. You can also use cooked holiday ham. You’ll need 1 lb. of cooked ham.

Prep the veggies: Dice 1 yellow onion, peel and slice 3 carrots, slice 3 celery ribs, and mince 2 cloves of garlic. I recommend keeping the vegetables fairly uniform in size so they soften evenly during the short cooking time.

Brown the ham: Start by dicing 1 lb. of fully cooked ham into bite-sized pieces.

Add the ham and 1 Tbsp cooking oil to a large soup pot or Dutch oven. Sauté over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the edges turn golden brown. You should also see some browned bits developing on the bottom of the pot. Keep an eye on the heat so they don’t burn. Remove the ham to a clean bowl and leave those browned bits behind.

Sauté the vegetables: Add the prepared onion, carrots, celery, and garlic to the same pot. Sauté over medium heat for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onions look translucent and the vegetables begin to soften. They’ll release some moisture as they cook, so use your spoon to gently scrape up the browned bits left by the ham.

Purée one can of beans: As the vegetables cook, add one 15.5 oz. can of cannellini beans to a blender, including all the liquid from the can. Blend until smooth and creamy. This mixture will help thicken the soup without adding cream!

Drain the other two cans: Now drain the remaining two cans of beans, but don’t purée them. We want those beans to stay whole for texture.

This is what the veggies should look like after sautéing for about 5 minutes. The bottom of the pot should also look noticeably cleaner as those bits dissolve into the vegetables.

Add the beans and seasonings: Add the puréed beans and both cans of drained beans to the pot with the softened vegetables. Sprinkle in ¼ tsp dried thyme and ¼ tsp freshly cracked black pepper. Stir gently until the creamy bean mixture coats the vegetables and whole beans. Try not to mash the whole beans as you stir.

Pour in the broth: Add 2 cups chicken broth and stir until everything is combined. The soup will look fairly watery at this point, but that’s normal! The puréed beans will give the broth more body as the liquid reduces during cooking.

Increase the heat to medium-high and bring the soup to a boil. Reduce the heat to medium and let it continue boiling for 15 minutes, stirring every few minutes to prevent the beans from sticking to the bottom of the pot. The broth should gradually reduce and become thicker and creamier. The vegetables should also be tender when pierced with a fork. If the soup gets too thick, add a splash of chicken broth or water until it reaches your preferred consistency.

Add the ham and serve: Once the soup has thickened, stir the browned ham back into the pot to warm through.

Give everything a taste before adding any salt, as the ham and chicken broth already contribute quite a bit! I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I did add a little freshly cracked black pepper to each bowl. Serve hot and enjoy!

What to Serve with Ham and Bean Soup

This chunky ham and bean soup is a meal in a bowl! Between the ham, vegetables, and all those beans (which are super filling), you really don’t need much else to make a satisfying weeknight dinner. That said, I almost always serve some bread with my soup…it’s a must. 😋 A crusty baguette, sliced sourdough, or soft dinner rolls from the grocery store are all easy options. But my personal favorite here is a warm slice of cornbread. Its slightly sweet flavor pairs perfectly with the smoky, salty ham, and its tender, crumbly texture soaks up the creamy broth. If you want something fresh on the side, I’d go with a simple side salad. The crisp vegetables and tangy dressing offer a nice contrast to the warm, hearty soup, and it’s easy to throw together while the soup simmers.

Shown here with a side of cornbread and a simple salad.

Storage & Reheating

This ham and bean soup makes great leftovers. Let it cool quickly, then transfer it to airtight containers and refrigerate within 2 hours. It’ll keep in the refrigerator for 3-4 days. The soup will naturally thicken as it sits because the beans will keep absorbing moisture.

Reheating

I like to reheat this soup on the stovetop over medium heat, stirring occasionally until it comes to a boil and is heated all the way through (165°F). Add a splash of chicken broth or water as it warms to loosen the consistency. You can also reheat individual servings in the microwave, stirring partway through so they heat evenly.

Freezing

This soup freezes really well, too! Once cooled, divide it into individual portions in freezer-safe containers or bags. I prefer to divide my soup into single servings before freezing, so they can be reheated as needed and in the amount needed. Quart-sized freezer bags are great for freezing soup, as are the small blue-top Ziploc food storage containers. Freeze for up to 3 months for the best quality.

Rayman’s designs traditionally have a more handcrafted, organic feel, so introducing these glossy surfaces, metallic details, and high-tech materials was something quite different from what we’re used to.

We wanted to stay as faithful as possible to Astro Bot’s iconic design while making sure it still felt authentically Rayman. That meant carefully adapting the shapes and proportions, especially his large nose, no arms / legs, and his big shoes, which are such recognizable parts of Rayman’s silhouette.

The goal was to incorporate as many of Astro Bot’s signature details as possible, from the reflective metallic parts and glowing blue elements to the smooth white surfaces, without losing Rayman’s personality or expressiveness.

It was a really interesting exercise in finding the right balance between two very different visual identities and making them feel like they naturally belong together.

In your own words, what DNA do Astro Bot and Rayman share?  

Arvieu: Astro Bot has truly established itself as a major player in the platforming genre, with an identity of its own.

What I particularly appreciate is how generous the game is: the richness of its worlds, the creativity of its scenarios, and the constant joy of discovering new gameplay ideas. 

This is something we also find in Rayman. Both games offer incredibly rich fantasy worlds, filled with diverse biomes that invite players to explore, discover, and experience a sense of wonder. They also place great importance on fluid movement, a variety of interactions, and the joy of mastering the possibilities available to the player.

Beyond these similarities, I think our two franchises share, above all, the same spirit. They’re both colorful, creative games that are full of surprises and driven by positive values. They aim to evoke emotions, make people smile, and rekindle that almost instinctive sense of joy we feel when discovering a platformer bursting with ideas.

These are games that make you feel good. They remind us that video games can be a wonderful source of joy, wonder, and shared experiences, whatever our age.

Why do you think collaborations like this are important? 

Arvieu: Since the early days of video games, creators have been inspiring one another.

An idea, a gameplay mechanic, a character, or an artistic approach can spark new ideas and open up unexpected possibilities. It’s an ongoing exchange that fuels our collective creativity.

Collaborations like this make that exchange tangible. They allow us to celebrate the work of other teams, bring together worlds that are close to our hearts, and create moments players might never have imagined otherwise.

It’s a virtuous circle: everyone brings their own perspective, ideas, and expertise to the table, and we all grow from the experience. Ultimately, it’s the players who benefit, discovering new encounters and sharing in the joy of seeing their favorite characters come together.

Rayman has a long history behind him and holds a special place in the hearts of many players, particularly those who first discovered him on the original PlayStation. Astro Bot, meanwhile, celebrates this legacy while establishing its own identity with a new generation of players. Seeing these two worlds come together is therefore also a way of bringing different generations of players together.

It’s a little nod to the history of video games, but also a wonderful illustration of the medium’s ability to reinvent itself and continue to surprise us.

Jusqu’au 27 août 2027, Train World, le musée ferroviaire installé en gare de Schaerbeek, tout près de Bruxelles, propose une exposition temporaire sur le lien unissant mode et chemin de fer. Un évènement qui se déroule dans le cadre des 100 ans de la SNCB.

Arrivé dans la belle salle des guichets de l’ancienne gare de Schaerbeek, le visiteur découvre six créations contemporaines imaginées par de jeunes diplômés du College of Art and Design Brussels (CAD), une école supérieure bruxelloise de design. Ces jeunes talents ont été invités par la Société nationale des chemins de fer belges à créer les tenues des voyageurs qui empruntent ses trains. Dans le cadre du centenaire de la SNCB, célébré tout au long de l’année à travers une multitude d’évènements, Train World, le musée de la compagnie ferroviaire belge, propose aux visiteurs, depuis le 18 septembre, « Fashion & Trains. La mode au fil du rail, depuis 1835 ». La scénographie de cette exposition temporaire montre comment l’avènement du chemin de fer et son évolution technique ont influé sur la mode à travers la tenue des voyageurs, mais également celle des agents de la SNCB. Tout au long de la visite, des affiches, des objets et de nombreuses photos constituent une véritable « esthétique » de l’usage du train.

Le premier hall s’intéresse à l’époque de la naissance du chemin de fer belge en 1835 et à son rapide développement jusqu’au début du XXe siècle. Le rail devient alors un symbole ultime de la révolution industrielle et de la modernité triomphante. Tout au long de la deuxième partie du XIXe siècle, les tenues de voyageurs évoluent.

Dans le deuxième hall, c’est la mode de l’entre-deux-guerres qui assure le spectacle. Les tenues diffèrent parfois entre lignes locales, utilisées par les habitants de villages, et trains plus rapides reliant les villes, ou entre les déplacements professionnels et ceux dédiés aux loisirs. Cette dernière dichotomie est explorée dans le troisième hall. La démocratisation des voyages d’agrément influence également l’évolution des vêtements. Dans le quatrième hall, on monte à bord des trains de luxe internationaux et des luxueuses voitures de la famille royale belge de 1901 et 1939.

Robe d’été portée par la reine Fabiola en 1969. © Train World – SNCB

Tout au long du parcours d’exposition, l’évolution des uniformes des agents de la SNCB est mise en lumière, de la tenue d’apparat du chef de gare aux uniformes contemporains, en passant par les premiers uniformes féminins.

En admirant ces différents modèles, le visiteur se rappellera cette sentence définitive de la grande Coco Chanel : « La mode se démode, le style jamais ».
