Palatability of geophyte underground storage organs partially explains their depth in the Cape Floristic Region, geophyte capital of the world

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise The soil depth of underground storage organs (USOs) of geophytes involves cost–benefit trade‐offs and may relate to USO age, size, and disturbances such as fire and herbivory. Yet determinants of USO depth are poorly studied. We posit that belowground mammalian predation of USOs is a selective force for the positive relationship between USO size and depth. Methods We tested the mammalian predation hypothesis using USO depth, mass, type (bulb, corm, tuber, rhizome) and palatability to human foragers for 1828 geophyte individuals (153 species, 54 genera, 22 families) from the lowlands of the Cape Floristic Region (South Africa) which harbors the world's highest concentration of geophytes. Results USOs were found at depths of 0–34 cm, but depth had no consistent relationship with mass. The depth–mass relationship was instead related to USO type: Compared with smaller counterparts, larger bulbs were at shallower depths, and larger corms were deeper. USO palatability affected the depth–mass relationship; palatability was generally associated with deeper burial across USO types: Palatable corms, tubers, and rhizomes mostly had positive depth–mass relationships, and unpalatable types had no relationship. Palatable bulbs had negative relationships that were less significant than for unpalatable bulbs. The phylogenetic signal in the study species was only moderate. Conclusions USO type and palatability explained some variation in their depth–mass relationship in accordance with the mammalian predation hypothesis. USO age and shape, soil type, and predation strategy and intensity should be explored as potential additional correlates of USO depth and size.

Geographic isolation promoted species divergence of two closely related Cycas species in distinct river basins

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise Understanding the process of species divergence and the mechanisms that drive genetic differentiation among closely related species is essential for explaining the origins of plant biodiversity. Geographic isolation and niche differentiation are key drivers of species divergence. Cycas chenii and C. guizhouensis are morphologically similar sister species from different river basins in Southwest China. Their distinct habitats in nearby river systems make them a potentially good model system for studying species divergence. Methods We used double‐digest restriction‐site‐associated DNA sequencing data and analyzed the data with a range of analyses to investigate the genetic divergence of C. guizhouensis and C. chenii and to assess the significance of geographic factors and ecological differentiation in their divergence. Results The two species had significant genetic differentiation (genetic differentiation index = 0.34). On the basis of phylogenetic and admixture analyses, the two species are genetically distinct lineages that diverged in the early Miocene. They experienced similar demographic histories over the past several million years. The genetic distance between the two species correlated positively with geographic distance but not with environmental distance. In a niche identity test, the ecological niches of the two species were statistically indistinguishable (Schoener's D = 0.68, I = 0.91, both within the null distribution). Conclusions Environmental variation is unlikely to have been the primary factor driving divergence of C. chenii and C. guizhouensis. Geographic separation instead may have been triggered by shifts in river channels during the early Miocene epoch, playing a pivotal role in interspecific differentiation.

From ice age to present: Clonal expansion and genetic variation in the world's oldest conifer stand

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise Clonality is widespread among plants, yet the processes governing genetic diversity within the most ancient clones remains understudied. We investigated these dynamics using a reputed 10,500‐year‐old clonal stand of one of the world's longest‐lived tree species, the paleoendemic conifer Lagarostrobos franklinii at Mt Read in Western Tasmania. Methods To quantify the clone's spatial extent and map its underlying genetic structure and gain insights into its origin, we used unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) photogrammetry and two complementary genetic data sets including nuclear simple sequence repeats (nSSRs) and multiplexed intersimple sequence repeat genotyping by sequencing (MIG‐seq), a reduced representation SNP discovery method. Results The putative clonal stand at Mt Read has been severely impacted by fire, with living stems occupying approximately 5260 m2, a ~65% reduction in the stand's previously estimated size. Contrary to the hypothesis that a single clone endures at Mt Read, both data sets revealed multiple, circumscribed patches, each with fixed multilocus genotypes (MLGs). Conclusions The Mt Read stand comprises three closely related MLGs, with the two largest clones being 3350 m2 and 1861 m2, respectively, they remain some of the largest conifer clones known. Although extremely low allelic diversity in the stand meant that nSSRs could not exclude a somatic origin for these MLGs, higher resolution MIG‐seq markers indicate an inbreeding origin. Overall, this study demonstrates how ancient clones previously regarded as single genets can harbor cryptic diversity. Accounting for this hidden structure is crucial to accurately estimate clone size and longevity and unravel their evolutionary history.

Placing very long branch taxa in the plant tree of life: A case study with Dioscoreales mycoheterotrophs

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise The inclusion of heterotrophic plants in broader systematic studies is challenging due to substantial morphological modification, gene loss, and accelerated substitution rates following photosynthesis loss. In particular, inclusion of very long branch (VLB) taxa with highly elevated rates may lead to phylogenomic misinference. Methods We explored how rapidly evolving plastomes behave in model‐based phylogenetic inferences in the yam order Dioscoreales, which experienced convergent photosynthesis losses. Our taxon sampling included mycoheterotrophic lineages displaying a broad range of substitution rates and all photosynthetic genera. Results When only moderately rapidly evolving heterotrophs are included, relationships within Dioscoreales are congruent with recent mitochondrial analysis, and generally strongly supported. Photosynthetic Stenomeris (represented by only three genes) is moderately supported as sister to Dioscoreaceae. Notably, Burmanniaceae and Thismiaceae, two mycoheterotrophic families including highly reduced plastomes, are strongly supported as distantly related within Dioscoreales. Constrained analysis rejects combining Thismiaceae with Burmanniaceae, conflicting with current angiosperm classification. Including a subset of VLB mycoheterotrophs in phylogenetic inference results in localized or broad reduction in branch support—substantially so for Afrothismiaceae, a family placing in disparate locations in Dioscoreales (and beyond) in variant analyses, with no ability to differentiate among its alternative placements. Conclusions Inclusion of highly rate‐elevated taxa in analyses of Dioscoreales phylogeny can depress branch support. Inferred relationships in the order are otherwise congruent with studies based on mitochondrial data; the family‐level classification needs updating. The extraordinarily rapidly evolving family Afrothismiaceae places inconsistently in diverse plastid analyses, pointing to probable analytical limits for plastid‐based phylogenomic analyses of VLB heterotrophs.

Evolutionary relationship between organellar DNA inheritance patterns and traits of seed plants

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Abstract Premise In plants, organellar DNA is predominantly maternally inherited, but biparental and paternal inheritance have also been observed. Specific mechanisms, such as plastid exclusion during pollen mitosis or genome degradation, ensure maternal inheritance. Certain factors, including low temperatures, disrupt cytoskeletal functions, thereby increasing paternal plastid transmission. Despite these findings, the ecological and evolutionary drivers of these inheritance modes remain unclear. We explored the traits associated with biparental and paternal inheritance to reveal the contributing ecological factors. Methods Plastid inheritance data were compiled for 378 species (302 genera and 112 families) and 12 functional traits obtained from the TRY database were analyzed. Missing values were filled according to Bayesian hierarchical modeling. On the basis of phylogenetic logistic regression, we investigated the relationships between inheritance patterns and plant traits, accounting for evolutionary history. Results Our models identified plant height, leaf area, and stem conduit density as traits associated with inheritance modes, with the strongest support for leaf area and limited support for the other traits. Coefficient directions indicated greater plant height, smaller leaves, and higher stem conduit density in species with non‐maternal inheritance. Excluding gymnosperms yielded similar tendencies, which suggests that similar ecological factors may be associated with plastid inheritance patterns in both groups. Conclusions Functional traits were associated with organelle inheritance patterns, with ecological and evolutionary implications. Similar ecological factors may be associated with the independent occurrence of paternal inheritance across lineages. Future work integrating molecular and ecological data will help elucidate the mechanisms underlying organelle inheritance and their adaptive significance.

Modeling diseased xylem networks

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
Abstract Vascular wilt pathogens induce xylem blockages that disrupt plant water transport, posing critical threats to agricultural productivity and food security. Key processes including xylem transport, vessel blockage, and pathogen spread within the xylem remain poorly understood, largely due to our limited ability to internally monitor infected tissues. This review explores how complementary mathematical modeling alongside laboratory studies can elucidate features governing these critical processes. I first provide a brief history of xylem imaging and quantification, highlighting technological advances, and persistent limitations. I then review modeling approaches to describe xylem water transport and pathogen dynamics, emphasizing that different pathogens require fundamentally different modeling frameworks. Existing xylem flow models may provide suitable hydraulic conductivity estimates for healthy stems under idealized conditions; however, they lack explicit representation of pit membranes and lumen‐occluding structures, limiting their applicability to diseased stems where these features are actively modified during infection. Previously described biofilm models can replicate bacterial colony growth in controlled experimental settings but are often too complex for integration with flow simulations and rely on parameters that are difficult to quantify experimentally. Finally, I highlight directions and challenges for modeling occlusion and spread at the local scale and discuss how these models might be scaled to network‐level architecture. While these types of models remain computationally challenging, advances in multiscale imaging technologies and scaling approaches promise significant progress in the coming decades. Insights from such models stand to advance both our understanding of disease progression and the development of resilient crops for an uncertain climate future.

During most of automotive history, you could only see a car’s badge clearly in daylight. They are mostly made of chrome, enamel and polished plastic. Therefore, once night falls, the badge will disappear in the darkness. Electric vehicles are changing that. Since they have no engine that needs a large radiator opening, the front of an EV has become a smooth, flat panel, and automakers are filling it with light.

Cadillac Lyriq – Photo by Michael Coates

Cadillac began this transition early. The Lyriq, the brand’s first all-electric model, has a black-and-white version of the Cadillac crest which can light up from behind. The Escalade IQ carried the idea to a much larger front-end design. In detail, an illuminated crest, a partly lit grille and vertical LED columns define the characteristics of its front fascia.

Audi has been more cautious in this regard, partly because of regulation. The Q8 E-tron marks the debut of a flat, chrome-free version of the “Four Rings” emblem. Also, Audi stated that it will add indirect grille lighting to frame the logo. The A6 E-tron then became the first Audi model with illuminated four-ring at the rear. Audi lighting engineers have said that step was possible only after new European rules came into effect in January 2023.

Why Light the Logo?

In fact, there are practical reasons behind the trend. EVs are very quiet when in motion, and their side profiles often look quite similar. Therefore, an illuminated badge gives a brand a highly recognizable “signature” at night. Moreover, LED lighting uses very little energy, so this design has almost no impact on a vehicle’s driving range. As headlights shrink into thin strips, the badge has become one of the few large surfaces where a brand can make a statement.

The Look Moves into the Aftermarket

Not every owner drives a new EV, but this look has spread to older and gas-powered vehicles. People can use some aftermarket kits to replace the factory badge with an LED unit that matches the original shape. An Audi light-up emblem, for example, is specially designed to match the dimensions of a specific model’s grille badge. Therefore, you don’t need to cut the grille any more.

Cadillac owners have similar options. A light-up Cadillac emblem is usually designed to follow the crest’s outline. Also, it can be wired to light up with the parking lights or when a door is opened.

What to Check Before Installing

  • Local lighting rules. In most U.S. states and Canadian provinces, there are restrictions for forward-facing red lights and flashing lights. So you had better check local regulations before you choose a color or a dynamic pattern.
  • Sensors behind the badge. Some newer models, including the Audi Q4 E-tron, are equipped with driver-assistance sensors behind the front badge. If a replacement badge blocks the radar, it may affect the adaptive cruise control system.
  • Wiring. Plug-and-play kits are easier to reverse than hardwired ones. A proper fuse can protect the 12-volt system, which is responsible for powering lights and accessories in electric vehicles.
  • Weather sealing. A badge is exposed to rain, road salt and car-wash spray. Therefore, the waterproof rating is really important.

The badge has always an iconic feature of cars. In the electric era, it is also becoming a light source, which owners of older vehicles can also enjoy now.

Why the world’s first spinal cord transplant is so controversial

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →
Shutterstock/PeopleImages

The virologist Jonas Salk said that “hope lies in dreams, in imagination and in the courage of those who dare to make dreams into reality”. Salk was certainly daring – he risked paralysing himself, his wife and their children while testing his polio vaccine. Thankfully, it paid off. None of them experienced any ill effects and his work is credited with saving millions.

Risks have to be taken for medicine to advance, and someone is always the first to receive a treatment. But there are risks, and then there are risks.

Salk tested his vaccine on cells, monkeys and children who had already had polio before turning his unexposed family into guinea pigs. His approach is far from standard protocol today, but he did a lot to reduce the risk of something going wrong.

Advertisement

There is less evidence to justify an operation that took place this month in Russia. Details are scant, but the procedure involved cutting out a section of spinal cord from someone with a complete spinal cord injury and gluing in a replacement piece from a donor. The transplant was a world first, but there is little evidence to believe it will have worked or that now was the time to try it.

The transplant was a world first, but there is little evidence to believe it will have worked

History tells us that acting ahead of medical evidence can have catastrophic results. Take the first synthetic organ transplants, which replaced tracheas with plastic tubes. The surgeon who fitted them admitted they hadn’t been tested on large animals because they “didn’t have the time”. Complications and deaths followed.

With new medical procedures, the gold standard is randomised controlled trials, which are costly and take time, but they aren’t the only route. When someone is critically ill, there are compassionate-use protocols that make interventions available before they are fully tested. Pig kidney transplants are currently being trialled this way, for example.

The dream of spinal cord transplants will surely become a reality one day, but only if the science underpinning them is built first. Courage, dreams and imagination are of course good, yet with experimental surgeries, evidence is better.

Cadillac Escalade IQ review: Huge battery, huge range, huge SUV

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

“Please have at least 125 miles of range left when we pick it up.” That request from the people who look after the Cadillac press cars wasn’t going to be hard to honor. The electric Escalade IQ arrived with an indicated range of 440 miles (708 km), and with a full week of work to squeeze in, getting anywhere close to needing to recharge it seemed unlikely. With more than 200 kWh of lithium-ion battery between its axles, range is one thing the big electric Caddy needn’t worry about.

Designed and debuted during a time of greater EV optimism, the Escalade is Cadillac’s statement that swapping out a V8 for a pair of powerful electric motors is no compromise to its ambitions, even if the brand’s future will now include a mix of electric and combustion cars beyond 2030. It shares a battery pack and much else with other full-size GM EVs we’ve tested: the Chevrolet Silverado EV, the GMC Sierra EV, and of course the divisive Hummer EVs. And like those trucks and SUVs, the Escalade is large and heavy—just small enough to fit into a standard parking space, with a curb weight sufficient to push it into the Class 3 truck category.

So, the side mirrors are flat, and there’s no formal EPA range—Cadillac says it achieves 460 miles (740 km) on the EPA test cycle—but there are plenty of airbags, even though the 9,046 lb (4,103 kg) curb weight does not require them. Yes, you did read that right, it does weigh 4 tons. And yes, the electric Escalade does weigh a third more than the last gas-powered one we tested.
