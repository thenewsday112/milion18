Food-Themed Fashion Is Starting to Feel Gluttonous

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Loewe | Loewe

There was a time, not too long ago, when a purse shaped like a baguette felt like a thrilling — even subversive — novelty. Now, we hardly bat an eye when we see that Moschino has made a trompe l’oeil celery purse, or that Big Bud Press is making a hot-dog-and-pretzel-print jumpsuit in collaboration with a Los Angeles bar. How did we get here? And has it all become a little too much? 

Fashion has long found inspiration from the culinary world, but the influx of food-fueled prints, graphics, and trompe l’oeil accessories that picked up steam during the pandemic have now reached a cultural tipping point. 

Today, there are truly countless examples of these food motifs and edible, wearable trompe l’oeil creations, at all price points. Loewe’s summer collection featured a $4,150 lambskin tomato clutch, while Walmart’s included a line of rotisserie chicken bag-like purses and charms starting at $5.97. As with many trends, this trajectory started in high fashion and worked its way down to mass-market ubiquity. In 2020, a chocolate chip cookie went down one of New York Fashion Week’s most buzzed-about fall runways. It protruded from a black purse by the designer Puppets and Puppets, a design that quickly went viral for its whimsical inclusion of a classic dessert on a high-end handbag. 

The then-emerging brand made food a central theme of its vision: Its cake-topped aviator caps, Ferrero Rocher heels, and beeswax-sculpted cheese hats — hyperrealistic, absurdist food designs capable of achieving instant social media virality. Prior to working in fashion, Puppets designer Carly Mark was working as an artist whose pieces revolved around candies and snacks. As she told Business of Fashion in 2023, the Puppets universe felt like the ideal pairing given what she considers food and fashion’s motivators: pleasure and money. 

View this post on Instagram

During the pandemic, the much-duped “strawberry dress” took over TikTok, and Tombolo Company found a hit on its hands with its vongole shirt, which debuted in 2019, but found peak popularity in 2021. The shirt appeared in Vogue in 2020, and InsideHook called it an “obvious bedfellow to day-drinks, rooftops and weekenders” — in other words, a status symbol. Katie Kimmel’s T-shirts made chicken-parm enthusiasm akin to being a devout sports fan, and in 2022, this very publication called “seafood kitsch” the look of the summer, as items like Rachel Antonoff’s super-popular seafood-tower dress flew off the digital shelves. 

Why was food so primed to become a motif in clothing at this point in culture? One could make the argument that it has roots in the millennial optimism years: Earlier in the 2000s and 2010s, the concept of “foodies” was new. Brands printed tongue-in-cheek food- and coffee-related motifs and slogans (a fervent love of avocados comes to mind) on hipster apparel, as the idea that “you are what you eat” developed growing fervor. That period was a key stepping stone in the evolution toward the far more elaborate and pervasive marketability of food-as-identity we see today. Content creator Cole Poska, who is a partner and creative director at Beyond Studios, tells Eater that “food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.” Poska’s own Instagram often reimagines restaurant merch just for fun; his designs for Cheesecake Factory apparel went viral, such as a hat that resembles the iconic brown bread. He points out that people will defend their favorite fast-food order like it’s a family member, and with the kind of loyalty that’s hard to manufacture. The same could be said about their martini order, or favorite taco spot; it’s all a way of telling others who we are through what we like.

View this post on Instagram

But the fatigue is undoubtedly here. In 2024, Bon Appétit asked “Why Does Every Dress Have Pasta and Cocktail Shrimp on It?” And as the novelty factor of a shrimp-print dress has worn off, the next question might be: Will there be any true longevity for these food-as-fashion moments? Our feeds overflow with products that are hurriedly designed for flash-in-the-pan viral trends and quickly destined for a landfill; arguably more so if the object in question prioritizes shock value and gimmicks over function and brand identity. 

This is undoubtedly all related to the accelerated speed of trend cycles and shortened consumer attention spans. However, as Leah Asmelash wrote last year for CNN, the rising costs of food also casts an increasingly sinister shadow over food-themed fashion. In 2026, a $425 cardigan emblazoned with oysters has waning originality compared to a few years ago; it falls into banality at best and the Hunger Games at worst; its wearer indicating that they’re financially comfortable and, more importantly, well-fed. In that light, beyond its role in identity, food has become more of a status symbol than ever. A sweater touting one’s love of caviar is shorthand for their access to and appreciation of luxury, culinary and otherwise. But sporting the mass-market Temu version? It’s more akin to wearing a faux Gucci belt.  

“Food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.”

Cole Poska

If it’s possible to escape the sense of accelerated-trend-cycle disposability, it may have to be through a more subtle approach than, say, a bag that looks like a giant stick of butter. Sam Salad is the co-founder of Meals, a Los Angeles-based, workwear-inspired apparel company that, by its own description, “bakes ‘food culture’ into apparel, blurring the lines between the clothes we eat and the food we wear.” Utility is at the forefront of Meals, which offers a classic chef pant with a slightly fuller cut, in both basic colors and subdued aesthetic homages to blue cheese and watermelon. When Meals launched in 2019, Salad was selling premium denim along Venice’s Abbot Kinney Boulevard, a hotbed of Instagrammy food culture and trendy restaurants like Gjelina that helped inspire his own brand. “Design today has to translate across languages and platforms,” Salad tells Eater. 

Food is hardly a new bedfellow for fashion designers, even if today’s hyper-fast trend cycle has kicked it into overdrive. “Food is totally universal,” Salad says. “Issey Miyake once merchandised their pleats collection as little omakase sets.”  He cites that collection among other fashion-meets-food moments that he considers successful, such as the iconic Salvador Dalí- and Elsa Schiaparelli-designed lobster dress. At the time of its 1937 creation, the dress was a scandal: It assumed a conformity-defying sartorial stance and was fittingly worn by Wallace Simpson. Today, its highly devolved spawn are available on Shein for $13. 

As it stands, one clear way out of corny, food motif purgatory is for brands — fashion or food — to focus less on quantity and more on storytelling and quality. “We do a few things really well, and that’s enough,” Salad says, “Like In-N-Out [Burger].” 

Poska’s philosophy is similar. “I’d rather make one thing people talk about for a year than 20 things people forget about tomorrow,” he says. “The first person to make a candle look like a stick of butter is a genius. The hundredth person is just making a candle.”

  • Titan Quest II launches on XBOX Series X|S on January 19, 2027, and is available for pre-order starting today.
  • Players combine two of six Masteries into a hybrid class that perfectly matches their playstyle.

High above the clouds, the gods of Olympus watch in silence – for once, even they are powerless against what is coming. The fate of the world now rests in mortal hands. Your hands. But do you have what it takes to face a goddess? The THQ Nordic and Grimlore Games teams are thrilled to announce that Titan Quest II launches on XBOX Series X|S on January 19, 2027. And starting today, you can pre-order the game on XBOX.

Titan Quest II is the successor to the beloved original Titan Quest: an action RPG set in a reimagined ancient Greece, where you set out to stop Nemesis, the Goddess of Retribution, from corrupting the Threads of Fate. Since the game entered Early Access on PC in August 2025, we’ve worked hand in hand with our community. We read the feedback, ran the numbers, and added new regions, new Masteries, new enemies and bosses, crafting, multiplayer, tons of loot, and much more along the way. With the full release, the story reaches its conclusion, and the complete journey comes to XBOX.

So, what makes Titan Quest II stand out in a genre full of great action RPGs? It starts with the heart of the series: the Mastery system. Instead of locking yourself into a fixed class at the start of your adventure, you pick two Masteries and combine them into a hybrid class of your own. Titan Quest II launches with six Masteries – Earth, Storm, Warfare, Rogue, Forge, and Spirit – and each of them can be freely paired with any other. That’s 15 distinct combinations before you’ve even looked at your gear.

Earth commands fire and stone for devastating area damage. Storm calls down lightning and frost to control the battlefield. Warfare is all about raw physical might, while Rogue strikes from the shadows with poison and piercing blades. Forge, inspired by Hephaistos himself, reinforces your weapons and armor in the middle of a fight and deploys blade traps, sentry turrets, and ferociously fighting automatons. And Spirit lets you summon the dead and draw on your hero’s life force to fuel otherworldly spells. Want to set up blade traps crackling with lightning? Pair Forge with Storm. Rather send the dead ahead while you strike from the shadows? Spirit and Rogue have you covered.

And the customization doesn’t stop at the pairing. Every skill can be shaped with modifiers that do far more than add a few percent of damage – some change how a skill behaves entirely, like turning a sweeping strike into a channeled whirlwind. Attribute points in Might, Agility, Vigor, and Knowledge let you fine-tune your hero even further, and if you ever want to rethink your build from scratch, you can respec at any time (the gods charge a little gold for it, of course).

Did we mention that every hero can use whatever weapons and equipment they prefer? You can wear full plate armor while wielding a frost magic staff, or charge into battle clad in leather while swinging a two-handed axe. And an archer dressed in fine linen works perfectly well with any Mastery. The choice is truly yours.

The second thing that sets Titan Quest II apart is how it plays. Combat is deliberate rather than frantic. Enemies fight in factions and combine their abilities, so reading the battlefield, dodging at the right moment, and smart positioning matter just as much as raw damage. Even your health potions encourage you to stay in the fight: they refill as you deal damage to your enemies. IGN Germany summed up the Early Access version as “the ARPG for everyone who finds Diablo too hectic” – and we’ll happily take that. With Rituals, you also decide for yourself how hard the gods should test you, adjusting the challenge freely to your liking.

Loot matters, too. Items aren’t just bigger numbers; they serve a real purpose for your build, with plenty of equipment options for every class. Socket Charms and Relics into your gear, and use crafting to reroll, add, or upgrade affixes until every piece is exactly what your build needs.

All of this takes place in a beautiful, handcrafted world rooted in Greek myth. Venture across sun-drenched coasts, the Arkadian Plains, the untamed Wild Lands, and even Mount Olympus itself. Discover dark dungeons, uncover hidden groves where legendary monsters lurk, and stumble upon World Quests you can complete simply by finding them. Along the way, you’ll face creatures straight out of legend, from centaurs and gryphons to cyclopes, all set to a soundtrack that blends traditional Greek music with orchestral grandeur. And if you’d rather not take on Nemesis alone, you can explore Greece together with friends in cooperative multiplayer.

Ready to answer the call? Pre-orders for Titan Quest II open today, October 28, on XBOX Series X|S, with two editions to choose from:

  • Titan Quest II Standard Edition – $59.99 / €59.99: Includes the Cosmic Turtle Companion as a pre-order bonus and 96 hours of early access. Owners of the original Titan Quest on XBOX also receive a 10% loyalty discount.
  • Titan Quest II Ultimate Edition – $79.99 / €79.99: Includes the Cosmic Turtle Companion as a pre-order bonus and 96 hours of early access, plus the original Titan Quest and its expansions Ragnarök and Atlantis – playable immediately upon pre-order.

With 96 hours of early access, you can begin your journey as early as January 15, 2027. And if you go for the Ultimate Edition, the original Titan Quest and both expansions are yours right away – the perfect way to warm up for the sequel (or to finally find out what happened in Atlantis).

Sir Patrick Stewart, Star Trek, X-Men, And Myriad Video Games Actor, Passes Away

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

According to a report from The Hollywood Reporter, actor Sir Patrick Stewart has passed away at the age of 86. Stewart spokesperson, Kelly Bush Novak, shared that he died after “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.”

Stewart was best known for his roles as Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation (and its various spinoffs) and Charles Xavier in the X-Men films, but he did plenty of video game performing, too. Stewart played Emperor Uriel Septim VII in The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion, one of the first characters you meet in the game, and Zobek in Castlevania: Lords of Shadow. He also played Jean-Luc Picard in about 10 different Star Trek games and Charles Xavier in five different X-Men games.

Patrick Stewart as Jean-Luc Picard in the 1994 Super Nintendo game, Star Trek: The Next Generation.

Stewart will always be a crucial voice and face in the legacy of sci-fi entertainment. Jean-Luc Picard will undoubtedly be an enduring and important character in the Star Trek franchise, and that's thanks to Stewart's portrayal of the starship captain. His portrayal of Charles Xavier also helped to legitimize the comic book movie, and he will appear again as the character in this year's upcoming Avengers: Doomsday.

Our thoughts are with his friends and family, and the millions of fans (of which I include myself) who are mourning his loss. Let's all drink some Earl Grey tea in his honor today.
